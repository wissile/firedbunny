

========== l-budget | Money Foundations #1 | Build a monthly budget (widget budgetBuilder)
Summary: Split your pay into needs, wants and saving, and see why saving sets your timeline.
Idea: Early retirement starts with a monthly budget. The share of your pay you save decides how many years you have to work.

### Start from take-home pay
Budget from the money that actually lands in your checking account, after taxes, health insurance and any 401(k) deduction. If part of your paycheck already goes into a 401(k), add it back to both your pay and your saving so your savings rate is honest.

### Needs, wants and saving

- Needs are bills you must pay to live and work: rent or mortgage, groceries, utilities, insurance, transport and minimum debt payments.
- Wants make life better but could stop tomorrow: eating out, travel, shopping, hobbies, most subscriptions.
- Saving is money for your future self: retirement accounts, the emergency fund, investing and paying off debt faster than the minimum.The 50/30/20 rule is a popular starting point: about half for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for saving. People who retire early often save 40% to 60%.

### Pay yourself first
Set an automatic transfer to savings and investments for the day your pay arrives, then spend what is left. Saving whatever remains at the end of the month rarely works, because there is rarely anything left. Start small if you need to and raise it by 1 percentage point a month, or save half of every raise.

### Why the savings rate sets your retirement date
Saving more does two things at once: the pile grows faster, and the lifestyle it must pay for gets smaller. With the assumptions in the tool (start from zero, 5% a year after inflation, live on 4% of savings), the years of work look like this:

- Save 10% of take-home pay: about 51 years.
- Save 20%: about 37 years.
- Save 30%: about 28 years.
- Save 50%: about 17 years.
- Save 65%: about 10 years.Your income matters less than you would think. Two people with the same savings rate need about the same number of years. The FIRE track goes deeper in Savings rate is the lever.

### Pick a method you will keep

- 50/30/20: three buckets, little tracking. Good for most people.
- Zero-based: every dollar gets a job before the month starts. More work, more control.
- Pay yourself first only: automate saving, then spend the rest freely. Simple and surprisingly effective.
- Envelopes: fixed cash or digital envelopes for categories that tend to run over, such as dining out.
### Fix the big three first
Housing, transport and food are most of a typical budget. One cheaper apartment or one paid-off car usually does more than a year of skipped coffees. Small habits still add up, though: see Small habits, big numbers, and track it all in Track your spending.

## Worked example: A couple’s $9,000 month
Take-home pay is $9,000 a month after taxes and insurance. Here is where it goes, and one round of changes.
1. Needs: rent $2,600, groceries $900, utilities and phone $350, car payment and gas $700, insurance and medical $350, student loan minimums $300. Total $5,200, or 58% of take-home pay.
2. Wants: eating out, travel, shopping and subscriptions take $2,500, or 28%.
3. What is left for saving is $1,300 a month, a 14% savings rate. At that pace, financial independence is about 44 years away.
4. They move $600 out of wants (fewer takeout nights, one trip a year instead of two) and save $400 on needs by switching car insurance and phone plans. Saving rises to $2,300 a month.
5. They automate it: $2,300 moves to savings and investments the day pay arrives, and the rest in checking is theirs to spend.
Result: Saving went from 14% to 26% of take-home pay, which cuts the road to financial independence from about 44 years to about 31.

## Mistakes
- Budgeting from gross pay instead of take-home pay, so the plan never matches what reaches the bank account.
- Saving whatever is left at the end of the month. There is usually nothing left. Automate saving first.
- Making the budget so tight it breaks in week two. Leave room for fun and for irregular costs such as gifts and car repairs.
- Letting spending rise with every raise (lifestyle creep), so the savings rate never moves.
## Takeaways
- Budget from take-home pay in three buckets: needs, wants and saving.
- 50/30/20 is a starting point. Early retirees often save 40% to 60%.
- Your savings rate, more than your income, sets how many years you work.
- Pay yourself first: automate saving on payday.
- Fix housing, transport and food before small habits.
## Quiz
Q: Two friends take home the same pay. One saves 10% of it and the other saves 50%. How do their timelines to financial independence compare?
  About the same, since they earn the same
* The 50% saver needs about a third as many years
  The 10% saver gets there first
Why: Under this lesson’s assumptions, saving 10% takes about 51 years and saving 50% about 17. Saving more grows the pile and shrinks the lifestyle it must pay for.
## Lesson glossary
- Zero-based budget: A budget where every dollar of income is assigned a job (a bill, a want or saving) before the month starts.

========== l-track-spending | Money Foundations #2 | Track your spending (widget spendTracker)
Summary: Bank categories, an app or a spreadsheet, plus a weekly 10-minute review.
Idea: Tracking is not about guilt. A short weekly look catches overspending while there is still time to fix the month.

### Three ways to track

- Your bank and card categories. Free and automatic. Fix the ones that are wrong: a big-box store can be groceries one week and a TV the next.
- A budgeting app that links all your accounts. Many charge a yearly fee; check what they do with your data before you connect anything.
- A spreadsheet. The most control and the most typing. Our spreadsheet templates are a starting point.Using one or two cards for everyday spending makes tracking much easier, as long as you pay them in full every month (see Credit card debt).

### The weekly 10-minute review

- Same day every week, for example Sunday morning.
- Open your accounts and look at each category against where it should be by now. By the end of week 2, about half the monthly budget is normal.
- Scan for charges you do not recognize or did not use.
- Pick one small change for the rest of the month, such as cooking twice more or pausing online shopping.
### Sinking funds for irregular costs
Car repairs, holiday gifts, yearly insurance bills and vacations are not emergencies; they are bills on a schedule. Divide each one by 12 and save that much every month in its own bucket. Gifts that cost $1,200 a year become $100 a month, and December stops wrecking the budget.

### Hunt the leaks
List every recurring charge: streaming, apps, cloud storage, gym, memberships, warranties. Cancel anything you did not use in the last 30 days. Set a reminder before free trials end. Leaks are small, but they repeat every month without you deciding anything.

### Watch for lifestyle creep
When income rises, spending quietly rises to match. Decide in advance what happens to raises and bonuses, for example save half and enjoy half. That one rule keeps your savings rate climbing as your career grows.

## Worked example: One review saves a month
The household in the tool budgets $1,790 a month for flexible spending across six categories.
1. After two weeks they have spent $1,069, against $895 if spending were even. Dining is $55 over plan and shopping $75 over.
2. The 10-minute review also finds a $35 a month gym membership nobody used.
3. For weeks 3 and 4 they skip two lunches out, one online order, one bar tab, one dinner out and one round of drinks: $240 in total.
4. The month ends at $1,777, $13 under budget, instead of $2,017, $227 over.
5. Cancelling the gym saves another $35 a month, $420 a year.
Result: Ten minutes a week turned a $227 overspend into a month on budget and found $420 a year of pure waste.

## Mistakes
- Tracking hard for a month, feeling bad and quitting. Make it a 10-minute weekly habit, not a judgment.
- Only looking at the month when it is over, when nothing can be changed.
- Forgetting irregular costs, so a car repair or a birthday “breaks” the budget. Use sinking funds.
- Trusting automatic categories without checking them.
## Takeaways
- Pick the simplest tool you will actually keep using.
- Review weekly and compare each category with where it should be by now.
- Unused subscriptions are pure leak: cancel them.
- Turn irregular costs into monthly sinking funds.
- Decide in advance how much of every raise you will save.
## Quiz
Q: It is day 14 of the month and you have already spent 70% of your dining budget. What does the weekly review tell you?
  Nothing yet; wait for the month to end
* You are ahead of pace, so ease off for the next two weeks
  Move the extra into next month’s budget
Why: Halfway through the month you should be near 50%. Adjusting now is easy; at month end it is too late.

========== l-latte | Money Foundations #3 | Small habits, big numbers (widget habitCost)
Summary: Daily coffee, pods and takeout: the yearly cost, and what it could grow into.
Idea: Small daily spending is easy to ignore. Priced by the year and invested for decades, it gets surprisingly big.

### Price it by the year
A $6.25 latte five workdays a week is $6.25 × 5 × 52 = $1,625 a year. Two coffee pods a day at $1.10 each is about $800 a year. A $15 takeout lunch three times a week is $2,340. None of these feels big on the day you buy it.

### Then price it in future dollars
Money you do not spend can be invested. $1,625 a year, invested monthly at 7% a year, grows to about $23,000 in 10 years, $71,000 in 20 and $165,000 in 30. That is the real price of the habit: its opportunity cost. The 7% is a long-run assumption for a stock index fund, not a promise.

### Café, pods or an espresso machine?

- Café to home is the big win. A café latte costs $5 to $7; the same drink at home costs well under $1.
- Pods to whole beans saves less. Pods with milk run about $1 to $1.50 a drink; fresh beans and milk run about $0.50 to $1.
- A good espresso machine and grinder costs from a few hundred dollars to several thousand. Against café drinks it pays for itself in months; against pods it can take years.So buy the machine if you love good coffee and will use it every day. The money comes from not buying café drinks.

### Other habits worth pricing

- Delivery app fees, service fees and tips on top of the food.
- Streaming and app subscriptions you forgot you had.
- Energy drinks, bottled water and convenience-store snacks.
- Bank fees and late fees, which buy nothing at all.
### Choose, do not just cut
Cutting every small joy usually fails within a month. Keep the habits you truly value and drop the ones you barely notice. And keep perspective: housing, cars and food matter more than any coffee (see Build a monthly budget).

## Worked example: Two coffees a day, three ways
Example prices: café latte $6.25, pod drink with milk $1.30, home espresso $0.75 a drink, machine and grinder $900.
1. Café: 2 drinks a day × $6.25 × 365 days = $4,562.50 a year.
2. Pods: 2 × $1.30 × 365 = $949 a year, in a pod machine you already own.
3. Machine and beans: $900 once, then 2 × $0.75 × 365 = $547.50 a year.
4. Café to machine saves $4,015 a year, so the $900 pays back in about 3 months. Pods to machine saves $401.50 a year, so it takes about 2.2 years.
5. Invest the $4,015 a year you no longer spend at cafés, monthly at 7%: about $58,000 after 10 years and about $408,000 after 30.
Result: The machine is a good buy against café drinks and a modest one against pods. The real money is in the habit you change, not the gadget.

## Mistakes
- Cutting every small pleasure at once and giving up on the budget a month later. Cut what you do not value.
- Buying an expensive machine and still buying café drinks, so the machine is just one more cost.
- Obsessing over coffee while a car payment or rent eats most of the budget.
- Forgetting delivery fees and subscriptions; they repeat quietly every month.
## Takeaways
- Multiply small habits by a year: $6 a day is about $2,200 a year.
- Invested monthly at 7% for 30 years, every $1,000 a year grows to about $100,000.
- Café to home is the big saving; pods to beans is a small one.
- Keep the habits you love and drop the ones you do not notice.
## Quiz
Q: You buy two $6 coffees every workday. About how much is that a year?
  About $600
* About $3,100
  About $4,400
Why: $12 a day × 5 workdays × 52 weeks = $3,120 a year.

========== l-emergency | Money Foundations #4 | Emergency fund and where to park cash (widget cashParking)
Summary: How many months to keep, and where cash earns the most after tax while staying safe.
Idea: An emergency fund turns a job loss or a big repair into an inconvenience instead of debt. Keep it safe, quick to reach and earning interest.

### How big should it be?
A common rule of thumb is three to six months of must-pay bills: rent or mortgage, food, insurance, utilities and minimum debt payments. Not your full spending, because in an emergency the extras stop. Keep more if you are self-employed, your household has one income, your pay is irregular or your job would be hard to replace. If you are starting from zero, a first goal of about $500 to $1,000 already covers many surprises.

### What it is for
Job loss, medical bills, a car or home repair you did not see coming. A vacation or holiday gifts are planned costs: save for them in a separate sinking fund (see Track your spending). If you use the emergency fund, refilling it comes before new investing.

### Where to keep it (October 2026 rates)

- High-yield savings account: about 3.9% to 4.3% at the top online banks, against a national average of about 0.64% for savings. FDIC-insured.
- Money market funds such as Vanguard’s VMFXX (7-day yield about 3.83%) and VUSXX (about 3.90%). They hold short-term government debt. Very low risk, but they are investments, not bank deposits, so they are not FDIC-insured. Selling gets you cash the next business day.
- Treasury bills (3-month T-bills about 4.26%): short loans to the US government, bought at a broker or TreasuryDirect. You can sell before they mature, or wait up to three months.Careful with names: a bank’s money market deposit account is FDIC-insured; a money market fund is not.

### Compare yields after tax
Bank interest is taxed by the IRS and your state. Interest from US Treasuries is taxed by the IRS but not by states. A government money market fund is partly state-tax-free: in 2025, 66.61% of VMFXX’s income and 100% of VUSXX’s came from US government obligations. California, New York and Connecticut only allow that exclusion when a fund holds at least 50% in such debt, which VMFXX reportedly met for 2025. You usually enter the exclusion yourself on your state return.

### Safety, not growth
Prices were up 3.7% over the year to September 2026. A 4% yield taxed at 22% to 30% does not quite keep up with that, and that is fine: the emergency fund is insurance. Money for goals more than a few years away belongs in investments (see Know your net worth and the investing course).

### Insurance limits
FDIC insurance covers at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, per insured bank. Money market funds and Treasuries held at a broker are not FDIC-insured; SIPC protects you if the broker fails, but never against market losses (see Choosing a broker).

## Worked example: A $24,000 cushion
One income, so six months. Federal bracket 22%, state income tax 5%. Rates as of October 2026.
1. Must-pay bills are $4,000 a month, so the target is 6 × $4,000 = $24,000.
2. In a savings account paying the 0.64% average, after 22% federal and 5% state tax, it earns about $112 a year.
3. A high-yield savings account at 4.20% keeps 3.07% after tax: about $736 a year, still FDIC-insured.
4. 3-month Treasury bills at 4.26% skip state tax, so they keep 3.32%: about $797 a year.
5. A practical split: one month of bills in checking or savings for speed, the rest in a high-yield account or T-bills.
Result: Same safety, about $600 to $700 more interest a year than an average savings account.

## Mistakes
- Keeping the emergency fund in stocks. Crashes and layoffs often arrive together.
- Leaving it for years in a savings account that pays almost nothing.
- Assuming a money market fund is FDIC-insured. A bank money market deposit account is; a fund is not.
- Spending it on a vacation, then meeting the next real emergency with a credit card.
## Takeaways
- Aim for three to six months of must-pay bills, more with one income or irregular pay.
- High-yield savings, Treasury money market funds and T-bills all pay far more than average savings.
- Compare yields after tax: Treasury interest skips state income tax.
- The fund is insurance, not an investment. Refill it before investing more.
## Quiz
Q: You live in a state with a high income tax. At the same yield, which usually keeps more interest after tax?
  A high-yield savings account
* Treasury bills or a Treasury money market fund
  They always come out the same
Why: Interest from US Treasuries is free of state and local income tax; bank interest is taxed by your state.

========== l-net-worth | Money Foundations #5 | Know your net worth (widget netWorth)
Summary: Add what you own, subtract what you owe, and track the number over time.
Idea: Net worth is your financial scoreboard: everything you own minus everything you owe. The direction matters more than the size.

### What you own
Cash and savings, retirement accounts, brokerage investments, an HSA, your home at what it would sell for today, and cars at their resale value. Furniture, clothes and electronics are worth far less than you paid; most people leave them out. Future paychecks and a hoped-for inheritance do not count.

### What you owe
Mortgage, car loans, student loans, credit card balances, medical bills, personal loans, 401(k) loans and buy-now-pay-later balances. Use the payoff amount, not the monthly payment.

### Three numbers worth watching

- Net worth: everything you own minus everything you owe.
- Investable net worth: cash and investments only. This is what can actually pay for retirement, and what your FIRE number is compared with.
- Liquid net worth: what you could reach within days without penalties, such as cash and a taxable brokerage account.A paid-off home is valuable, but you cannot buy groceries with it without selling or borrowing.

### How to track it
Pick one day a month or a quarter and write the numbers in a spreadsheet: each account, each debt, the total and the change since last time. Add a note on why it moved: did you save more, or did markets move? Our spreadsheets include a simple tracker.

### If it is negative
A negative net worth is common in your twenties, mostly from student loans. Work in order: pay off high-interest debt first (credit cards averaged about 21% APR in August 2026), build the emergency fund, capture any 401(k) match, then invest. Watch the direction: up a little every quarter is the goal.

## Worked example: A mid-career snapshot

1. Own: $25,000 in cash, $180,000 in retirement accounts, $40,000 in a brokerage account, a home worth $450,000 and cars worth $22,000. Total $717,000.
2. Owe: a $310,000 mortgage. Net worth is $717,000 − $310,000 = $407,000.
3. Of that, $140,000 is home equity ($450,000 − $310,000) and $22,000 is cars. Investable net worth (cash plus investments) is $245,000.
4. They save $2,000 a month, so net worth grows by $24,000 a year before market moves, plus the mortgage principal they pay down.
5. They log the numbers every quarter. A bad market can lower net worth for a quarter or two; the multi-year trend is what they judge.
Result: Net worth is $407,000, but the money that could fund retirement today is $245,000.

## Mistakes
- Valuing the home at what you hope it will sell for, or the car at what you paid for it.
- Checking daily and reacting to every market swing. Monthly or quarterly is enough.
- Forgetting easy-to-miss debts: 401(k) loans, medical bills and buy-now-pay-later plans.
- Comparing your number with friends or online averages instead of with your own past.
## Takeaways
- Net worth = what you own − what you owe. Write it down on a schedule.
- Watch investable net worth: your home and car do not pay the bills.
- Pay off high-interest debt before investing beyond any employer match.
- The trend over years matters more than any one month.
## Quiz
Q: You own a $300,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage, have $20,000 in savings and owe $10,000 on credit cards. What is your net worth?
  $320,000
* $60,000
  $50,000
Why: $300,000 + $20,000 − $250,000 − $10,000 = $60,000.
## Lesson glossary
- Investable net worth: Your cash and investments, leaving out your home and cars. The part of your wealth that can pay for retirement.
- Liquid net worth: What you could turn into cash within days without penalties, such as savings and a taxable brokerage account.

========== l-credit-score | Debt & Credit #1 | How credit scores work (widget creditScore)
Summary: What debt costs, what a credit score measures and how to raise yours.
Idea: Debt is renting future money. Your credit score sets the rent, and five habits set the score.

### What debt really is
When you borrow, you spend money you have not earned yet. Interest is the rent you pay for that. A $5,000 card balance at 22% costs about $1,100 a year in rent, and you get nothing for it.
Some debt can make sense: a fixed-rate mortgage on a home you live in, or a low-rate loan for something that earns or saves money. High-rate debt for things that lose value, such as card balances for vacations or gadgets, is the kind that keeps people poor. The smart leverage lesson goes deeper.

### What the score is
A credit score is a number from 300 to 850 that predicts how likely you are to pay a loan back. FICO, the most used score, groups it like this: 300 to 579 poor, 580 to 669 fair, 670 to 739 good, 740 to 799 very good, 800 to 850 exceptional.
Lenders use it to decide whether to lend and at what rate. In the second quarter of 2026, new-car borrowers with the best scores paid 4.41% on average, while subprime borrowers paid 13.52%. On a $30,000 five-year loan, that gap is about $8,000 of interest.

### The five factors

- Payment history, 35%. Did you pay on time? A single late payment that gets reported can hurt for years, and most negative items can stay on your report for seven years.
- Amounts owed, 30%. Mostly your credit utilization: card balances divided by card limits. Lower is better. In Experian’s data, people with scores of 800 to 850 used about 7% of their limits on average.
- Length of history, 15%. How old your accounts are. Time is the only fix.
- New credit, 10%. Each application adds a hard inquiry. FICO counts inquiries from the last 12 months, and each one usually costs fewer than 5 points.
- Credit mix, 10%. Having both cards and an installment loan helps a little. Never borrow just for this.
### How to raise it

- Set every card and loan to autopay at least the minimum, so you are never late.
- Pay your card before the statement closes. The balance on the statement is what gets reported, so a lower one means lower utilization.
- Keep old cards open, even if you rarely use them. Closing one shrinks your total limit and can shorten your history.
- Apply only when you need credit. When you shop for a car loan or mortgage, do it within a couple of weeks: FICO counts those inquiries as one.
- Check your reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com and dispute mistakes. Freezing your credit with the three bureaus is free and blocks most identity theft.
### Myths

- Checking your own score does not hurt it. That is a soft inquiry.
- Carrying a balance does not help your score. Paying in full every month gives the same score benefit with no interest.
- Your income and savings are not part of the score.

## Worked example: Cleaning up before a car loan
Maya plans to buy a car in three months. Her two cards have $10,000 of limits and $4,500 of balances.
1. Her utilization is $4,500 ÷ $10,000 = 45%. That is the biggest drag on her score.
2. She pays $4,000 before the statement closing dates. The balances reported next are $500, so utilization drops to 5%.
3. She sets both cards to autopay the full statement balance and makes no new applications.
4. When she shops for the loan, she gets three quotes within one week. FICO treats them as one inquiry.
5. If her score lands in the best tier, a $30,000 five-year loan at 4.41% costs $558 a month and $3,484 in interest. At 6.15% it would be $582 a month and $4,925 in interest.
Result: Paying down cards before applying could save her about $1,440 on one loan.

## Mistakes
- Closing your oldest card after paying it off. Your total limit shrinks and utilization jumps.
- Missing a payment because it was not on autopay. Payment history is the heaviest factor.
- Opening store cards at checkout for a one-time discount. Each one is a hard inquiry and a new account.
- Paying a company to "fix" your credit. You can dispute errors yourself for free; nobody can remove accurate late payments.
## Takeaways
- Interest is rent on money you have not earned yet. High-rate debt for things that lose value is the costly kind.
- Payment history (35%) and amounts owed (30%) are two-thirds of a FICO score.
- Keep card utilization low, ideally in the single digits, by paying before the statement closes.
- A better score means lower rates: thousands of dollars on a car loan or mortgage.
## Quiz
Q: Which change usually raises a score fastest?
  Opening two new cards
* Paying card balances down before the statement closes
  Closing your oldest card
Why: Lower reported balances cut utilization, which counts for about 30% of a FICO score, and the score updates once the new balance is reported.
## Lesson glossary
- Soft inquiry: A credit check that does not affect your score, such as checking your own score or a pre-approval offer.
- Credit freeze: A free lock on your credit file at each bureau, so nobody can open new credit in your name until you lift it.

========== l-card-debt | Debt & Credit #2 | Credit card debt: get out, stay out (widget debtPayoff)
Summary: Why card interest is an emergency, the minimum-payment trap, and two payoff plans.
Idea: Card interest runs above 20%. Paying it off is a guaranteed return no investment can match.

### The goal: never pay card interest
Use cards for convenience and rewards, then pay the full statement balance every month. Do that and you pay no interest at all. Carry a balance and the rate is brutal: in August 2026, card accounts that were charged interest paid 22.36% on average.
Paying off a 22% card is like earning a guaranteed 22% return. No index fund or savings account promises that. The one exception worth keeping while you pay down cards is your 401(k) match, which is an instant return of 50% to 100% on the money you put in (see the match lesson).

### The minimum-payment trap
Many cards set the minimum at the month’s interest plus 1% of the balance. On $5,000 at 22.36%, paying only that minimum takes about 19 years and costs about $8,100 in interest. Paying a fixed $200 a month instead clears it in under three years for about $1,800.

### Avalanche or snowball

- Avalanche: pay minimums on everything, then put every extra dollar on the highest APR. It always saves the most interest.
- Snowball: put the extra on the smallest balance first. You clear a card sooner, and that win keeps many people going.Both roll the payment of each paid-off card into the next one. The best plan is the one you will keep doing. On a single card with several balances, the law already sends any amount above the minimum to the highest-rate balance first.

### A plan that sticks

- Stop adding: take the card out of your phone wallet and online stores.
- Build a small cushion, around $500 to $1,000, so a flat tire does not land on the card again (see the emergency fund lesson).
- List every debt with balance, APR and minimum. Pick avalanche or snowball.
- Automate a fixed payment above the minimums.
- Stop the interest: move the balance to a 0% card if you qualify (next lesson), or call the issuer and ask for a lower rate or a hardship plan.
### Avoid the traps on the way out
Debt settlement companies that promise to cut your balance in half usually tell you to stop paying, which wrecks your score and adds fees. A consolidation loan at a lower fixed rate can help, but only if the cards stay at zero afterward. A common trap is paying off the cards with a loan and then running them back up.

## Worked example: Three cards, $700 a month
Same three cards as the widget: $1,200 at 19.9%, $4,800 at 29.99% and $9,500 at 24.24%. Total $15,500.
1. Minimums only: the debt takes about 24 and a half years to clear and costs about $29,200 in interest.
2. Avalanche with $700 a month: pay the 29.99% card first, then the 24.24% card, then the 19.9% card. Debt-free in 30 months, about $5,410 of interest.
3. Snowball with $700 a month: pay the $1,200 card first. It is gone in month 6. Debt-free in 31 months, about $5,660 of interest.
4. Avalanche saves about $240 more than snowball. Snowball gives a first win nine months sooner (month 6 instead of month 15).
5. Moving the $4,800 card to a 0% offer would cut the interest further. That is the next lesson.
Result: Either plan saves about $23,500 to $23,800 compared with paying only the minimums.

## Mistakes
- Paying only the minimum. Most of each payment goes to interest and the balance barely moves.
- Investing in a taxable account while carrying a 22% card. Pay the card first; keep only the 401(k) match going.
- Paying off cards with a consolidation loan, then using the cards again.
- Having no cash cushion, so every surprise bill goes right back on the card.
## Takeaways
- Pay the full statement balance every month and you never pay card interest.
- Paying off a 22% card is a guaranteed 22% return.
- Avalanche (highest APR first) saves the most; snowball (smallest balance first) gives faster wins.
- Automate a fixed payment above the minimums and stop adding new charges.
## Quiz
Q: You have $2,000 at 27% and $6,000 at 18%. Which payoff order saves the most interest?
  The $6,000 card first, because it is bigger
* The 27% card first (avalanche)
  Split the extra money evenly
Why: Every extra dollar on the highest APR stops the most expensive interest first. That is the avalanche method.
## Lesson glossary
- Minimum payment: The smallest amount the card requires each month. Often the month’s interest plus about 1% of the balance.
- Statement balance: What you owed when the billing cycle closed. Paying it in full by the due date avoids interest on purchases.

========== l-balance-transfer | Debt & Credit #3 | 0% APR and balance transfers (widget transferPlanner)
Summary: Use 0% offers to stop interest, with the fee, the clock and the fine print in view.
Idea: A 0% transfer swaps high interest for a one-time fee. It only works if you clear the balance before the clock runs out.

### How a transfer works
You open a card with a 0% intro APR on balance transfers. The new card pays off your old card, and you owe the new card instead. You pay a balance transfer fee, usually 3% to 5% of the amount moved (often at least $5), added to the balance on day one. In October 2026 the best offers run 15 to 21 months at 0%.
On $8,000, a 3% fee is $240, paid once. Staying at 22% would cost about $150 of interest in the first month alone.

### The one number that matters
Divide the transferred balance plus the fee by the months at 0%. That is the monthly payment that clears it in time. For $8,000 with a $240 fee and 18 months: $8,240 ÷ 18 = $458 a month. Set it up as an automatic payment. Whatever is left when the promo ends starts charging the card’s regular rate.

### The fine print

- Same bank: you generally cannot transfer between two cards from the same issuer.
- Deadline: the 0% usually applies only to transfers made within the first 60 to 120 days.
- Purchases: on some cards the 0% covers only transfers, and new purchases charge interest. Do not use the transfer card for shopping.
- Late payments: read what happens if you pay late. Treat autopay as required.
- Approval: the limit you get may be lower than your balance. Move the highest-rate debt first.
### When the promo ends: transfer again?
If a balance remains, you can move it to another 0% card from a different bank. That works, but each move costs another fee, needs a new approval and adds a hard inquiry and a new account. If your score drops or offers dry up, you are stuck at the regular rate. Plan to finish on the first card and treat a second transfer as a backup, not the plan.

### Deferred interest is a different animal
Store cards and medical financing often say "no interest if paid in full in 12 months". That is deferred interest. If even $1 is left at the deadline, they charge all the interest back to day one, on the original amount. A true 0% intro APR only charges interest on what remains after the promo ends. Example: $5,000 financed, $1,000 left at the end. Deferred interest charges a year of interest on $5,000; a 0% card starts charging interest on $1,000.

### What it does to your score
The application adds a hard inquiry (usually fewer than 5 points, counted for a year) and a new account lowers your average age. The new limit lowers your overall utilization, which helps. Expect a small dip that fades as the inquiry ages and the balance falls.

## Worked example: $8,000 moved to an 18-month 0% card
Your card charges 22.36%. You qualify for a card with 0% for 18 months and a 3% fee.
1. Fee: 3% of $8,000 = $240. You now owe $8,240 at 0%.
2. Payment to finish in time: $8,240 ÷ 18 = $457.78, so you autopay $458 a month.
3. Staying put and paying $458 a month at 22.36% would take 22 months and about $1,770 of interest.
4. With the transfer you pay $240 in total. You save about $1,530.
5. If you could only pay $350 a month, $1,940 would be left at month 18 and start charging the regular rate. Raise the payment or plan a second transfer.
Result: A transfer works when the fee is small next to the interest and the payment fits inside the 0% window.

## Mistakes
- Moving the balance, then running the old card back up. Now you have two balances.
- Paying only the minimum on the new card and hitting the end of the promo with most of the balance left.
- Using the transfer card for new purchases that charge interest from day one.
- Treating a store card’s deferred-interest promo like a true 0% APR.
## Takeaways
- A transfer trades high interest for a one-time fee of about 3% to 5%.
- Payment to clear it in time = (balance + fee) ÷ months at 0%. Automate it.
- Transfers usually cannot go between two cards from the same bank, and must happen within the first months.
- Deferred interest charges everything back if any balance is left. A true 0% APR does not.
- A second transfer is a backup, not a plan: another fee, approval and inquiry.
## Quiz
Q: You finance a $3,000 sofa with "no interest if paid in full in 12 months" and owe $200 at month 12. What happens?
  Interest is charged only on the $200
* Interest from day one on the full $3,000 can be charged
  Nothing, the promo is extended
Why: That is deferred interest. Missing the deadline by any amount brings back all the interest from the start.

========== l-car-cash | Debt & Credit #4 | Car: pay cash or take the loan? (widget carCashVsLoan)
Summary: Compare the loan rate with what your cash earns after tax, then add the new deduction.
Idea: Take the loan only when its rate is below what your cash earns after tax. Otherwise paying cash wins.

### The rule
If you pay cash, you lose the interest that money would have earned. If you borrow, you pay the loan’s interest. So compare the loan APR with the yield on your cash after tax.
In October 2026, Vanguard’s Federal Money Market Fund (VMFXX) yielded about 3.83%. Its income is taxed as ordinary income. In the 22% federal bracket with 5% state tax, 3.83% shrinks to about 2.80%. The average new-car loan in the second quarter of 2026 was 6.35%, and the average used-car loan 11.19%. At those rates, paying cash wins.

### Taxes on your cash

- Money market income is taxed at your federal rate every year, even if you never withdraw it.
- Part of VMFXX’s income comes from US government debt, which most states let you leave out of state income (about two-thirds of it in 2025). A Treasury money market fund such as VUSXX or Treasury bills are almost fully state-tax-free, which helps in high-tax states.
- Money market funds are very low risk but are not FDIC-insured.
### The new car loan interest deduction (2025 to 2028)
A 2025 law lets you deduct up to $10,000 a year of interest on a car loan, whether or not you itemize, if:

- the car is new (you are its first owner) and had its final assembly in the US,
- the loan started after December 31, 2024, is for personal use and is secured by the car,
- your income is low enough: the deduction shrinks by $200 for each $1,000 of modified AGI above $100,000 (single) or $200,000 (married filing jointly).You report the car’s VIN on your return. Used cars and leases do not qualify. The deduction lowers the loan’s cost by your tax bracket, but at normal rates it rarely flips the answer.

### When the loan wins
Dealer promotions at 0% to about 2.9% can beat what your cash earns after tax. Then take the loan and leave the cash invested, and do not touch it. Watch for one catch: dealers often make you choose between promo financing and a cash rebate. Compare the rebate with what the cheap loan is worth.

### Keep the cushion, and mind depreciation
Pay cash only from money above your emergency fund of three to six months of expenses. A car paid in cash with no cushion left often ends with the next repair on a credit card.
The car loses value either way. New cars commonly lose 10% to 20% in the first year and roughly 35% to 60% over five years, depending on the model and miles. Long loans (72 months or more) can leave you owing more than the car is worth. Edmunds suggests keeping a new-car payment under about 15% of take-home pay.

### Shop the loan like a pro
Get a pre-approval from your bank or a credit union before you visit the dealer, then let the dealer try to beat it. Auto-loan inquiries made within a short window count as one for FICO. Negotiate the car price first, the financing second.

## Worked example: $35,000 new car: cash or 6.35% loan
You have $35,000 in VMFXX beyond your emergency fund. You are in the 22% bracket and your state has no income tax.
1. Loan: 60 months at 6.35% is $682.36 a month, about $5,940 of interest in total.
2. Your cash earns 3.83% before tax, 2.99% after 22% federal tax. Kept invested for five years, the $35,000 earns about $5,630 after tax.
3. Compare equal monthly budgets: pay cash and invest $682.36 a month instead, or take the loan and keep $35,000 invested. After 60 months, paying cash leaves you about $3,470 ahead.
4. If the car qualifies for the interest deduction and your income is under $100,000, the deduction saves about $1,310 of federal tax. Cash still wins by about $2,050.
5. Now the dealer offers 0% for 60 months ($583.33 a month). Keeping your cash invested wins by about $2,930, unless a cash rebate for paying up front is worth more.
Result: Borrow only when the loan rate is below your after-tax yield. At 6.35% against 2.99%, pay cash.

## Mistakes
- Comparing the loan APR with your yield before tax. Taxes shrink what your cash really earns.
- Draining the emergency fund to avoid a loan, then putting the next repair on a 22% card.
- Shopping by monthly payment. A 72- or 84-month loan makes any car look affordable and costs far more interest.
- Taking a 0% promo and giving up a bigger cash rebate without doing the math.
## Takeaways
- Compare the loan APR with your cash’s after-tax yield. The higher number decides.
- At average 2026 rates (6.35% new, 11.19% used), paying cash usually wins.
- Promo loans near 0% can beat cash; keep the cash invested and untouched.
- The 2025-2028 deduction helps on new US-assembled cars, but rarely changes the answer.
- Never pay cash with your emergency fund.
## Quiz
Q: Your loan offer is 5.5%. Your cash earns 4% in a money market and you pay 30% combined tax. Which is cheaper?
  Take the loan, because 4% is close to 5.5%
* Pay cash: 4% after 30% tax is 2.8%, well below 5.5%
  It is always better to borrow and invest
Why: After tax your cash earns 2.8%. Borrowing at 5.5% to keep earning 2.8% loses money.
## Lesson glossary
- Car depreciation: The value a car loses over time. Steepest in the first year for a new car.
- Upside down: Owing more on a car loan than the car is worth. Common with long loans and small down payments.

========== l-smart-leverage | Debt & Credit #5 | Leverage the smart way (widget leverageLab)
Summary: Good debt and bad, margin calls, leveraged ETFs and paying off a mortgage early.
Idea: Borrowing magnifies results both ways. Use cheap, fixed debt nobody can call in, for things that last, and never borrow to spend.

Leverage means owning more than your own money could buy. If you put $80,000 down on a $400,000 home, a 4% price rise is $16,000, which is 20% on your money. A 10% price fall wipes out half of it. The same tool that builds wealth can also force you to sell at the worst moment.

### A smart-leverage checklist

- It costs less, after tax, than what the asset is likely to earn, with room to spare.
- The rate is fixed and the term is long.
- Nobody can call it in or sell your assets when prices fall.
- It buys something that lasts or earns (a home, skills, a business), not a vacation or a car that loses value.
- You could still make the payment if your income dropped, and you keep an emergency fund.
### Kinds of borrowing, compared

- Fixed-rate mortgage: the classic good debt. Long, fixed and impossible to call in while you pay. The 30-year average was 7.40% in mid-October 2026. The interest is deductible only if you itemize, and only about one household in ten does.
- HELOC: a variable-rate line of credit on your home. The lender can freeze or cut the line if your finances or home value drop, and missed payments put the house at risk. Interest is deductible only if the money buys, builds or substantially improves that home.
- Margin loan: a loan from your broker against your investments. Federal rules let you borrow up to 50% of a purchase; you must keep at least 25% equity after that, and most brokers require 30% to 40%. The broker can sell your shares without asking, and you can lose more than you put in.
- Securities-based line of credit (SBLOC): borrows against a portfolio for anything except buying more securities. It is variable-rate and the lender can demand more collateral within days or sell your holdings without notice.
- 401(k) loan: up to the lesser of $50,000 or half your vested balance, repaid within five years. The interest goes back into your account, so the real cost is the growth you miss. If you leave your job, repay it by your tax-return due date or it becomes a taxable distribution, possibly with a 10% penalty.
- Credit cards: about 22% for people who carry a balance. Never borrow at that rate to invest. See getting out of card debt.
### Leveraged ETFs reset every day
A 2× or 3× fund aims to double or triple an index’s move for one day. Over months, the daily reset makes it drift away from 2× or 3× the index, most of all in choppy markets. In an SEC and FINRA example, an index rose 2% over four months while a 2× fund on it fell 6%.

### “Buy, borrow, die”
Wealthy families often borrow against stock they have held for decades instead of selling and paying capital gains tax. When they die, heirs get a step-up in basis and the untaxed gain disappears. It works only with a large taxable portfolio and survives only if a crash does not trigger calls on the loan while rates rise.

### Should you pay off the mortgage early?
Every extra dollar you pay earns a guaranteed return equal to the mortgage rate (after tax, if you itemize). Compare that with what safe money earns after tax: a 3-month Treasury bill yielded about 4.26% in October 2026. A 3% mortgage is cheap to keep; a 7.4% mortgage is expensive to keep. Do it in order: get the full 401(k) match, clear high-interest debt, build the emergency fund, then compare. Money paid into a house is hard to get back out.

## Worked example: A margin call, step by step
Made-up numbers: $50,000 of your own money plus $50,000 borrowed on margin buys $100,000 of stock. The broker requires 30% equity.
1. The market falls 20%. The stock is worth $80,000, the loan is still $50,000, so your equity is $30,000. Your own money is down 40%, twice the market’s fall.
2. The call comes when equity drops below 30% of the account: when the account falls under $50,000 ÷ 0.7 = $71,429. That is a 28.6% market drop.
3. The market falls 35%. The account is worth $65,000 and your equity is $15,000, only 23%. The broker needs 30% × $65,000 = $19,500, so it calls for $4,500.
4. You cannot add cash in time, so the broker sells shares near the low. Your $50,000 is now $15,000, a 70% loss locked in.
5. Without margin, the same drop takes $50,000 to $32,500. Painful, but nobody can force you to sell, and you can wait for a recovery.
Result: The same 35% crash cost 35% without margin and 70% with it, plus a forced sale.

## Mistakes
- Using margin or an SBLOC for a long-term bet. The lender can call the loan or sell your shares at the bottom.
- Moving card debt to a HELOC, then running the cards up again. Now the house backs the debt.
- Holding a 2× or 3× leveraged ETF for months and expecting 2× or 3× the index return.
- Rushing to prepay a cheap mortgage while you carry card debt or have no emergency fund.
## Takeaways
- Smart leverage is cheap after tax, fixed, long term and cannot be called in.
- Margin, SBLOCs and HELOCs can be called, frozen or sold out from under you in a crash.
- Leverage multiplies losses: a 20% drop with half the money borrowed costs 40% of yours.
- Leveraged ETFs track one day; held for long, they drift far from 2× or 3× the index.
- Prepaying a mortgage earns its after-tax rate, guaranteed; compare it with safe yields once the match, card debt and emergency fund are handled.
## Quiz
Q: You add $50,000 borrowed on margin to $50,000 of your own and buy stock. The market falls 20%. How much of your own money is gone?
  20%
* 40%
  10%
Why: The $100,000 position loses $20,000, and all of that comes out of your $50,000: 40%.
## Lesson glossary
- Leveraged ETF: A fund that aims for 2 or 3 times an index’s move for a single day, using borrowed money or derivatives.
- Maintenance requirement: The minimum share of a margin account that must be your own equity. Fall below it and you get a margin call.

### Who gains, and who does not

- Gains: people who travel abroad and take cash out, send wires, or will borrow for a home soon.
- Gains: people with investments to move who like having everything in one place.

4. Her idle cash now costs about $20,000 × (3.8% − 0.01%) ≈ $758 a year in interest given up.
5. She takes 10 cash withdrawals abroad a year that would cost about $8 each at Total Checking ($5 Chase fee plus the owner’s fee) and sends 3 wires at $25: about $155 of fees saved.
Result: Way two keeps her money working and still unlocks the perks. Way one costs over $6,000 a year to save $155.

- Parking a large sum in 0.01% checking just to reach a tier, and losing thousands of dollars of interest a year.

- Checking pays 0.01%. Idle cash is the hidden price of the perks.

4. His $30,000 of cash earns 0.01% instead of about 3.8%: roughly $30,000 × 3.79% ≈ $1,137 of interest given up.

- Judging the tier by its perk list instead of what you actually use in a year.

- Saying yes to managed accounts or annuities without asking for every fee in writing.
- Thinking the mortgage discount is a Private Client perk. Any Chase customer with the same balances gets it.

========== l-travel-money | Banking & Cards #3 | Travel money with no foreign fees (widget fxFees)
Summary: Cards, ATMs and the “pay in dollars?” question: keep fees off your trip.
Idea: Travel fees hide in three places: the card, the ATM and the exchange rate. A two-card setup and one word, “local”, removes almost all of them.

### Fee one: the card’s foreign transaction fee
Many credit cards add a foreign transaction fee, typically about 3%, to every purchase made in another currency or with a merchant abroad. On a $3,500 trip that is $105. Plenty of cards charge none: the Chase Sapphire Preferred ($95 a year) and Sapphire Reserve, every US Capital One card, and several others. Check the card’s terms before you go.

### Fee two: “Would you like to pay in dollars?”
Shops, restaurants and ATMs abroad often offer to charge you in US dollars. This is dynamic currency conversion. Its exchange rate is usually considerably worse, and your card may still add its foreign fee. Always choose the local currency and let your card network convert it.

### Fee three: ATMs

- Your bank may charge for using a non-network ATM abroad. Chase Total Checking charges $5 per withdrawal outside the US.
- Your bank may add a foreign exchange fee on the amount, often 3%.
- The ATM owner may add its own surcharge.
- The ATM may offer its own conversion. Decline it.Chase Premier Plus has no Chase ATM fee worldwide and no foreign exchange fee on its debit card; Private Client also refunds the owner’s fee (see bank tiers). Other banks and brokerages offer similar cards. Take fewer, larger withdrawals, and skip airport currency counters.

### A simple setup

- A credit card with no foreign transaction fee for purchases.
- A debit card with low or no ATM and foreign fees, for cash.
- A backup card kept in a different place, in case one is lost or blocked.
- Pay the card in full when you get home (see card debt).

## Worked example: Two weeks in Lisbon
Sam spends $3,500 by card and takes $800 in cash over 6 withdrawals.
1. His card charges 3% on foreign purchases: $3,500 × 0.03 = $105.
2. He says “dollars” for about $1,050 of payments, and the worse rate costs about 5% in this example: about $53 more.
3. His bank charges $5 per foreign withdrawal and the ATM owner $4: 6 × $9 = $54.
4. His bank adds 3% on the $800 of cash: $24. Total fees: $105 + $53 + $54 + $24 = $236.
5. Next trip he uses a no-foreign-fee card, always picks euros, and takes 3 withdrawals with a fee-free debit card: only the owner’s fee is left, 3 × $4 = $12.
Result: Same trip: $236 in fees versus $12.

## Mistakes
- Choosing “pay in dollars” because it feels familiar. The exchange rate is usually considerably worse.
- Packing only a card that charges 3% on every foreign purchase.
- Taking many small ATM withdrawals and paying the fixed fees every time.
- Traveling with one card. If it is lost or frozen, you have no money.
## Takeaways
- Use a credit card with no foreign transaction fee for purchases.
- Always pay in the local currency. Decline dynamic currency conversion at shops and ATMs.
- Use a debit card with no foreign ATM or exchange fees, and take fewer, larger withdrawals.
- Chase Premier Plus waives Chase ATM fees worldwide; Private Client also refunds the owner’s fee.

========== l-rewards | Banking & Cards #4 | Rewards cards without the trap (widget rewardsCalc)
Summary: Points vs annual fees vs interest: when a rewards card actually pays.
Idea: Rewards are a small rebate on spending you would do anyway. A year of carried interest wipes out a year of points.

### How rewards work
Cards earn cash back or points on each dollar you spend. A flat card might pay 2% on everything. A travel card pays more in bonus categories: the Chase Sapphire Preferred, for example, earns 3 points per dollar on dining and gas, 2 on most travel and 1 on everything else, for a $95 annual fee. A point is often worth 1¢ as cash and can be worth more when moved to airline or hotel partners.

### Does the fee pay for itself?
Compare the extra rewards with a no-fee flat card, then subtract the fee. A fee card wins only if you spend a lot in its bonus categories and use the points well. Perks such as travel credits count only if you would have spent that money anyway.

### The trap: interest
In August 2026 the average APR on card accounts paying interest was about 22.4% (Federal Reserve). Carry a $2,500 balance for a year at that rate and you pay about $560, more than most people earn in rewards. Rewards only make sense if you pay the full statement balance every month. If you carry debt, pay it off first (see card debt and 0% transfers).

### Sign-up bonuses and your credit score
Bonuses can be worth hundreds of dollars but require spending a set amount in the first few months. Never spend extra just to hit the target. Each application is a hard inquiry, usually costing a few points for about a year, and new cards lower the average age of your accounts. Keep credit utilization low, ideally under 10% (see credit scores).

### Travel features worth having

- No foreign transaction fee (see travel money).
- Travel protections you understand, such as trip delay or rental car coverage.
- A card on a widely accepted network for use abroad.

## Worked example: Flat card or travel card?
Ana spends $400 on dining, $600 on groceries, $250 on travel, $150 on gas and $1,500 on everything else each month: $34,800 a year.
1. A flat 2% card earns $34,800 × 0.02 = $696 a year, with no fee.
2. The travel card earns 3 points on dining and gas ($6,600 × 3 = 19,800), 2 on travel ($3,000 × 2 = 6,000) and 1 on the rest ($25,200): 51,000 points.
3. At 1¢ a point that is $510, minus the $95 fee: $415. The flat card wins by $281.
4. If she moves points to travel partners at 1.5¢, they are worth $765, minus $95: $670. Still $26 behind the flat card.
5. If she carried a $2,500 balance at 22.4% all year, about $560 of interest would wipe out most of either card’s rewards.
Result: For Ana, a simple 2% card paid in full beats the fee card.

## Mistakes
- Carrying a balance on a rewards card. Interest above 20% cancels the rewards many times over.
- Spending more to chase points or a sign-up bonus.
- Paying an annual fee for credits and perks you would not have used anyway.
- Valuing points at their best possible rate when you actually redeem them for cash.
## Takeaways
- Rewards are a rebate of roughly 1%-3% of spending. Card interest averages over 20% a year.
- Pay the full statement balance every month, or skip rewards cards until you can.
- A fee card wins only with heavy bonus-category spending and good point use.
- For travel, a card with no foreign transaction fee matters more than the points.
## Quiz
Q: You carry a $3,000 balance at 24% APR and earn 2% back on $30,000 a year of spending. How do they compare?
  Rewards of $600 beat interest of $720
* Interest of about $720 beats rewards of $600
  They cancel out exactly
Why: $3,000 × 24% ≈ $720 of interest versus $30,000 × 2% = $600 of rewards. Paying in full is worth more than any card.
## Lesson glossary
- Sign-up bonus: Extra points or cash a card pays if you spend a set amount within the first few months.
- Cents per point: What one reward point is worth when you redeem it. About 1¢ as cash, sometimes more through travel partners.

========== l-insurance | Protect What You Build #1 | Insurance you actually need (widget insuranceNeeds)
Summary: Life, disability and lawsuit cover: what to buy, how much, and what to skip.
Idea: Insurance protects the plan, not your returns. Buy cheap cover for disasters you could never pay for, and self-insure the small stuff.

### What insurance is for
Insurance moves a risk you could not survive onto a company that pools it with thousands of others. Use it for the big, rare disasters: an early death, years without income, a lawsuit, a house fire. For small costs, an emergency fund and higher deductibles are cheaper than paying premiums every year.

### Life insurance: term, not a savings plan
If anyone depends on your income or your unpaid work, such as raising children, you need life insurance. For most families the right kind is term: a fixed premium for 10 to 30 years and a payout only if you die in that window. It is cheap because most policies never pay.
Whole life and other permanent policies bundle insurance with a savings account. They cost several times more for the same payout and come with commissions and surrender charges. For most people, buying term and investing the difference in a 401(k) or IRA leaves more money. Permanent insurance can fit narrow cases, such as funding a trust for a disabled child, and that is a job for a fee-only adviser.

### How much life cover: the DIME method

- Debts: cards, car and student loans.
- Income: your yearly income times the years your family would need it.
- Mortgage: the balance, so the family keeps the home.
- Education: what you want to set aside for college.Subtract savings your family could use. Pick a term long enough to cover the youngest child to adulthood or the mortgage to payoff. A stay-at-home parent needs cover too: replacing childcare and household work is expensive.
Group life through work is usually one or two times salary and ends when the job ends. Treat it as a bonus, not the plan.

### Disability insurance: protect the paycheck
Early in your career, your future paychecks are worth more than everything you own. A long illness or injury is more likely than an early death, and it stops the income while the bills keep coming.

- Percent replaced and the cap. Group long-term disability pays a share of base pay up to a monthly maximum. Bonuses and stock often are not covered.
- Definition of disability. “Own occupation” pays if you cannot do your job; “any occupation” pays only if you cannot do any job. Own-occupation is better and costs more.
- Waiting period. Checks start after 30 to 180 days. Your emergency fund covers the gap.
- Taxes. If your employer pays the premium, the benefit is taxable income. If you pay with after-tax money, it is tax-free (IRS Publication 525).Social Security disability exists, but approval is strict and the check is modest. An individual policy can top up a thin group plan and follows you between jobs.

### Lawsuits: liability limits and umbrellas
Your car and home (or renters) policies include liability cover: they pay if you injure someone or damage their property. State minimums for car liability are low. As your savings grow, raise those limits and add an umbrella policy that sits on top of both and pays legal defense too. It is usually sold in $1 million layers and costs far less than the home or car policy beneath it. Teen drivers, pools, dogs and rental properties all raise the risk.

### Home, renters, car and health

- Home: insure the cost to rebuild, not the market price. Flood and earthquake need separate policies.
- Renters: covers your belongings and liability for little money. Landlords’ policies do not cover your things.
- Car: collision and comprehensive matter less as a car ages; liability matters more as you get richer.
- Health: picking a plan is its own lesson: see open enrollment and, if you retire early, health insurance before 65.
### Usually skip

- Credit life and mortgage life insurance: they pay the lender and shrink over time. Term covers the same debt for less.
- Accident-only, cancer-only and flight insurance: narrow cover for rare events.
- Extended warranties on electronics and appliances: your emergency fund handles these.
- Any policy sold mainly as an investment.

## Worked example: A young family runs the DIME numbers
Sam earns $85,000, has two kids, a mortgage and some savings. All numbers are made up.
1. Debts: a $20,000 car loan and $5,000 on cards, so $25,000.
2. Income: $85,000 a year for 15 years, until the youngest finishes school, is $1,275,000.
3. Mortgage: $300,000 left. Education: $60,000 for each of two kids, so $120,000.
4. Total need: $25,000 + $1,275,000 + $300,000 + $120,000 = $1,720,000.
5. Minus $70,000 of savings and $85,000 of group life through work leaves a gap of $1,565,000.
6. Sam rounds up and prices a 20-year term policy for $1.6 million. His partner, who handles childcare, gets her own term policy too.
Result: A 20-year term policy of about $1.6 million covers the family. The work policy is a bonus, because it ends if Sam changes jobs.

## Mistakes
- Relying only on life insurance through work. It is usually small and ends when the job does.
- Buying whole life as an investment without comparing it with term plus a 401(k) or IRA. The fees and surrender charges are easy to miss.
- Skipping disability insurance, or never reading whether the group plan covers your own occupation and how much it caps.
- Keeping state-minimum car liability while your savings grow. One serious accident can reach your house and brokerage account.
## Takeaways
- Insure the disasters you cannot afford; self-insure the small stuff with an emergency fund.
- Most families need term life insurance, sized with DIME, for as long as others depend on them.
- Disability cover protects your biggest early asset: your future paychecks.
- Raise car and home liability limits and add an umbrella once you have real savings.
## Quiz
Q: Your employer pays the premium for your long-term disability plan. If you collect benefits, how are they taxed?
  They are tax-free
* They are taxable income
  They are taxed as capital gains
Why: When the employer pays the premium, the benefits count as taxable income. Benefits from a policy you paid for with after-tax money are tax-free.
## Lesson glossary
- DIME method: A simple way to size life insurance: Debts + Income × years + Mortgage + Education, minus savings.
- Own-occupation: A disability policy that pays if you cannot do your own job, even if you could do a different one.
- Waiting period: The days you must be disabled before disability benefits start.

- IRS Publication 525: Taxable and Nontaxable Income (sickness and injury benefits) — https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525

========== l-estate | Protect What You Build #2 | Wills, beneficiaries and trusts (widget estateMap)
Summary: Make sure the right people get your money quickly, without court delays or old mistakes.
Idea: Most money passes by beneficiary forms, not your will. Keep the forms current, then use a will or trust for everything else.

### The roads money takes when you die

- Beneficiary form: 401(k), IRA, HSA, life insurance and annuities go to the people named on the form.
- Payable-on-death (POD) and transfer-on-death (TOD): bank and brokerage accounts, and in many states cars and real estate, can name heirs the same way.
- Joint ownership with right of survivorship: the other owner simply keeps it.
- A trust: whatever is titled in the trust follows the trust’s instructions.
- Everything else goes through your will and the court process called probate. With no will, state law decides who inherits.
### Beneficiary forms beat the will
If your will says “everything to my spouse” but an old IRA form names your sister, your sister gets the IRA. Workplace plans pay whoever the plan’s form names, and the US Supreme Court has upheld paying an ex-spouse named on an old form. Review every form after a marriage, divorce, birth or death.

- Name a primary and a contingent (backup) beneficiary on every account.
- For a 401(k), federal law generally makes your spouse the beneficiary unless your spouse signs a written waiver.
- Minors cannot take money directly. Name a trust or a custodian under your state’s Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (UTMA), or a court may appoint someone.
- Avoid naming “my estate”: it pushes the account into probate and can make heirs empty an inherited IRA faster.
### The will: guardians first
If you have young children, the most important line in a will names their guardian. A will also names the executor who settles your affairs and says who gets anything without a beneficiary or joint owner. Probate is public, can take months to more than a year, and costs court and legal fees that vary a lot by state.

### Revocable living trust
You control the trust while alive and can change it any time. At death, your chosen trustee hands out the assets without probate, privately. It helps most if you own real estate in more than one state, want to control how heirs receive money, or want someone to step in smoothly if you become unable to manage things. It only works for assets you actually retitle into it, which is called funding the trust. A short “pour-over” will catches anything you forgot.

### Documents for while you are alive

- Durable financial power of attorney: lets someone you trust pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot.
- Healthcare power of attorney and living will (an advance directive): names who speaks for you with doctors and records your wishes.
- HIPAA release: lets family get medical information.Without these, your family may need a court-ordered guardianship to act for you, which is slow and costly.

### Taxes at death

- The federal estate and gift tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, set by the 2025 tax law and adjusted for inflation in later years. Very few estates owe federal estate tax. A surviving spouse can also claim the unused part of the first spouse’s exemption (called portability) by filing an estate tax return.
- You can give up to $19,000 per person in 2026 without filing a gift tax return; larger gifts just use up part of the lifetime exemption.
- Some states have their own estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds.
- Step-up in basis: stocks and funds inherited in a taxable account get a new cost basis equal to their value at death, so the gain built up during your life is never taxed. Inherited IRAs and 401(k)s are different: heirs pay income tax on withdrawals, and most non-spouse heirs must empty them within 10 years.
### Make it easy to find
Keep a one-page list of accounts, policies, the location of documents and how to reach your adviser or attorney. Tell your executor where it is. Many accounts and phones now let you name a legacy contact for digital access.

## Worked example: The old form that beats the new will
Dan, 52, remarried. Every name and number here is made up.
1. Dan has a $400,000 401(k), $300,000 of life insurance through work, a $150,000 brokerage account, $60,000 in the bank, and a home owned jointly with his wife, Maya.
2. His new will splits everything between Maya and his two children from his first marriage.
3. The work life insurance form, signed in 2009, still names his ex-wife. Workplace plans pay whoever the form names, so the $300,000 can go to her.
4. The brokerage and bank accounts have no TOD or POD, so $210,000 goes through probate under the will: court filings, fees and months of waiting.
5. The fix takes an afternoon: a new life insurance form naming Maya and a trust for the children, TOD and POD on the accounts, and a talk with Maya about the 401(k), which goes to her by law unless she signs a waiver.
Result: Before: $300,000 to the wrong person and $210,000 stuck in probate. After: every asset has a direct road, and the will only handles the leftovers.

## Mistakes
- Never updating beneficiary forms after a divorce, marriage or new child. The form wins over the will.
- Naming young children directly as beneficiaries instead of a trust or custodian.
- Paying for a living trust and never retitling accounts and the home into it, so everything still goes through probate.
- Planning only for death and skipping the power of attorney and healthcare directive you would need if you were alive but unable to act.
## Takeaways
- Beneficiary forms, TOD/POD and joint ownership decide most of where money goes. Check them every year.
- A will names guardians for your children and handles whatever has no other road.
- A funded revocable living trust skips probate and keeps things private.
- In 2026 the federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person, so most families should focus on paperwork, not estate tax.
## Quiz
Q: Your will leaves everything to your spouse, but your IRA’s beneficiary form names your sister. Who gets the IRA?
  Your spouse, because the will is newer
* Your sister, because the form controls the account
  A probate court splits it between them
Why: Accounts with a beneficiary form pass by the form, outside the will. Keep the forms current.
## Lesson glossary
- TOD / POD: A designation on a brokerage or bank account that passes it straight to named people, outside probate.
- Executor: The person your will names to settle your estate: pay bills, file taxes and hand out what is left.
- Intestacy: Dying without a will. State law then decides who inherits.
- Portability: A surviving spouse claiming the unused part of the first spouse’s estate tax exemption by filing an estate tax return.

- IRS: What’s new, estate and gift tax (2026 basic exclusion $15,000,000; annual exclusion $19,000) — https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/whats-new-estate-and-gift-tax

========== l-scams | Protect What You Build #3 | Scams that target savers (widget scamSpotter)
Summary: Spot impostor calls, fake investments and romance scams before they reach your savings.
Idea: Every scam rushes you, isolates you and asks for money you cannot get back. Slow down and check on your own.

### How big the problem is
Americans reported $15.9 billion lost to fraud in 2025 to the Federal Trade Commission, up from about $12.5 billion in 2024. Investment scams caused the most damage, $7.9 billion, and impostor scams were the most common, with more than a million reports. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center counted $20.9 billion in reported losses, including $7.7 billion from people aged 60 and over. Savers and retirees are targets because they have money in accounts that can be moved quickly.

### The five red flags

- Out of the blue: a call, text, email, pop-up or new online friend you did not expect.
- Pressure: a deadline, a threat of arrest or a locked account, or a once-only chance.
- Secrecy: “do not tell your bank”, “do not tell Mom”.
- Payment you cannot reverse: gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, cash handed to a courier, or a “safe account”.
- Too good to be true: high returns with no risk, or a fee you must pay before you can withdraw.
### The scams to know

- Impostors: fake bank fraud teams, IRS, Social Security, police or tech support. Caller ID can be faked to show a real name.
- Investment and romance scams: a friendly stranger, often after a “wrong number” text, introduces a trading app or crypto platform that shows fake profits. The ending is always a fee or “tax” to withdraw.
- Grandparent and family emergencies: a panicked call, sometimes with an AI-cloned voice, asking for bail or hospital money.
- Phishing and smishing: emails and texts that copy real companies to steal logins and one-time codes.
- Fake checks and overpayments: you are sent too much and asked to send the difference back before the check bounces.
### Defenses that work

- Hang up and call back on a number you already trust: the back of your card, the app, or a site you type yourself.
- Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. It is free and stops new accounts in your name; you can lift it when you apply for credit.
- Use two-factor authentication, ideally an authenticator app, and never read a one-time code to anyone.
- Agree on a family code word for emergencies.
- Add a trusted contact person to your brokerage and retirement accounts, so the firm has someone to call if something looks wrong.
- Check any adviser or firm on FINRA BrokerCheck before you send money (see choosing a broker).
- Real government agencies never demand gift cards, crypto or wire transfers.
### If it already happened

- Call your bank or broker right away. Wires and transfers can sometimes be stopped if you act fast.
- Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI at ic3.gov. Use IdentityTheft.gov if your identity was stolen.
- For people 60 and over, the National Elder Fraud Hotline is 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311).
- Change passwords, freeze your credit, and watch for “recovery” services that promise to get your money back for a fee. That is a second scam.

## Worked example: The “safe account” call

1. Your phone shows your bank’s name. The caller says your account was hacked and reads the last four digits of your card to sound real.
2. They say a branch employee is involved, so you must not tell anyone there, and you need to move $48,000 to a “safe account” today.
3. Count the flags: unexpected call, pressure, secrecy, and moving money to protect it. Real banks never ask you to move money to keep it safe.
4. You hang up and call the number on the back of your card. The bank confirms your account is fine and notes the attempt.
5. You freeze your credit at the three bureaus and add a trusted contact to your brokerage account. It takes about 20 minutes.
Result: Hanging up and calling back on a number you trust beat the whole script, and the $48,000 never moved.

## Mistakes
- Trusting caller ID or a sender name. Both are easy to fake.
- Thinking you are too careful to fall for it. Investment scams are slow, friendly and convincing, and they target smart, busy people.
- Paying a “tax” or “fee” to unlock a withdrawal from an app. Money that needs a fee to come out is already gone.
- Staying quiet out of embarrassment. Fast reporting is the only chance to stop a transfer, and it helps protect others.
## Takeaways
- Unexpected contact, pressure, secrecy and payments you cannot reverse mark nearly every scam.
- Hang up, then contact the company yourself on a number or site you already know.
- A free credit freeze, two-factor authentication and a family code word block most attacks.
- If money moved, call your bank at once and report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov.
## Quiz
Q: A caller from “your bank’s fraud team” says your money is at risk and asks you to move it to a safe account today. What should you do?
  Move it quickly; they already know your card number
* Hang up and call the number on the back of your card
  Ask them to email you the account details first
Why: Banks never ask you to move money to protect it. Calling back on a number you trust ends the scam.
## Lesson glossary
- Credit freeze: A free lock on your credit report at each bureau that stops new accounts being opened in your name until you lift it.
- Phishing: Messages that copy a real company to trick you into giving a login, code or payment. By text it is called smishing.
- Spoofing: Faking a caller ID, sender name or web address so a scam looks like it comes from someone you trust.
- Trusted contact person: Someone your broker may call if it suspects fraud or cannot reach you. They cannot trade or see your account.

========== l-compound | Investing for Decades #1 | The power of compound interest (widget compoundPlus)
Summary: Your age, your money and time: how growth on growth builds wealth.
Idea: Money that earns returns on its past returns grows slowly at first, then fast. Time matters more than amount.

### Growth on growth
Put $10,000 in an investment that earns 7% a year. Year one adds $700. Year two adds $749, because you now earn on $10,700. Leave it alone and it becomes about $19,700 after 10 years, $38,700 after 20 and $76,100 after 30. You added nothing after day one. That snowball is compound interest.
The curve looks flat for years and then bends upward. Most of the money arrives at the end, which is why people who start early look like geniuses and people who start late feel stuck.

### The rule of 72
Divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate how long money takes to double. At 7% it doubles about every 10.3 years. At 10%, every 7.2 years. At 4%, every 18 years. Flip it around for debt: a credit card at about 21% (the average rate on all card accounts in August 2026) doubles what you owe in under four years if you pay nothing.

### Time beats amount
Alex saves $300 a month from 25 to 35, then stops: $36,000 in total. Bo saves the same $300 a month from 35 to 65: $108,000 in total. At 7% a year, Alex ends at 65 with about $421,000 and Bo with about $366,000. Alex put in a third as much and still finished ahead, because Alex’s money had 30 extra years to compound.
The best time to start was years ago. The second best is your next payday. Small amounts count; automating them counts more (see where extra money should go).

### Today’s dollars
Prices rise too. Consumer prices were up 3.7% in the year to September 2026. At 3% inflation, $1 million in 40 years buys what about $307,000 buys today. A 7% return with 3% inflation is roughly a 4% real return. Turn on “Today’s dollars” in the chart to plan in money you can picture.

### What return should you use?

- No return is promised. Stocks have grown faster than bonds or cash over long periods, with big drops on the way: a world stock fund lost 18% in 2022 alone.
- For planning, many people test 5% to 7% a year for a stock-heavy mix and check that the plan still works at the low end.
- Fees and taxes come straight off the top, so they compound against you. Keep both low (see index funds and asset location).
### What breaks the snowball

- Stopping: cashing out a 401(k) when you change jobs costs tax, often a 10% penalty, and every future year of growth.
- Selling in a crash: you lock in the loss and miss the recovery.
- High-interest debt: it compounds against you faster than investments compound for you. Pay it off first (see credit card debt).

## Worked example: Starting at 25 vs 35
Two friends each invest $400 a month in a low-cost stock index fund. Assume 7% a year, compounded monthly.
1. Maya starts at 25 and invests until 65: 40 years × 12 × $400 = $192,000 of her own money.
2. Jordan starts at 35 and invests until 65: 30 years × 12 × $400 = $144,000.
3. At 65 Maya has about $1,049,900. Growth is $857,900 of it, more than four times what she put in.
4. At 65 Jordan has about $488,000. Growth is $344,000.
5. Maya saved $48,000 more but ends with about $561,900 more. The extra decade did most of the work.
Result: Ten more years of compounding more than doubled the result. Rule of 72: at 7% money doubles about every 10 years, so the first decade’s dollars double one extra time.

## Mistakes
- Waiting until you “have real money” to start. $100 a month at 22 can beat $300 a month at 40.
- Expecting a steady 7% every year. Real returns jump around, and a crash right before you retire hurts most (see sequence risk).
- Forgetting inflation. A $1 million goal in 40 years buys roughly what $307,000 buys today at 3% inflation.
- Breaking the chain: cashing out retirement accounts at a job change or selling everything in a downturn.
## Takeaways
- Compounding is growth on growth: slow at first, then fast.
- Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ yearly return.
- Starting ten years earlier can be worth more than saving twice as much later.
- Plan in today’s dollars by subtracting inflation from your return.
- Debt, fees and taxes compound too, against you.
## Quiz
Q: At about 8% a year, roughly how long does money take to double?
  About 5 years
* About 9 years
  About 15 years
Why: Rule of 72: 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years.

========== l-index-funds | Investing for Decades #2 | Index funds: VTWAX, VTI and VOO (widget fundCompare)
Summary: What the most popular index funds hold, what they cost and how far they fall.
Idea: One low-cost fund can own thousands of companies. Diversified and cheap, but still 100% stocks.

### What an index fund is
An index fund buys every company in a market index, such as the S&P 500, in the same proportions. Nobody picks stocks, so fees are tiny and you get the market’s return minus a sliver. Over long periods, most stock pickers struggle to beat their index once fees are counted. More on the basics in index funds and ETFs.

### Mutual fund or ETF twin
Vanguard sells most of its index funds two ways. The mutual fund (VTWAX, VTSAX, VFIAX) trades once a day at the closing price and needs $3,000 to start at Vanguard. The ETF twin (VT, VTI, VOO) trades like a stock all day at any broker for the price of one share, or less where fractional shares are offered. Inside, they own the same stocks.
If you bank at Chase, J.P. Morgan Self-Directed Investing charges $0 for online stock and ETF trades, so VT, VTI or VOO cost nothing to buy there. Whether it offers Vanguard’s mutual funds is something to check on its fund list; the ETF twin is the easy route.

### Five funds, side by side

- Total world (VTWAX, VT): about 10,180 stocks, roughly 61% US and 38% abroad. Fees 0.09% and 0.06% a year.
- Total US (VTSAX, VTI): about 3,500 US companies of every size. 0.04% and 0.03%.
- S&P 500 (VFIAX, VOO): about 500 of the largest US companies. 0.04% and 0.03%.
- Target-date 2060 (VTTSX): one fund of six index funds, about 90% stocks today and moving toward bonds as 2060 nears. 0.08%, $1,000 to start.
- Money market (VMFXX, VUSXX): short-term government debt. About 3.8% and 3.9% yields on October 19, 2026.
### “The safest fund”? Not quite
VTWAX is about as diversified as one fund gets: no single company or country can sink it. That is not the same as safe. It is 100% stocks. It lost 18.0% in 2022, and its ETF twin VT fell about 50% from June 2008 to March 2009, 35% in early 2020 and 28% from late 2021 to October 2022.
For money you need within a few years, the safe places are FDIC-insured savings, Treasury bills and government or Treasury money market funds (see emergency fund). A world stock fund is a great home for money you will not touch for 10 years or more, as long as you can sit through a 50% drop without selling.

### Fees: small numbers, big money
A fee comes off every year, and it also takes the growth those dollars would have earned. With $50,000 to start and $500 a month for 30 years at 7%, a 0.06% fund ends near $1,001,000. The same money at 1% ends near $803,000. That gap of about $198,000 went to fees.

### How to choose

- One fund: a total world fund, or a target-date fund near your retirement year. Simple and complete.
- Two or three funds: a total US fund plus a total international fund (and a bond fund if you want one), set in the mix you choose.
- Do not stack overlapping funds. The S&P 500 companies already make up most of a total US fund by value, and a target-date fund already holds both.

## Worked example: $10,000 in a world fund through 2022
You invest $10,000 in VTWAX on January 1, 2022. These are the fund’s actual calendar-year returns.
1. 2022: −18.0%. Your $10,000 is $8,200 at year end. The fee that year was about $9 (0.09%).
2. You hold. 2023: +21.9%, so $8,200 × 1.219 = $9,995.80. Almost back to even.
3. 2024: +16.4%, so $9,995.80 × 1.164 = $11,635.11.
4. 2025: +22.4%, so $11,635.11 × 1.224 = $14,241.38.
5. Someone who sold at the end of 2022 locked in a $1,800 loss and missed a $6,041 recovery.
Result: Holding through the drop turned $10,000 into about $14,241 in four years. Diversified does not mean it cannot fall; it means you can afford to wait.

## Mistakes
- Calling a 100% stock fund “safe” and using it for a house deposit due next year.
- Owning an S&P 500 fund, a total US fund and a target-date fund and thinking that is three times the diversification. It is mostly the same stocks.
- Paying 1% or more for a fund that tracks the same market a 0.03% fund tracks.
- Switching to whichever fund did best last year.
## Takeaways
- Index funds own a whole market at very low cost.
- VTWAX/VT is the whole world in one fund; VTSAX/VTI the whole US; VFIAX/VOO the 500 largest US companies.
- Diversified is not safe: every stock fund here lost 17% to 20% in 2022.
- Mutual fund and ETF twins hold the same stocks; the ETF is easier to buy at other brokers.
- Fees compound against you; under 0.10% is the target.

========== l-allocation | Investing for Decades #3 | Stocks, bonds and your age (widget allocationSim)
Summary: How your mix of stocks, bonds and cash sets your growth and your worst years.
Idea: Your mix of stocks, bonds and cash decides most of your risk and return. Match it to when you need the money.

### Three ingredients

- Stocks: the growth engine, with big swings. A world stock fund lost 18.0% in 2022.
- Bonds: loans to governments and companies. Steadier, but they can fall when interest rates jump: a total US bond fund lost 13.2% in 2022 (see bonds).
- Cash: savings, money market funds, T-bills. Steady, and in October 2026 paying about 3.8% to 4.3%, roughly the 3.7% inflation rate.
### What a mix did in 2022
A 60/40 mix (60% stocks, 40% bonds) lost about 0.6 × 18.0% + 0.4 × 13.2% ≈ 16.1% in 2022. That year was unusual: bonds usually cushion a stock crash, but rates rose so fast that both fell together. The “In a 2022-type year” tile shows what your mix would have done.

### Rules of thumb

- “110 minus your age” in stocks is a common starting point: about 80% at 30, 50% at 60.
- Target-date funds do it for you. Vanguard’s 2060 fund is about 90% stocks today and shifts toward bonds every year as 2060 gets closer. That schedule is called the glide path.
- Rules of thumb ignore your pension, Social Security, job security and nerves. Adjust for them.
### Rebalancing
When stocks race ahead, they become a bigger slice than you chose, and your risk rises without you deciding it. Once a year (or when a slice is 5 points off), sell some of what grew and buy what lagged. Do it inside retirement accounts or with new money to avoid taxes. A target-date fund rebalances itself.

### Time horizon beats age
A 30-year-old saving for a house in three years should not hold that money in stocks, and a 60-year-old’s money for age 85 can hold plenty. Give each goal its own mix (see where extra money should go).

### The mix you can live with
The best allocation is the one you will not abandon in a crash. A 90/10 mix that you sell in a panic does worse than a 60/40 mix you hold. Picture a 30% to 50% drop in your stock money and ask what you would do (see drawdowns).

## Worked example: Rebalancing a 60/40 mix after 2022
You start 2022 with $100,000: $60,000 in a world stock fund and $40,000 in a total bond fund.
1. Stocks fall 18.0% to $49,200. Bonds fall 13.2% to $34,720. Total: $83,920, down 16.1%.
2. Your mix is now 58.6% stocks and 41.4% bonds. Target stocks: 60% × $83,920 = $50,352.
3. You sell $1,152 of bonds and buy $1,152 of stocks, back to 60/40. Inside an IRA this costs no tax.
4. 2023: stocks +21.9% ($50,352 → $61,379) and bonds +5.7% ($33,568 → $35,481).
5. Total: $96,860. Rebalancing made you buy stocks when they were cheaper, without guessing.
Result: A simple once-a-year rule kept the risk you chose and bought low automatically.

## Mistakes
- Choosing an aggressive mix during a boom, then selling stocks in the next crash.
- Keeping 30-year money in cash, or next year’s money in stocks.
- Never rebalancing, so stocks drift to 80% or more without you choosing it.
- Holding a target-date fund next to other funds, which undoes its careful mix.
## Takeaways
- Your stock, bond and cash mix drives most of your risk and return.
- More stocks: higher middle outcome, deeper bad years.
- Match each pot of money to when you need it.
- Rebalance once a year, ideally inside tax-sheltered accounts.
- Pick the mix you will hold in a crash.
## Quiz
Q: In a year when stocks fall 18% and bonds fall 13%, a 60/40 mix loses about…
  18%
* 16%
  8%
Why: 0.6 × 18% + 0.4 × 13.2% ≈ 16.1%.

========== l-extra-money | Investing for Decades #4 | Where your extra money should go (widget extraMoney)
Summary: Match money to when you need it, pick an account, automate and invest.
Idea: Money you need soon stays safe. Money you will not touch for years goes into low-cost stock funds, automatically.

### First, the order

- A starter emergency fund, then three to six months of costs (emergency fund).
- Pay off high-interest debt such as credit cards (card debt).
- Get the full 401(k) match (the match).
- Fill an IRA or HSA (which account first).
- Then a taxable brokerage account for everything else.
### Match the money to the date

- Within 2 years: cash. Top high-yield savings paid about 4.0% to 4.3% in October 2026, Vanguard’s Federal Money Market (VMFXX) about 3.8%, and 3-month Treasury bills 4.26%. T-bill interest is free of state income tax.
- 2 to 5 years: mostly bonds, CDs or a ladder of Treasuries, with little or no stock.
- 5 to 10 years: a balanced mix.
- 10+ years: mostly low-cost stock index funds, such as a total world fund.
### Opening a taxable brokerage account
Any large broker works: Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, or J.P. Morgan Self-Directed Investing if you bank at Chase. J.P. Morgan charges $0 for online stock and ETF trades and lets you set up free recurring investments. Those investment balances also count toward Chase relationship tiers (see banking relationships). J.P. Morgan’s robo-adviser has been shut down, so Self-Directed means you pick the funds.
A simple choice: VTWAX at Vanguard ($3,000 to start), or its ETF twin VT at almost any broker, J.P. Morgan included. More on picking a broker in choosing a broker.

### Taxes in a taxable account
You pay tax each year on dividends and when you sell at a gain. Qualified dividends and long-term gains get the 0%, 15% or 20% rates. In 2026 the 0% rate applies up to $49,450 of taxable income for single filers and $98,900 for married couples filing jointly. Keep turnover low, hold broad index funds, and use losses wisely (tax-loss harvesting, asset location).

### Automate it
Set a recurring transfer and investment for the day after payday. Raise it by 1% of pay with every raise. Automation removes the two things that ruin returns: forgetting and reacting to headlines.

### A windfall: all at once or spread out?
Because markets rise more often than they fall, investing a lump sum right away usually ends ahead of feeding it in over months. Spreading it out (dollar-cost averaging) wins when prices drop early, and it can make a big first step easier to take. If spreading it out is what gets you invested, it is a fine choice; just set the schedule in advance and stick to it.

## Worked example: A $15,000 bonus
You have three months of costs saved, no card debt and the full 401(k) match. A $15,000 bonus lands after tax.
1. You split it by date. $5,000 is for a car you will replace next year, so it goes into a high-yield savings account at about 4.2%: roughly $210 of interest in a year, before tax.
2. $10,000 is for the long run. You buy VT in a taxable account at J.P. Morgan Self-Directed for $0 commission. Its 0.06% fee costs about $6 a year.
3. You set a $300 recurring purchase each month after payday, free of charge.
4. At 7% a year, $10,000 plus $300 a month for 25 years grows to about $300,300. You put in $100,000.
5. Each year the fund pays small dividends. For most people they are qualified dividends taxed at 0% or 15%, so you keep holding and avoid selling.
Result: Short-term money stayed safe, long-term money went to work immediately, and the habit runs itself.

## Mistakes
- Putting money you need in a year or two into stocks.
- Leaving a large cash pile in checking. The average savings account paid about 0.6% in October 2026 while good ones paid about 4%.
- Waiting months or years for the “right time” to invest a windfall.
- Paying for a managed account with high fees when one low-cost index fund would do the job.
## Takeaways
- Order: emergency fund, high-interest debt, 401(k) match, IRA or HSA, then taxable investing.
- Short-term money in cash or T-bills; long-term money in low-cost stock funds.
- VTWAX at Vanguard or VT at almost any broker gives you the world in one fund.
- Automate the transfer and the purchase.
- A lump sum usually beats spreading out, but any plan you stick to beats waiting.

========== l-kids-money | Investing for Decades #5 | Kids: 529s, Trump accounts and custodial Roths (widget kidsMoney)
Summary: Four ways to invest for a child, and which fits college, freedom or retirement.
Idea: Pick the account by the goal: a 529 for school, a Roth for a working teen, your own account for flexibility.

### Your retirement comes first
Students can borrow for college; nobody lends you money to retire. Get your own emergency fund, 401(k) match and retirement saving on track, then add accounts for your children.

### 529 plans

- Growth is tax-free when spent on qualified education: college tuition, books, room and board.
- From 2026, up to $20,000 a year can go to K-12 tuition (up from $10,000), and more K-12 costs count: curriculum, books, online courses, tutoring and test fees. Post-high-school credential and licensing costs count too.
- Spend it on anything else and the growth is taxed as income plus a 10% penalty.
- A leftover 529 can move up to $35,000 over a lifetime into the child’s Roth IRA if the 529 has been open 15 years or more, within the yearly Roth limits.
- A parent-owned 529 counts lightly on the federal aid form (FAFSA). Many states also give a tax deduction for deposits.
### Trump accounts

- Created by the 2025 tax law. Any child under 18 with a Social Security number can have one, elected on IRS Form 4547.
- US-citizen children born from January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2028 get a one-time $1,000 deposit from the government. By March 2026 about 4 million children had been signed up.
- Contributions started July 4, 2026, up to $5,000 a year in total. Employers can add up to $2,500 a year without it counting as the parent’s income.
- The money is invested in US stock index funds and generally cannot come out before 18. After that, traditional IRA-style rules apply: growth is taxed as income when withdrawn.
### Custodial Roth IRA
A teen with earned income (a summer job, babysitting, real work in a family business) can have a Roth IRA that you manage. Contributions are capped at the lesser of what they earned that year and the $7,500 IRA limit for 2026. You can fund it as a gift. Contributions can come out any time; the growth compounds tax-free for 40+ years.

### UTMA and UGMA accounts
A custodial account is an irrevocable gift: the money belongs to the child, who takes control at 18 to 21 depending on your state, and can spend it on anything. Investment income above a small yearly amount is taxed at the parent’s rate (the kiddie tax), and the account counts heavily against college aid.

### Gifts from family
Each person can give each child up to $19,000 in 2026 without filing a gift tax return. Grandparents can put that toward a 529, a Trump account or a Roth (the Roth still needs the child’s earned income).

## Worked example: A newborn in 2026
A US-citizen baby with a Social Security number is born in March 2026. Assume steady returns.
1. The parents elect a Trump account on Form 4547 and receive the one-time $1,000. Left alone at 7% for 18 years, it grows to about $3,380.
2. Grandparents add $1,000 at the end of each year for 18 years. At 7% that adds about $34,000, for roughly $37,400 at 18.
3. The parents save $200 a month in a 529 at 6%: $43,200 put in, about $77,500 at 18, tax-free for college.
4. At 16 and 17 the teen earns $3,000 each summer, and the parents put $3,000 a year into a custodial Roth IRA.
5. Left alone to 60 at 7%, that $6,000 could grow to about $113,900, all tax-free.
Result: Each account did one job: the 529 for school, the Trump account and Roth for the long run.

## Mistakes
- Saving for college before your own emergency fund and retirement.
- Putting college money in a UTMA without realizing the child controls it at 18 to 21 and it hurts aid.
- Opening a custodial Roth without real earned income to back the contributions.
- Missing the one-time $1,000 Trump account deposit for a child born 2025 through 2028.
## Takeaways
- 529: best for education, now including more K-12 costs.
- Trump account: $1,000 seed for eligible kids, up to $5,000 a year, locked until 18.
- Custodial Roth: the strongest long-term account, but only with earned income.
- UTMA: flexible, but it becomes the child’s money and hurts aid.
- Your own retirement first, then your children’s accounts.
