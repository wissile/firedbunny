

========== l-tax-brackets | Smart Taxes #1 | How tax brackets really work (widget bracketViz)
Summary: Marginal vs effective rate, the 2026 brackets and why a raise never lowers your pay.
Idea: Income is taxed in slices. Each slice pays only its own rate, so your overall rate sits far below your top bracket.

### Income fills buckets, one after another
Picture a row of buckets. The first bucket is tax-free: it is your standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household in 2026). Once it is full, the next dollars pour into the 10% bucket, then 12%, then 22%, and so on. Each bucket only charges its own rate on the dollars inside it.

### The 2026 brackets
These apply to taxable income: income after pre-tax savings and deductions.

- Single: 10% to $12,400 · 12% to $50,400 · 22% to $105,700 · 24% to $201,775 · 32% to $256,225 · 35% to $640,600 · 37% above.
- Married filing jointly: 10% to $24,800 · 12% to $100,800 · 22% to $211,400 · 24% to $403,550 · 32% to $512,450 · 35% to $768,700 · 37% above.
- Head of household: 10% to $17,700 · 12% to $67,450 · 22% to $105,700 · 24% to $201,750 · 32% to $256,200 · 35% to $640,600 · 37% above.
### Marginal rate vs effective rate
Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar: the bucket you are filling now. Use it to judge decisions at the edge, such as a raise, a side job or a 401(k) contribution. Your effective rate is total tax divided by income: what you pay on average. Use it to understand your whole bill. A single person earning $85,000 is in the 22% bracket but pays about 10% overall.

### A raise never shrinks your pay
Moving into a higher bracket only taxes the new dollars at the higher rate; the dollars below stay where they were. Turning down a raise or overtime to "stay in a lower bracket" always costs you money. The real traps are phase-outs and cliffs: some benefits shrink as income rises (the senior deduction, many credits, and health insurance subsidies, see health insurance before 65), so a dollar near those lines can cost more than your bracket says.

### Pre-tax saving works at your top rate
Money you put into a traditional 401(k) or HSA comes off the top of your income, out of your highest bucket. In the 22% bracket, every $1,000 you save pre-tax cuts this year’s federal tax by $220. That is why the same contribution is worth more to a high earner, and why someone in the 10% or 12% bracket may prefer a Roth (see Traditional or Roth?).

### What the brackets leave out

- Payroll tax: 6.2% Social Security (up to a yearly wage cap) plus 1.45% Medicare on every paycheck.
- State and local income tax: from 0% in states such as Texas and Florida to over 10% at the top in California.
- Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends: their own 0%, 15% and 20% rates. In 2026 the 0% rate covers taxable income up to $49,450 single or $98,900 joint (see the 0% bracket and capital gains).
- Credits: subtracted from the tax itself, after the brackets (see deductions and credits).

## Worked example: A single earner at $85,000 in 2026
Federal income tax only, standard deduction, no credits.
1. Salary $85,000. She puts $5,000 into her 401(k), so her adjusted gross income is $80,000.
2. Subtract the $16,100 standard deduction: taxable income is $63,900.
3. The first $12,400 is taxed at 10% ($1,240). The next $38,000, up to $50,400, at 12% ($4,560).
4. The last $13,500, from $50,400 to $63,900, lands in the 22% bucket: $2,970.
5. Total: $1,240 + $4,560 + $2,970 = $8,770. That is 10.3% of her $85,000 salary, though her top rate is 22%.
6. Another $1,000 into the 401(k) would save $220. A $1,000 raise would add $220 of income tax and $76.50 of payroll tax, so she keeps $703.50.
Result: Top rate 22%, effective rate 10.3%: about one dollar in ten goes to federal income tax.

## Mistakes
- Turning down a raise or overtime to "stay in a lower bracket". Only the extra dollars pay the higher rate.
- Using your top rate to estimate your whole tax bill. It overstates it; the effective rate is the average.
- Using your effective rate to judge a 401(k) contribution. Pre-tax money comes out of your top bracket, so it saves at your marginal rate.
- Forgetting payroll and state taxes when you budget a raise, or ignoring phase-outs that make some income cost more than your bracket.
## Takeaways
- Each bracket taxes only the dollars inside it.
- Marginal rate: the rate on your next dollar. Effective rate: your average.
- The standard deduction is a tax-free first bucket: $16,100 single or $32,200 joint in 2026.
- Pre-tax 401(k) and HSA money comes out of your top bracket.
- A raise always leaves you with more money; watch for phase-outs and cliffs instead.
## Quiz
Q: A single filer has $60,000 of taxable income in 2026. Which dollars are taxed at 22%?
  All $60,000
* Only the $9,600 above $50,400
  None, because the effective rate is lower
Why: Only income above the $50,400 line falls into the 22% bracket; the dollars below pay 10% and 12%.
## Lesson glossary
- Payroll tax: Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) withheld from wages, separate from income tax.
- Phase-out: An income range over which a deduction or credit shrinks, so extra income there costs more than your bracket.

========== l-deductions | Smart Taxes #2 | Deductions and credits worth knowing (widget deductionFinder)
Summary: Standard vs itemized, the new 2025-2028 deductions and the credits that cut tax dollar for dollar.
Idea: A deduction shrinks the income you are taxed on. A credit shrinks the tax itself. Know which ones fit your life, and claim every one.

### Deduction or credit?
A deduction lowers taxable income, so it saves your marginal rate: a $1,000 deduction in the 22% bracket saves $220. A credit comes off the tax bill itself: a $1,000 credit saves $1,000. Some credits are refundable, so you get the money even if you owe no tax.

### The deductions you control: pre-tax accounts

- 401(k), 403(b), TSP: up to $24,500 in 2026, plus $8,000 at age 50+ ($11,250 at ages 60 to 63). See the limits most people miss.
- HSA: $4,400 self-only or $8,750 family coverage, plus $1,000 at 55+. Through payroll it also skips the 7.65% payroll tax. See the HSA.
- Traditional IRA: up to $7,500 ($8,600 at 50+). If you have a workplace plan, the deduction phases out between $81,000 and $91,000 of income single, or $129,000 to $149,000 joint.
### Standard deduction or itemize?
You take whichever is bigger. In 2026 the standard deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 joint and $24,150 head of household, plus $2,050 for a single filer 65+ or $1,650 per married spouse 65+. Itemized deductions are mainly:

- State and local taxes (SALT): capped at $40,400 in 2026. The cap shrinks by 30% of income above $505,000, never below $10,000.
- Mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of home debt. From 2026, mortgage insurance premiums count again.
- Charity above 0.5% of your AGI. In the top bracket, itemized deductions are worth 35 cents per dollar.
- Medical costs above 7.5% of your AGI.Most people take the standard deduction. If you are close, bunch: give two years of donations in one year, itemize that year and take the standard the next.

### New for 2025 to 2028: deductions you get without itemizing

- Seniors: an extra $6,000 per person 65+, shrinking by 6% of income above $75,000 ($150,000 joint).
- Tips: up to $25,000 of qualified tips in jobs that customarily receive them. The cap shrinks by $100 per $1,000 of income above $150,000 ($300,000 joint).
- Overtime: the extra "half" of time-and-a-half pay, up to $12,500 ($25,000 joint), same phase-out.
- Car loan interest: up to $10,000 a year on a loan taken after 2024 for a new car with final assembly in the US, for personal use. The cap shrinks by $200 per $1,000 of income above $100,000 ($200,000 joint). Put the VIN on your return. See cash or loan.
- Charity, from 2026: up to $1,000 of cash gifts ($2,000 joint) with the standard deduction. Gifts to donor-advised funds don’t count.
### Credits: worth more per dollar

- Child tax credit: $2,200 per child under 17 in 2026, up to $1,700 refundable. It shrinks by $50 per $1,000 of income above $200,000 ($400,000 joint).
- Child and dependent care credit: 20% to 50% of up to $3,000 of care for one child or $6,000 for two or more; the top rate rose to 50% in 2026 and falls as income rises. A dependent care FSA at work is the alternative.
- Education: the American opportunity credit pays up to $2,500 per student for the first four years of college; the lifetime learning credit up to $2,000 per return. Both phase out between $80,000 and $90,000 ($160,000 to $180,000 joint).
- Saver’s credit: 10% to 50% of up to $2,000 you save for retirement, if your 2026 income is at or under $40,250 single, $60,375 head of household or $80,500 joint.
- Earned income credit: up to $8,231 in 2026 for working families with three or more children, less for smaller families.
### Side business and kids
With self-employment income you can deduct business costs, half of self-employment tax and health insurance premiums, take the qualified business income deduction (up to 20% of profit, with limits at higher incomes) and save far more in a solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA. A 529 plan has no federal deduction, but many states give one, and the new Trump accounts give US-citizen children born from 2025 through 2028 a one-time $1,000 federal deposit (see kids’ money). Once your return has a business, rentals or stock options, a CPA or enrolled agent is usually worth the fee.

## Worked example: A family decides whether to itemize
Married filing jointly in 2026, two salaries totalling $180,000, one child aged 6.
1. They put $20,000 into their 401(k)s, so AGI is $160,000. At 22%, that alone saves $4,400 of tax.
2. Itemized: $14,000 of state and local taxes (under the $40,400 cap) + $9,500 of mortgage interest + charity of $3,000 minus 0.5% of AGI ($800) = $25,700.
3. The $32,200 standard deduction beats $25,700 by $6,500, so they take the standard deduction.
4. Without itemizing they can still deduct $2,000 of their cash gifts. Taxable income: $160,000 − $32,200 − $2,000 = $125,800.
5. Tax: 10% of $24,800 ($2,480) + 12% of $76,000 ($9,120) + 22% of $25,000 ($5,500) = $17,100.
6. The $2,200 child tax credit comes straight off the bill: $14,900.
Result: Federal income tax of $14,900 on $180,000 of pay, about 8.3%.

## Mistakes
- Itemizing out of habit. With today’s higher standard deduction most households are better off with the standard.
- Missing the new 2025-2028 deductions (senior, tips, overtime, car loan interest) or the 2026 charity deduction because you don’t itemize. They don’t require itemizing.
- Treating a deduction like a credit. A $1,000 deduction at 22% saves $220, not $1,000.
- Giving through a donor-advised fund and expecting the non-itemizer charity deduction, or paying for child care with both FSA dollars and the credit.
## Takeaways
- Credits beat deductions dollar for dollar.
- Take the bigger of the standard deduction or your itemized total; bunch if you are close.
- Pre-tax 401(k), HSA and IRA money is the deduction you control most.
- The senior, tips, overtime and car-loan deductions run 2025 to 2028 and work without itemizing.
- Check every credit that fits: children, child care, school and the saver’s credit.
## Quiz
Q: Which saves more for someone in the 22% bracket?
  A $1,000 deduction
* A $1,000 credit
  They save the same
Why: A credit cuts the tax bill by $1,000; a deduction cuts taxable income by $1,000, which saves $220 at 22%.
## Lesson glossary
- Bunching: Grouping two or more years of deductible spending, such as charity, into one year so you can itemize that year and take the standard deduction the next.
- Refundable credit: A credit that is paid to you even if it is bigger than the tax you owe.

========== l-asset-location | Smart Taxes #3 | Asset location: the right account for each fund (widget assetLocation)
Summary: Hold tax-heavy funds in sheltered accounts and tax-light ones in taxable to keep more.
Idea: Asset allocation decides what you own. Asset location decides which account holds each piece, and it can add return at no extra risk.

### Three kinds of accounts

- Taxable brokerage: interest and dividends are taxed every year, and gains when you sell.
- Tax-deferred (traditional 401(k), traditional IRA): no tax along the way; every withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income.
- Roth (Roth IRA, Roth 401(k)): no tax along the way and none on qualified withdrawals.The same fund can cost very different amounts of tax depending on which account holds it.

### How each investment is taxed in a taxable account

- Bonds and bond funds: interest is taxed at your ordinary rate every year, the worst treatment there is.
- REITs: most of their dividends are taxed at ordinary rates (with a 20% deduction), and they pay a lot of them.
- Broad stock index funds: low dividends, mostly qualified (0%, 15% or 20%), and very few gains passed out to you. Highly tax-efficient.
- International index funds: qualified dividends plus foreign taxes withheld, which you can claim back as a foreign tax credit, but only in a taxable account.
- Municipal bonds: interest is federal tax-free, so they belong in taxable if anywhere.
### The placement order

- Tax-deferred accounts first get the least tax-efficient assets: taxable bonds, REITs, high-yield funds.
- The Roth gets your highest expected growth, usually stocks: every dollar of growth there is tax-free forever, and Roth IRAs have no required withdrawals.
- Taxable gets the tax-efficient assets: total-market or S&P 500 index funds, international index funds (to use the foreign tax credit) and municipal bonds if you need bonds there.Tax-loss harvesting only works in taxable accounts, another reason to hold broad stock funds there (see fund pairs).

### Count after-tax dollars, not balances
$100,000 in a traditional 401(k) is not $100,000 to spend: at a 22% withdrawal rate it is worth $78,000. $100,000 in a Roth is worth $100,000. If you put all your bonds in the traditional account, your true after-tax mix holds a little more stock than the statements suggest. Keep that in mind when you set your allocation.

### When not to bother

- Almost everything is in one type of account. There is nothing to move.
- Location would push you into a worse or more expensive fund because your 401(k) menu is poor. Low fees come first.
- You would need to sell taxable holdings at big gains to rearrange. Steer new contributions and dividends instead.Typical gains run a few tenths of a percent a year: free money that compounds, but never worth breaking your overall mix.

## Worked example: $300,000 across three accounts
$100,000 each in taxable, a traditional 401(k) and a Roth IRA. Target: 30% bonds ($90,000), 70% stock index funds ($210,000). 24% bracket, 15% on qualified dividends.
1. Same mix everywhere: each account holds $30,000 of bonds and $70,000 of stock funds.
2. In taxable, the $30,000 of bonds paying 4.5% throws off $1,350 of interest, taxed at 24%: $324. The $70,000 of stocks yields 1.5%, $1,050 taxed at 15%: $157.50. Year-one tax: $481.50.
3. Smart location: all $90,000 of bonds plus $10,000 of stocks go in the traditional 401(k). The Roth holds $100,000 of stocks. Taxable holds $100,000 of a total-market stock index fund.
4. Taxable now yields $1,500 of qualified dividends, taxed at 15%: $225 a year.
5. Year-one tax drops by $256.50 with the same 30/70 mix and the same risk. The difference stays invested and compounds every year.
Result: Same investments, different addresses: $256.50 less tax in year one, and the gap widens every year.

## Mistakes
- Holding bonds or REITs in a taxable account while stock funds sit in the 401(k).
- Comparing balances without adjusting for tax: pre-tax money is worth less to spend than Roth or taxable money.
- Letting location drive a worse overall mix, or picking a high-fee fund just because it fits an account.
- Selling taxable holdings at large gains to rearrange, when new contributions could do it for free.
## Takeaways
- Allocation first, location second.
- Fill tax-deferred space with tax-inefficient assets such as bonds and REITs.
- Give the Roth your highest expected growth.
- Keep broad stock index funds and international funds in taxable.
- The gain is often a few tenths of a percent a year: worth it, but never worth bad funds or fees.
## Quiz
Q: Which holding usually belongs in a traditional 401(k) rather than a taxable account?
  A total US stock index fund
* A taxable bond fund
  An international stock index fund
Why: Bond interest is taxed at ordinary rates every year in a taxable account; a tax-deferred account shelters it.
## Lesson glossary
- REIT: A company that owns property and must pay out most of its income as dividends, which are mostly taxed at ordinary rates.
- Municipal bond: A bond issued by a state or city. Its interest is usually free of federal income tax.

========== l-tlh-pairs | Smart Taxes #4 | Tax-loss harvesting with fund pairs (widget tlhPairs)
Summary: Sell a fund at a loss, buy a similar one and keep both the tax break and your plan.
Idea: Swap a losing fund for a similar fund on a different index. You book the loss without leaving the market.

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that is worth less than you paid, so the loss lowers your tax bill. The trick with index funds is that you do not have to sit in cash: you buy a similar fund the same day and stay invested. Done well, you keep your plan and get a tax break. This builds on the trading lesson on harvesting and the wash-sale rule.

### How the swap works

- Sell the fund that is down, say VGT, and lock in the loss.
- Buy a similar fund the same day, say XLK. You stay in US tech.
- Wait at least 31 days before buying the original fund again, in any account.
- After that, stay in the new fund or switch back. Either is fine.
### What “substantially identical” means for funds
The wash-sale rule blocks a loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale. The IRS has never said exactly when two funds are substantially identical. Practitioners use a simple test: funds that track the same index are the risky swap (VOO, IVV and SPYM all track the S&P 500), while funds on different indexes are common practice (VOO to VTI, VTI to ITOT, VGT to XLK).
VGT and XLK are a classic pair: both hold US technology companies, but VGT follows an MSCI index with hundreds of holdings and XLK follows the S&P Technology Select Sector index with fewer, larger ones. FTEC, by contrast, tracks effectively the same MSCI tech index as VGT, so the two are nearly identical: avoid that pair.
A note on tickers: we could not find any US fund with the ticker “SPDL”. It is most likely SPLG, the low-cost SPDR S&P 500 fund, which State Street renamed SPYM on October 31, 2025. Same fund, same S&P 500 index, so it is a risky partner for VOO or IVV.

### Traps that break the loss

- Every account counts. A purchase in your IRA or Roth IRA within the window triggers a wash sale, and the IRS has ruled that loss is lost for good, because it cannot be added to an IRA’s basis (Rev. Rul. 2008-5). That ruling does not address 401(k) purchases, so treat those as risky too.
- Your spouse counts. If your spouse buys the same fund within the window, it is a wash sale too.
- Dividend reinvestment and automatic investing quietly buy the old fund. Turn them off in every account before you sell.
- Buying a call option on the same fund counts as buying it. Reinvested dividends wash only the few shares they buy, but it still makes a mess of your records.
- Day 30 is still inside. The window runs 30 days before and 30 days after the sale: 61 days in all.
### What the loss is worth
Losses first cancel capital gains. If losses are left over, up to $3,000 a year ($1,500 if married filing separately) comes off your ordinary income, and the rest carries forward with no expiry. Harvesting usually defers tax rather than erasing it: your new fund has a lower cost basis, so you owe more when you finally sell. The deferral is still valuable, and the tax can disappear if you give the shares to charity or hold them until death, when the basis steps up.

### Harvest gains too
If your taxable income is low enough to sit in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket (for 2026, taxable income up to $49,450 single or $98,900 married filing jointly), you can do the reverse: sell winners, pay 0% tax, and buy them right back. The wash-sale rule only applies to losses, so there is no waiting. Your cost basis resets higher, which shrinks future taxes. Do not waste losses offsetting gains that would have been taxed at 0% anyway, and watch side effects of a higher income, such as health insurance subsidies. Early retirees use this a lot; see the 0% bracket lesson.

### A harvesting checklist

- Is the loss big enough to be worth the paperwork? Small losses rarely are.
- Pick a partner on a different index, with a similar risk level and low cost.
- Turn off dividend reinvestment and automatic buys of the old fund everywhere, including IRAs and your spouse’s accounts.
- Sell and buy on the same day. Write the date down; day 31 is the earliest safe buy-back.
- Check your year-end tax forms. Brokers only track wash sales inside one account, not across accounts or households.

## Worked example: Swapping VGT for XLK in a down year
Made-up numbers. You are in the 24% bracket and already took $2,500 of short-term gains this year.
1. You bought $20,000 of VGT in March. By November it is worth $16,000: a $4,000 loss on paper.
2. You turn off dividend reinvestment on VGT in your brokerage account and stop the automatic VGT purchase in your IRA.
3. You sell all the VGT and, the same day, buy $16,000 of XLK. You still own US tech; the loss is now booked.
4. At tax time the $4,000 loss cancels your $2,500 of short-term gains, and the remaining $1,500 comes off your salary income: 4,000 × 24% = $960 less tax.
5. Your XLK cost basis is $16,000. You can switch back to VGT from day 31, or stay in XLK. Either way, more gain is taxed when you finally sell, unless you hold for life or donate the shares.
Result: Same market exposure, $960 less federal tax this year, and no wash sale.

## Mistakes
- Swapping between two funds on the same index, such as VOO and IVV. It is the least defensible pair if the IRS ever asks.
- Leaving dividend reinvestment or an automatic IRA purchase on. A small buy inside the window blocks part of the loss, and in an IRA that part is gone for good.
- Buying the original fund back on day 29 or 30. The window runs a full 30 days after the sale.
- Thinking harvesting erases tax. It mostly defers it, because your new shares have a lower basis.
## Takeaways
- Sell the loser, buy a similar fund on a different index the same day, and stay invested.
- Same-index swaps are the gray zone; different-index swaps are common practice.
- Wash sales count across all your accounts, your IRA and your spouse’s purchases.
- Losses cancel gains, then up to $3,000 of income a year, and the rest carries forward.
- In the 0% bracket, harvest gains instead: sell, buy back at once, raise your basis tax-free.
## Quiz
Q: You sell VOO at a loss. Which purchase that same day is the most common, safest swap?
  IVV, which also tracks the S&P 500
* VTI, which tracks the whole US market on a different index
  VOO in your Roth IRA
Why: VTI follows a different index, so it is not substantially identical. IVV tracks the same index, and buying VOO in an IRA would wipe out the loss for good.

========== l-wheel-tax | Smart Taxes #5 | How the wheel is taxed (widget wheelTaxSim)
Summary: Puts, assignments, calls and rolls each follow their own tax rule. Here is every one.
Idea: Option premium is taxed when the option ends, not when you collect it. Assignment folds it into the share price.

The wheel looks simple: sell puts, get assigned, sell calls, get called away. The tax side is not. Every option can end three ways, assignment changes your cost basis, and covered calls can even change how long the IRS thinks you have owned your shares. Here are the rules for a regular taxable account, from IRS Publication 550.

### Rule 1: premium is taxed when the option ends
When you sell an option, the premium is not income yet. It waits until the option is closed. Then:

- It expires worthless: the premium is a short-term capital gain on the expiration date, even if the option was open for months.
- You buy it back: the difference is a short-term gain or loss.
- You are assigned: the premium is folded into the stock trade (rules 2 and 3).So a put you sell in December that expires in January is taxed in January’s tax year, even though the cash arrived in December.

### Rule 2: an assigned put lowers your cost basis
If your put is assigned, you buy 100 shares at the strike. The premium is not taxed separately; it reduces the shares’ cost. Sell a $48 put for $1.20 and get assigned: your basis is $46.80 a share. The holding period of the shares starts the day after assignment.

### Rule 3: covered calls follow the shares

- Call expires: short-term gain on the premium.
- You buy it back: short-term gain or loss.
- You are called away: the premium is added to the sale price of the shares. Whether that gain is short- or long-term depends on how long you held the shares, not the call.A roll is simply two trades: the buy-back is taxed now, the new option later.

### Rule 4: some covered calls touch your holding period
A qualified covered call is traded on an exchange, has more than 30 days to expiration and is not too deep in the money (roughly, no lower than the first strike below the share price; the exact limits depend on price and time). Then:

- Out of the money and qualified: nothing changes. The holding-period clock keeps running.
- In the money but qualified: for shares held a year or less, the days the call is open do not count toward long-term.
- Not qualified (deep in the money or 30 days or less): the shares and the call form a tax straddle. For shares held a year or less, the holding period is wiped out and starts again when the straddle ends, and losses on one side can be deferred while you hold the other.If the shares were already long-term when you wrote an unqualified call, they keep their long-term status, but a loss from buying the call back becomes a long-term loss, and it is deferred while the shares still have an unrealized gain at year end. This is how a wheel trader can hold shares for 13 months and still pay short-term tax on the gain, or find a loss they cannot use yet.

### Rule 5: wash sales apply to options too

- Sell a stock at a loss and then get assigned on a put on the same stock within 30 days: you acquired the shares, so treat it as a wash sale.
- Buying a call on a stock you just sold at a loss also counts.
- Selling a put soon after a loss sale can count too if it is likely to be exercised, such as a deep in-the-money put (Rev. Rul. 85-87). Out-of-the-money puts are the safer choice.
- Brokers often miss these when the trades are in different accounts. You are still responsible.
### Rule 6: index options are different (Section 1256)
Broad-based index options such as SPX and XSP are generally Section 1256 contracts. Every gain or loss is taxed 60% long-term and 40% short-term, however long you held it, and open positions are marked to market at year end (Form 6781). They are cash-settled, so there are no shares to wheel. Options on ETFs such as SPY, QQQ and IWM are treated by brokers and most tax pros as ordinary equity options, not Section 1256 (the IRS has not ruled explicitly); they follow rules 1 to 5. Most states tax all of it as ordinary income anyway.

### Why the wheel is hard to do in a taxable account

- Almost everything is short-term, taxed at your full income tax rate plus state tax.
- Dozens of small trades a year, each with its own date, basis adjustment and term.
- Year-end timing splits trades across two tax years.
- Your broker’s 1099-B may net premiums into basis or proceeds in ways that look odd; check it against your own records.That is why many people run the wheel inside a Roth IRA instead: see the next lesson. If you trade a lot in a taxable account, a CPA or enrolled agent who knows options is worth the fee.

## Worked example: One wheel across New Year
Made-up stock DEMO, one contract, taxable account.
1. Nov 20, 2026: sell a $50 put for $1.50 ($150). It expires Dec 18: a $150 short-term gain for 2026.
2. Dec 21: sell a $48 put expiring Jan 15, 2027 for $1.20 ($120). The cash arrives in 2026, but nothing is taxed for 2026.
3. Jan 15, 2027: assigned at $48. Your basis is $48 − $1.20 = $46.80 a share, or $4,680. The $120 is never taxed on its own.
4. Sell a $50 call for $1.00 ($100), buy it back for $0.30 ($30): a $70 short-term gain in 2027. Then sell a new $50 call for $0.90 ($90).
5. Apr 16, 2027: called away at $50. Proceeds $5,000 + $90 = $5,090, minus $4,680 basis = a $410 short-term gain.
Result: You netted $630: $150 taxed in 2026 and $480 in 2027, all short-term at ordinary rates.

## Mistakes
- Counting premium as income in the year the cash arrived. It is taxed when the option ends, which can be the next year.
- Paying tax on an assigned put’s premium and also using the full strike as your basis. The premium belongs in the basis, once.
- Selling deep in-the-money calls on shares held under a year, which resets the holding period and turns a long-term gain short-term.
- Selling shares at a loss and getting assigned on a put on the same stock within 30 days. That is a wash sale.
## Takeaways
- Sold options are taxed when they expire, are bought back or are assigned, not when you collect the premium.
- An assigned put’s premium lowers your cost basis; a called-away call’s premium raises your sale price.
- Gains on the shares follow the shares’ holding period; option-only gains are short-term.
- In-the-money and deep in-the-money covered calls can suspend or reset that holding period.
- SPX and XSP options get 60/40 treatment; SPY and QQQ options do not.
## Quiz
Q: You sell a put in December 2026 for $200 and it expires worthless in January 2027. When is the $200 taxed?
  2026, when the cash arrived
* 2027, when the option expired
  Never, because it expired
Why: Premium from a sold option is taxed when the option ends. It expired in January 2027, so it is a 2027 short-term gain.
## Lesson glossary
- Tax straddle: Holding positions that offset each other’s risk, such as shares plus a deep in-the-money call you sold. Losses can be deferred and holding periods reset.
- Mark to market: Treating an open position as sold at its year-end value for tax purposes, as with Section 1256 contracts.

========== l-wheel-ira | Smart Taxes #6 | Run the wheel in an IRA (widget wheelAccounts)
Summary: Same trades, no yearly tax bill. When an IRA beats a taxable account, and its traps.
Idea: Wheel income is mostly short-term and taxed like salary. Inside a Roth IRA it compounds without any yearly tax bill.

As the last lesson showed, nearly all wheel income is short-term: taxed at your full income tax rate, plus state tax, every single year. Tax paid each year is money that stops compounding. Inside an IRA there is no tax on each trade at all.

### What you can do in an IRA

- Most brokers allow cash-secured puts and covered calls in IRAs, usually at their lowest options approval levels. The broker lesson covers how to compare them.
- No margin, so every put must be fully backed by cash in the account.
- No naked calls, and usually no complex spreads at the basic level.
### Roth or traditional?

- Roth IRA: no tax on trades and no tax on qualified withdrawals after age 59½ once the account is five years old. The best home for high-turnover, high-income strategies.
- Traditional IRA: no tax on trades, but every dollar you withdraw is taxed as ordinary income, and required withdrawals start in your 70s.
- Taxable account: taxed every year, but losses are deductible and money is available any time.
### The trade-offs

- Contribution limits. The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500 ($8,600 if you are 50 or older). One cash-secured put with a $75 strike ties up the whole $7,500, so a new IRA can only wheel one cheap stock. Bigger balances usually come from a rollover of an old 401(k).
- Losses do not help your taxes. A bad year inside an IRA is just a smaller balance. In a taxable account the same loss offsets other gains and up to $3,000 of income.
- Access. Withdrawals before 59½ generally face a 10% penalty plus tax (Roth contributions, not earnings, can come out any time).
### The IRA wash-sale trap
Sell a stock at a loss in your taxable account, then buy it or get assigned on a put on it in your IRA within 30 days. That is a wash sale, and the IRS has ruled the loss cannot be added to the IRA shares’ basis (Rev. Rul. 2008-5). The loss is gone for good. Keep a simple rule: never wheel a stock in your IRA that you have just sold at a loss elsewhere, and never harvest a loss on a stock your IRA might buy within 30 days.

### A sensible split
Many people run the wheel in a Roth IRA and keep broad index funds in the taxable account, where most of the return is long-term and qualified dividends are taxed at lower rates. That is called asset location. Whatever you do, keep a trade log; you will still want to know your real return.

## Worked example: $50,000 wheel: taxable vs Roth IRA
Made-up but plausible: 8% a year net of losses, 24% federal plus 5% state on short-term gains.
1. In the taxable account each year’s 8% gain is short-term, so 29% goes to tax. You keep 8% × 71% = 5.68% a year.
2. After 15 years the taxable account grows to $50,000 × 1.0568^15 ≈ $114,500.
3. In the Roth IRA the full 8% compounds: $50,000 × 1.08^15 ≈ $158,600.
4. The difference, about $44,100, is the tax paid along the way plus the growth that tax money never earned.
5. Catch: $50,000 is far more than one year’s IRA limit, so this works only with years of contributions or a rollover.
Result: The Roth ends about 39% bigger than the taxable account with the same trades.

## Mistakes
- Getting assigned in an IRA on a stock you just sold at a loss in your taxable account. That loss is lost for good.
- Wheeling a high-priced stock with a small IRA, which ties the whole account to one or two positions.
- Expecting to deduct a bad year in an IRA. Losses inside it never reach your tax return.
- Withdrawing early to fund trades elsewhere and paying a 10% penalty plus tax.
## Takeaways
- Wheel income is mostly short-term, so a taxable account loses a slice of every year’s gain to tax.
- In a Roth IRA the trades and qualified withdrawals are tax-free; a traditional IRA taxes withdrawals instead.
- IRAs allow cash-secured puts and covered calls, but no margin, and losses do not count for tax.
- Never let your IRA buy a stock you just sold at a loss elsewhere: the loss disappears.
## Quiz
Q: You sell DEMO at a $2,000 loss in your brokerage account. Ten days later a put on DEMO is assigned in your Roth IRA. What happens to the loss?
  You claim it as usual
  It is added to the IRA shares’ basis
* It is disallowed and lost for good
Why: Buying the same stock in an IRA within 30 days is a wash sale, and the IRS has ruled the loss cannot be added to IRA basis, so it is permanently lost.

========== l-rent-vs-buy | Home & Real Estate #1 | Rent or buy? (widget rentVsBuy)
Summary: When owning beats renting, and how rates, prices and time change the answer.
Idea: Renting is not throwing money away. Compare the money neither path gets back, and how long you will stay.

### Both paths have costs you never get back
Rent is gone once you pay it. So are big parts of owning: mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, upkeep, and the return your down payment could have earned invested. Only the loan principal you pay down and any rise in the home’s price build wealth. A fair comparison puts these unrecoverable costs side by side, which is what the calculator does month by month.

### The costs of owning people forget

- Buying costs: closing costs typically run 2%–5% of the price.
- Selling costs: agent commissions have stayed around 5%–6% of the price even after the 2024 NAR settlement (buyer-agent fees averaged about 2.4% in 2025), plus transfer taxes and other fees.
- Property tax: often around 1% of the value a year, and much more or less depending on your state.
- Maintenance: plan on about 1% of the value a year.
- Insurance and HOA dues: insurance has been rising fast in states like Florida and California.
- PMI if you put less than 20% down (see Mortgage basics).
### Two quick tests
Price-to-rent ratio: the home’s price divided by a year of rent for a similar place. Under about 15 tends to favor buying; over about 20 favors renting. At the national numbers for September 2026 (median existing-home price $417,500, typical rent about $1,932) it is roughly 18: a close call that depends on your town.
The 5% rule: owning costs roughly 5% of the home’s value a year in unrecoverable costs: about 1% property tax, 1% maintenance and 3% cost of money. Multiply the price by 5% and divide by 12. If you can rent a similar home for less, renting is competitive. For a $420,000 home that is $1,750 a month. The 3% was set when mortgages were cheaper, so with rates above 7% treat the rule as a floor.

### Interest rates change the answer
In today’s range, each 1-point rise in the rate adds about $65 a month for every $100,000 you borrow. On October 15, 2026 the average 30-year rate was 7.40% (Freddie Mac). A $336,000 loan costs $2,326 a month at that rate and $1,804 at 5%. In the calculator, the typical home never catches renting within 30 years at 7.4%, but pulls ahead after about 6 years at 5%. High rates push the answer toward renting and investing; falling rates pull it toward buying. Refinancing later is possible, but no one can promise rates will fall.

### When buying tends to win

- You expect to stay at least 5–7 years, long enough to earn back roughly 10% in buying and selling costs.
- Your job and income are steady and you still have an emergency fund after closing.
- Local price-to-rent is reasonable and the payment fits easily in your budget.
- You value stability: no landlord, no forced moves, freedom to renovate. That is a fair reason; just know its price.
### When renting tends to win

- You might move within a few years, or your job is uncertain.
- Prices are high compared with rents (a ratio above about 20).
- You would rather keep money liquid and invested. Renting only wins if you actually invest the difference; spent, it is just a higher lifestyle.Next: what a mortgage really costs in Mortgage basics, and whether a home is a good investment in Is a house an investment?

## Worked example: A $420,000 home at 7.4%

1. You can rent a home for $2,100 a month, or buy a similar one for $420,000 with 20% down ($84,000) plus about $12,600 (3%) in closing costs.
2. A 30-year loan of $336,000 at 7.4% costs $2,326 a month. In year 1, $24,758 of the $27,917 you pay is interest; only $3,158 builds equity.
3. Add property tax ($350 a month at 1%), insurance ($175) and upkeep ($350). Owning takes about $3,201 a month in cash, $1,101 more than rent.
4. Quick tests: price-to-rent is $420,000 ÷ $25,200 = 16.7, and the 5% rule gives $1,750 a month. Both say “close call”, leaning slightly toward buying.
5. The full model has the renter invest the $96,600 up front and the monthly gap at 6% a year, with the home growing 3.5% a year and 7% selling costs. Selling in year 10, renting is about $49,000 ahead, and buying never catches up within 30 years at 7.4%. At a 5% rate, buying pulls ahead after year 6.
Result: At October 2026 rates this home favors renting, unless prices grow faster, rents jump or rates fall. Run your own numbers above.

## Mistakes
- Comparing rent with the mortgage payment alone, and forgetting property tax, insurance, upkeep, HOA dues and PMI.
- Saying “rent is throwing money away” while ignoring that interest, taxes and upkeep are also gone for good.
- Buying for a two- or three-year stay, so about 10% in buying and selling costs wipes out any price gain.
- Renting to “invest the difference” and then spending it. The renting path only wins if the money is invested.
## Takeaways
- Compare unrecoverable costs: interest, tax, insurance, upkeep and lost returns versus rent.
- Price-to-rent under about 15 leans buy, over about 20 leans rent; the 5% rule is a fast second check.
- High mortgage rates push the answer toward renting; lower rates pull it toward buying.
- The longer you stay, the more the buying and selling costs are spread out. Under about 5 years, renting usually wins.
- Stability, schools and control are valid reasons to buy. Just know what they cost.
## Quiz
Q: You can rent a home for $1,500 a month. Using the 5% rule, what purchase price is roughly equivalent?
  $180,000
* $360,000
  $600,000
Why: $1,500 × 12 ÷ 5% = $360,000. If similar homes cost more than that, renting tends to be the better deal.

========== l-mortgage | Home & Real Estate #2 | Mortgage basics (widget mortgageCalc)
Summary: Payments, amortization, PMI, points, 15 vs 30 years and when to refinance.
Idea: A mortgage payment is mostly interest at first. The rate, the term and every extra dollar change what your home really costs.

### What your payment pays for
Lenders talk about PITI: principal (paying down the loan), interest, property taxes and home insurance. Taxes and insurance are often collected with the payment into an escrow account and paid for you. Add PMI if you put less than 20% down, and HOA dues if the home has them.

### Amortization: why the first years feel slow
Every payment is the same size, but interest is charged on what you still owe. On a $336,000 loan at 7.4%, about 89% of the first year’s payments is interest. The split slowly flips, and in the last years almost all of each payment is principal. That is why extra payments early in the loan save the most.

### Fixed or adjustable
A fixed-rate loan keeps the same rate for its whole life. An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM), such as a 7/6 ARM, is fixed for the first years and then resets with a market index plus a margin, within caps. An ARM usually starts cheaper but moves the risk of rising rates onto you.

### 15 or 30 years
On October 15, 2026 the average 15-year rate was 6.71% and the 30-year 7.40% (Freddie Mac). On $336,000 that is $2,966 a month for 15 years versus $2,326 for 30, and about $198,000 versus $502,000 of total interest. The 30 gives breathing room; the 15 forces saving. Taking the 30 and investing the difference only wins if you really invest it and earn more than the loan costs.

### PMI, points and extra payments

- PMI: with under 20% down on a conventional loan you pay private mortgage insurance, priced on your credit and down payment. You can ask to cancel it once the balance reaches 80% of the original value, and it ends automatically at 78% if you are current.
- FHA loans charge their own insurance (MIP): 1.75% of the loan up front plus a yearly premium that lasts for the life of the loan if you put less than 10% down.
- Points: one point costs 1% of the loan and buys a lower rate; how much lower depends on the lender. Divide the cost by the monthly saving to get the break-even, and only pay points if you will keep the loan longer than that.
- Extra payments: every extra dollar goes straight to principal. $200 more a month on the $336,000 loan saves about $132,500 of interest and ends it 6 years 8 months early.
### How much can you borrow?
Lenders look at your debt-to-income ratio (DTI): monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. Fannie Mae’s automated underwriting can approve up to 50%, but a payment that big leaves nothing for saving or surprises. A long-used comfort guideline keeps housing near 28% of gross income and all debts near 36%. Get Loan Estimates from at least three lenders; they are standardized so you can compare rates and fees line by line.

### Refinancing
When rates fall, a new loan can cut your payment. Divide the closing costs by the monthly saving: if you will keep the home longer than that, it can pay. Watch the term: restarting a 30-year clock can raise your total interest even when the payment drops. Whether a home is worth buying at all is in Rent or buy?

## Worked example: Payoff choices on a $336,000 loan

1. You buy a $420,000 home with 20% down and borrow $336,000 at 7.4% for 30 years: $2,326 a month for principal and interest.
2. Over 30 years you would pay about $501,500 of interest, more than the amount you borrowed.
3. A 15-year loan at 6.71% costs $2,966 a month, $639 more, but total interest drops to about $197,900.
4. Keeping the 30-year loan and paying $200 extra every month ends it in 23 years 4 months and saves about $132,500 of interest.
5. Later, rates fall. Refinancing a $380,000 balance from 7.8% to a 30-year loan at 6.5% cuts the payment by $413 a month; $7,600 of closing costs (2%) pays back in about 18 months.
Result: Rate, term and extra payments can each move what the home really costs by six figures.

## Mistakes
- Choosing the house first and the loan as an afterthought. Compare Loan Estimates from several lenders; a small rate gap costs thousands.
- Paying points on a loan you will sell or refinance before the points break even.
- Borrowing the most a lender approves, leaving nothing for savings, repairs or a job loss.
- Refinancing into a fresh 30-year loan for a lower payment without noticing the total interest goes up.
## Takeaways
- Early payments are mostly interest, so extra principal early saves the most.
- A 15-year loan has a higher payment and a lower rate, and costs far less interest.
- PMI applies under 20% down; you can drop it at 80% of the original value and it ends at 78%.
- Points and refinancing are break-even math: up-front cost ÷ monthly saving.
## Quiz
Q: A refinance costs $6,000 and lowers your payment by $250 a month. When does it break even?
  After 12 months
* After 24 months
  After 60 months
Why: $6,000 ÷ $250 = 24 months. If you expect to move or refinance again before then, it does not pay.

========== l-home-taxes | Home & Real Estate #3 | Your home and taxes (widget homeSaleTax)
Summary: The $250,000/$500,000 home-sale exclusion, cost basis and the deductions most owners miss out on.
Idea: Up to $250,000 of profit on your home, $500,000 for a married couple, can be tax-free. The yearly deductions help fewer people than you think.

### The home-sale exclusion (Section 121)
When you sell your main home at a profit, you can exclude up to $250,000 of gain from tax, or $500,000 if you are married filing jointly. You must have owned and lived in the home for at least 24 months of the 5 years before the sale; the months need not be in a row. You can use the exclusion once every 2 years. For a married couple, one spouse must meet the ownership test and both must meet the use test.

### Selling early: the partial exclusion
If the main reason you sold early was a change of workplace, health or an unforeseeable event, you get a share of the exclusion: the shortest of your ownership, your use, or the time since your last exclusion, divided by 24 months. A single filer who lived there 12 months and moved for a new job can exclude up to 12/24 × $250,000 = $125,000.

### Your cost basis: keep every receipt
Your gain is the sale price minus selling costs minus your adjusted basis: what you paid, plus certain purchase costs, plus capital improvements such as a new roof, an addition or a remodel. Repairs that only keep the home in shape do not count. Keep the records as long as you own the home and for a few years after you sell. Depreciation you claimed for a home office or rental use is taxed on sale even when the rest of the gain is excluded.

### Mortgage interest, property tax and PMI

- Mortgage interest is deductible on up to $750,000 of loan used to buy, build or improve your home (for loans taken out after December 15, 2017). The 2025 tax law made that limit permanent.
- Home equity loan or HELOC interest counts only if the money was used to buy, build or substantially improve that home, not to pay off cards or buy a car.
- Property tax shares the SALT cap with state and local income tax: $40,400 for 2026, reduced by 30% of income above $505,000 but never below $10,000.
- Mortgage insurance (PMI or FHA premiums) is deductible again from 2026 for people who itemize, phasing out above $100,000 of income.
### Why most homeowners get no tax break
All of these are itemized deductions. They only help if, with your other itemized items, they beat the standard deduction: $16,100 single or $32,200 married filing jointly in 2026. A couple with $14,000 of mortgage interest and $8,000 of property tax has $22,000, well under $32,200, so the mortgage saves them nothing extra. Only about 1 in 10 households itemizes. When you do itemize, only the amount above the standard deduction saves tax. Check yours with the second calculator below.

### When the owner dies
Heirs get a step-up in basis: the home’s basis resets to its value on the date of death, wiping out the untaxed gain. Giving a highly appreciated home away while alive passes on your old, low basis instead. Big gains, rentals and estates are worth a session with a CPA or enrolled agent.

## Worked example: A married couple sells after nine years

1. A married couple bought their home for $350,000 nine years ago and lived in it the whole time. They added a kitchen and a new roof for $60,000.
2. They sell for $1,050,000 and pay 6% in selling costs ($63,000), so they receive $987,000.
3. Their adjusted basis is $350,000 + $60,000 = $410,000. The gain is $987,000 − $410,000 = $577,000.
4. They pass the 2-of-5-year test, so $500,000 is excluded and $77,000 is taxable.
5. At the 15% long-term rate the federal tax is $11,550. With $200,000 of other income their MAGI is $277,000, so the 3.8% net investment income tax also applies to the $27,000 above $250,000: another $1,026.
Result: Without the kitchen and roof receipts the taxable gain would be $137,000, and the capital gains tax alone $9,000 higher.

## Mistakes
- Throwing away receipts for improvements. Without proof, your basis is just the purchase price and more of the gain is taxed.
- Moving out and renting the home for years, then selling after the 2-of-5-year window has closed.
- Buying a bigger home or taking a bigger loan “for the deduction” when you do not even itemize.
- Using a HELOC to pay off cards or buy a car and expecting to deduct the interest.
## Takeaways
- Up to $250,000 ($500,000 married) of home-sale gain is tax-free after 2 years of owning and living there.
- Capital improvements raise your basis; repairs do not. Keep the receipts.
- Mortgage interest and property tax only help if itemizing beats the 2026 standard deduction of $16,100 or $32,200.
- Property tax shares the $40,400 SALT cap with state income tax in 2026.
- Heirs get a step-up in basis; a gift during your life does not.
## Quiz
Q: A single filer lived in her home for 3 years and sells at a $300,000 gain. How much is taxable?
  $0
* $50,000
  $300,000
Why: She passes the 2-of-5-year test, so $250,000 is excluded and the other $50,000 is taxed as a long-term gain.

========== l-house-invest | Home & Real Estate #4 | Is a house an investment? (widget houseLeverage)
Summary: Leverage, the costs that eat price gains, house hacking and the risks of HELOCs.
Idea: Your home is first a place to live. Leverage makes small price moves big in both directions, and costs eat most of the price gain.

### What history says
Over the long run, US home prices have grown only a little faster than inflation: about 0.6% a year above inflation from 1890 to 2022 in Robert Shiller’s data, with faster gains since 1989. Stocks have returned roughly 6%–7% a year above inflation. A price chart leaves out two big things: the costs of owning, and the rent you no longer pay, which is most of a home’s real return.

### Leverage cuts both ways
With 20% down you control a home five times the size of your cash. A 4% rise on a $400,000 home is $16,000: a 20% gain on your $80,000. A 10% fall is $40,000: half your equity gone. With 5% down, a 5% dip leaves you owing as much as the home is worth, and selling would cost you money. That is what happened to many owners after the 2006 peak.

### Costs drag hard
In the $400,000 example at 7.4%, year 1 brings about $23,600 of interest and $10,000 of property tax, insurance and upkeep: more than double the $16,000 price rise. Count the $25,200 of rent you no longer pay and the home earns about 8% on your $92,000 of cash. Leave the rent out and it loses money. Selling adds commissions of about 5%–6% of the price plus other fees.

### Other trade-offs

- Illiquid: selling takes months and a big slice of the price.
- Concentrated: one building in one town, often the same place your job depends on.
- Forced saving: the good side. Paying down principal builds wealth even for people who would never invest on their own, and a fixed payment gets easier as wages rise.
### House hacking
Living in one unit of a 2–4 unit building, or renting out rooms, lets tenants cover part of your mortgage. FHA loans allow 1–4 units with 3.5% down (credit score 580+) if you live in one unit for at least a year; for 3–4 units the rents must pass a self-sufficiency test. Conventional loans allow 5% down on owner-occupied 2–4 unit homes. Buying without really moving in is occupancy fraud. Running numbers on a pure rental is in Rental property: the real numbers.

### HELOCs: handle with care
A home equity line of credit lets you borrow against your equity, usually at a variable rate. Payments can jump when the draw period ends, the lender can freeze or cut the line if your home’s value or your finances fall, and default can cost you the home. Use it for repairs or improvements, not for spending. More on borrowing wisely in Leverage the smart way.

## Worked example: A $400,000 home, one year later

1. You buy a $400,000 home with $80,000 down plus $12,000 of closing costs: $92,000 of your cash. The loan is $320,000 at 7.4%.
2. Prices rise 4%: the home gains $16,000, a 20% gain on your $80,000 of equity. That is leverage at work.
3. Year 1 also brings $23,579 of interest and $10,000 of property tax, insurance and upkeep (2.5% of the price).
4. You no longer pay $2,100 a month of rent: $25,200 saved. Net: $16,000 − $23,579 − $10,000 + $25,200 = $7,621, about 8.3% on your $92,000.
5. Had prices fallen 10% instead, the same math gives −$48,379: about 53% of your cash, while $92,000 in an index fund that fell 10% would lose $9,200.
Result: A home is a solid long-term store of value once you count the rent you save, but leverage and costs make any single year a gamble.

## Mistakes
- Judging a home by its price chart and forgetting interest, taxes, upkeep and selling costs.
- Putting almost nothing down in a hot market, so a small dip leaves you underwater and unable to sell.
- Treating home equity as a cash machine, using a HELOC for vacations or cars.
- Counting your home as retirement savings when you will still need somewhere to live.
## Takeaways
- Long-run home prices beat inflation only slightly; most of the return is the rent you do not pay.
- Leverage multiplies price moves: with 20% down, a 4% move is a 20% move in your equity.
- Owning costs can be bigger than the price gain in any single year.
- House hacking and forced saving are real advantages; HELOCs and thin equity are real risks.
## Quiz
Q: You put 10% down on a $300,000 home and prices fall 10%. What happens to your equity, before costs?
  It falls 10%
  It falls 50%
* It is wiped out
Why: Your $30,000 of equity absorbs the whole $30,000 drop, so it is gone. Leverage works both ways.

========== l-rental | Home & Real Estate #5 | Rental property: the real numbers (widget rentalCalc)
Summary: Cash flow, cap rate, depreciation and the taxes on a long-term rental.
Idea: A rental pays you only after vacancy, repairs, management, taxes and the mortgage. Depreciation then changes the tax picture.

A rental home can build wealth three ways: monthly cash flow, a loan your tenants pay down, and a price that may rise. It can also cost you money every month. The difference is in costs most first-time landlords leave out.

### Four numbers that matter

- Net operating income (NOI): rent you actually collect minus operating costs (vacancy, manager, repairs, property tax, insurance, HOA). It leaves out the mortgage.
- Cap rate: yearly NOI divided by the price. It compares properties as if you paid cash.
- Cash flow: NOI minus the mortgage payment. This is what reaches your bank account.
- Cash-on-cash return: yearly cash flow divided by the cash you put in (down payment plus buying costs).
### The costs people forget

- Vacancy: empty weeks between tenants. Landlords often budget 5% to 10% of rent.
- Management: property managers usually charge 8% to 12% of collected rent, plus a fee of a few hundred dollars to place each new tenant.
- Repairs and big-ticket items: roofs, heating and cooling, water heaters and appliances wear out. Set aside a reserve every month, often 5% to 10% of rent each for repairs and replacements.
- Landlord insurance, property tax and HOA dues, which can all rise after you buy.
### Why the old 1% rule rarely works now
The 1% rule says monthly rent should be at least 1% of the price. In September 2026 Zillow’s typical US rent was $1,932 and its typical home value $366,913, about 0.53% a month. With 30-year mortgages averaging 7.40% in mid-October 2026, many rentals bought with a loan do not cash flow. Their return then depends on rent growth, the loan being paid down, price gains and tax breaks.

### Depreciation: a deduction you never pay for
The IRS lets you deduct the building’s cost over 27.5 years, straight line. Land is not depreciable, so only the building counts. A $300,000 rental where the building is 80% of the value gives $240,000 ÷ 27.5 = $8,727 a year of deduction. That is why a rental can show a tax loss while it puts cash in your pocket.

### The passive loss rules

- Rental income and losses are passive by default, so losses usually offset only other passive income.
- If you actively participate (you approve tenants, set rent, okay repairs), up to $25,000 of rental losses a year can offset your pay. The allowance shrinks by 50 cents for every dollar of MAGI over $100,000 and is gone at $150,000.
- Losses you cannot use carry forward. They are released when you sell the property in a fully taxable sale.
- Real estate professionals (more than 750 hours a year and more than half of their working time in real estate) can treat rental losses as non-passive. A full-time employee rarely qualifies.
### When you sell

- Depreciation recapture: the depreciation you deducted is taxed at up to 25% when you sell.
- The rest of the gain is a long-term capital gain (0%, 15% or 20%), and higher earners may owe the 3.8% net investment income tax too.
- A 1031 exchange defers all of it if you buy another investment property: name the replacement in writing within 45 days and close within 180 days, using a qualified intermediary to hold the cash. It works only for real estate held for investment, not your home.
- Hold until death and heirs get a step-up in basis, which wipes out the untaxed gain.
### Before you buy

- Keep a separate cash reserve for repairs and empty months, on top of your emergency fund.
- Get landlord insurance and think about an umbrella policy (insurance lesson).
- Learn fair housing rules and screen tenants the same way every time.
- Living in one unit of a duplex can cut the down payment and the risk: see house hacking.Rental taxes have many edge cases. A CPA or enrolled agent who works with landlords is worth an hour before you buy.

## Worked example: A duplex, year one
Made-up numbers: a $260,000 duplex renting for $2,600 a month in total, bought with 25% down at 7.75%.
1. Rent is $2,600. Take off 6% vacancy ($156), a 10% manager fee on what you collect ($244), a 10% repair reserve ($260), property tax of $2,860 a year ($238) and insurance of $1,600 a year ($133). NOI is $1,568 a month, $18,815 a year: a 7.2% cap rate.
2. The $195,000 loan costs $1,397 a month. Cash flow is $171 a month, $2,051 a year. You put in $65,000 down plus about $7,800 of buying costs, so cash-on-cash is $2,051 ÷ $72,800 = 2.8%.
3. The building is 80% of the price: $208,000 ÷ 27.5 = $7,564 of depreciation a year.
4. First-year mortgage interest is about $15,053. Taxable result: $18,815 − $15,053 − $7,564 = −$3,801. A tax loss, while you collected $2,051.
5. Your MAGI is $120,000, so your allowance is $25,000 − 50% × $20,000 = $15,000. The whole $3,801 offsets your pay, saving about $912 at a 24% bracket.
Result: Year one: $2,051 of cash, about $912 of tax saved and $1,711 of loan paid down, before any price change. Thin, but positive.

## Mistakes
- Counting rent as income without vacancy, repairs and big replacements. Those turn many “positive” deals negative.
- Treating price gains and loan paydown as cash. They do not pay for the roof when it leaks.
- Expecting rental losses to cut taxes on a high salary. Above $150,000 of MAGI the $25,000 allowance is gone unless you are a real estate professional.
- Forgetting recapture. The depreciation you deducted is taxed at up to 25% when you sell, so plan the exit, or a 1031 exchange, before you buy.
## Takeaways
- Judge a rental by cash flow after every cost and the mortgage, plus cap rate and cash-on-cash return.
- Budget vacancy, management, repairs and big-ticket reserves before you trust any deal.
- Depreciation on the building (27.5 years) can create a tax loss while you collect cash.
- The $25,000 loss allowance shrinks between $100,000 and $150,000 of MAGI; unused losses carry forward.
- At sale, depreciation is recaptured at up to 25%; a 1031 exchange defers the tax.
## Quiz
Q: Your MAGI is $160,000 and your rental shows a $6,000 tax loss. What happens to the loss this year?
  It cuts the tax on your salary in full
* It carries forward until you have passive income or sell
  It is lost for good
Why: The $25,000 allowance is gone above $150,000 of MAGI, so the loss is suspended and carried forward.
## Lesson glossary
- NOI: Rent collected minus operating costs such as repairs, management, property tax and insurance, before the mortgage.
- Real estate professional: IRS status for people who spend more than 750 hours and more than half their working time in real estate. Their rental losses can be non-passive.

========== l-airbnb | Home & Real Estate #6 | Airbnb: gross vs real revenue (widget strCalc)
Summary: Fees, cleaning, managers, seasons and short-term rental tax rules.
Idea: Gross bookings are not income. Fees, cleaning, management and the mortgage can eat most of it before tax.

A listing that “books $80,000 a year” can leave its owner with almost nothing. Short-term rentals earn more per night than long-term rentals, but they cost far more to run and are taxed by their own rules.

### Why gross bookings mislead

- Occupancy taxes are paid by guests and passed on to the city or state. Airbnb collects and remits them in many places; where it does not, you must.
- Cleaning fees mostly pay the cleaner.
- Platform fees: since late 2025 Airbnb has moved US hosts to a host-only fee of about 15.5% of the booking subtotal (nightly price plus cleaning and other fees, not taxes). Guests then pay no separate Airbnb service fee. The older split fee charged hosts 3% and guests about 14% to 16.5%. To net the same as before, a host has to raise prices by about 18%.
- Vrbo charges its own commission and payment fees; check its current terms.
### The costs that eat the rest

- Cleaning after every stay, plus laundry and supplies (coffee, soap, paper goods).
- Management: full-service short-term managers commonly take 20% to 30% of revenue; co-hosts often less.
- Utilities, internet and streaming, which you pay instead of a tenant.
- Insurance that covers paying guests; a regular homeowners policy may not. Ask your insurer.
- Furniture and replacements, maintenance, software, permits, property tax and the mortgage.
### Seasons and occupancy
AirDNA’s mid-2026 outlook put average US occupancy near 57%, about where it was before 2020. Beach and ski homes swing hard by season, while the mortgage, insurance and internet bills arrive every month. Plan with monthly numbers and a cash cushion, not the yearly average.

### Check the rules before you buy
Cities, counties and HOAs can require permits, cap the number of nights or ban whole-home rentals. New York City’s Local Law 18, enforced from September 2023, requires registration and a host living in the home, allows at most two guests and bans whole-unit stays under 30 days. Most of the city’s listings disappeared. Read local rules and HOA bylaws first.

### How short-term rental income is taxed

- 14-day rule: rent a home you use for fewer than 15 days a year and you report no rent and deduct no rental costs. Handy for a big local event.
- Personal use: use the place yourself more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of rented days and your rental deductions are limited to rental income.
- 7-day average stay: if guests stay 7 days or less on average, it is not a “rental activity” under the passive rules. The $25,000 allowance does not apply, but if you materially participate (for example 500+ hours, or 100+ hours and more than anyone else, cleaners and managers included), losses can offset your pay. People call this the short-term rental loophole. Keep a dated hours log.
- Cost segregation moves part of the cost into 5-, 7- and 15-year parts. For homes bought and placed in service after January 19, 2025, 100% bonus depreciation lets you write those parts off in year one. It is a deferral: those deductions come back at ordinary rates when you sell. Some states do not follow bonus depreciation.
- Hotel-like services such as daily cleaning or meals make it a business on Schedule C with 15.3% self-employment tax. Cleaning between guests does not count.
- Payment platforms send a 1099-K only above $20,000 and 200 transactions, but all rental income is taxable either way.Tax pros disagree on some short-term rental points, and the IRS watches these returns closely. Use a CPA who handles them.

## Worked example: A beach house, first year
Made-up numbers: a $350,000 house, 25% down at 7.4%, 220 nights at $300, guests stay 4 nights on average, a co-host takes 20% of nightly revenue.
1. Nights earn 220 × $300 = $66,000 and 55 stays pay $125 cleaning fees ($6,875): a $72,875 subtotal. Guests also pay 12% occupancy tax ($8,745), so they pay $81,620 in total.
2. Take off the tax you pass on, Airbnb’s 15.5% host fee ($11,296), cleaning at $110 a stay ($6,050) and the co-host’s 20% ($13,200). $42,329 is left.
3. Supplies ($1,100), utilities and internet ($4,200), insurance ($2,400), property tax ($3,850), maintenance ($3,500) and a furniture reserve ($4,000) leave an operating profit of $23,279.
4. The $262,500 loan costs $21,810 a year. Cash before tax: $1,469.
5. For tax: $23,279 − $19,342 of first-year interest − $10,182 of depreciation = a $6,245 loss. Stays are short, but the co-host works more hours than you, so you likely fail material participation: the loss is passive and carries forward.
Result: Guests paid $81,620. You keep about $1,469, less than $2 of every $100 booked.

## Mistakes
- Treating gross bookings, including occupancy taxes and cleaning fees, as your income.
- Counting on the short-term rental loophole while a manager or cleaner works more hours on the property than you do.
- Buying before checking permits, HOA rules and local caps. A ban can turn a rental into an empty second home.
- Forgetting that cost segregation is a deferral. The early deductions come back as recapture when you sell.
## Takeaways
- Follow the money from what guests pay down to what you keep; taxes and cleaning fees are not yours.
- A 15.5% host-only fee, cleaning and management can take half of revenue before the mortgage.
- Fixed costs run every month, so plan for the slow season, not the yearly average.
- Average stays of 7 days or less plus material participation can let losses offset your pay; keep an hours log and use a CPA.
- Rent your own home for 14 nights or fewer and the income is tax-free.
## Quiz
Q: Guests stay 4 nights on average, and a co-host works more hours on the property than you. Can the tax loss offset your salary?
  Yes, any average stay of 7 days or less qualifies
* Probably not: you likely fail material participation, so the loss is passive
  Yes, as long as you use cost segregation
Why: Short stays only remove the “rental” label. You still need material participation, and the 100-hour test fails if someone else works more hours than you.
## Lesson glossary
- Host-only fee: Airbnb’s single service fee charged to the host, about 15.5% of the booking subtotal for most US hosts in 2026. Guests pay no separate service fee.
- Occupancy tax: A local hotel-style tax on short stays. Guests pay it and it is passed on to the city, county or state.
