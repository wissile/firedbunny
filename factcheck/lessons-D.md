

========== l-fire-intro | Your FIRE Number #1 | What FIRE really means (widget fireFlavors)
Summary: Financial independence, retire early: the idea, the flavors and the math behind them.
Idea: Save and invest enough that your investments pay your bills, so work becomes a choice instead of a need.

### The idea in one line
FIRE stands for financial independence, retire early. You are financially independent when your investments can pay your yearly spending for the rest of your life. The "retire early" part is optional: many people keep working, just on their own terms.

### The target is set by spending
Your target depends on what you spend, not what you earn. A common rule of thumb is about 25 times your yearly spending, which comes from the 4% rule. Spend $50,000 a year and the target is about $1.25 million. Spend $35,000 and it drops to $875,000.
Every $1,000 a year you don't need cuts $25,000 from the target and adds $1,000 a year to what you can save. That double effect is why savings rate matters so much.

### The flavors

- Lean FIRE: a small budget. Faster, with little room for surprises.
- FIRE: roughly your current lifestyle.
- Fat FIRE: a large budget with room for travel and comfort. A bigger number and a longer road.
- Barista FIRE: leave the full-time job while part-time work covers some spending and, ideally, health insurance.
- Coast FIRE: you have enough invested that it will grow into your number by a normal retirement age, so you only need to earn today's costs (more here).The labels are rough community terms. There are no official cutoffs.

### What FIRE takes

- A high savings rate, often 30% to 70% of take-home pay.
- Low-cost, diversified investing held for many years (index funds).
- A plan for what your job used to cover: health insurance (before 65), taxes on withdrawals and getting money out of retirement accounts before 59½.
### What it does not take
You don't need a huge salary, stock picking or a lucky bet. Most people who get there use ordinary index funds and keep a big gap between income and spending for 10 to 25 years.

## Worked example: Three People, Five Flavors
Same 25-times rule, very different targets. All amounts in today's dollars, with a 5% return after inflation.
1. Ana spends $36,000 a year. Her Lean FIRE number is $36,000 × 25 = $900,000.
2. Ben and Cole spend $70,000 a year as a couple. Their FIRE number is $70,000 × 25 = $1,750,000.
3. Dee wants $140,000 a year for travel and a bigger home: $140,000 × 25 = $3,500,000, a Fat FIRE target.
4. Ben takes a part-time job paying $25,000 a year. Now the portfolio only has to cover $45,000: $45,000 × 25 = $1,125,000. That Barista FIRE number is $625,000 lower.
5. Ana is 30 and has $250,000 invested. With no more saving it grows to about $250,000 × 1.05^30 ≈ $1,080,000 by 60, more than her $900,000 target. She has reached Coast FIRE and only needs to earn her $36,000 a year of costs.
Result: The flavor you pick sets the target, and spending is the biggest dial you control.

## Mistakes
- Copying a number from a blog instead of using your own spending. Your target is your spending times about 25.
- Forgetting costs your job covered: health insurance, taxes on withdrawals, and replacing cars and roofs.
- Treating Lean FIRE as a finish line with no cushion. A thin budget leaves little room for a bad market or a big bill.
- Assuming FIRE means never earning again. A little income later makes almost any plan much safer.
## Takeaways
- Financial independence means your investments can pay your spending for life.
- A rough target is 25 times yearly spending, from the 4% rule.
- Lean, regular and Fat FIRE differ by budget. Barista and Coast FIRE are ways to leave full-time work sooner.
- Before you quit, plan for health insurance, taxes and early access to retirement accounts.
## Quiz
Q: You spend $48,000 a year. Using 25 times spending, what is your rough FIRE number?
  $480,000
* $1,200,000
  $2,400,000
Why: $48,000 × 25 = $1,200,000, the same as dividing by a 4% withdrawal rate.

========== l-avg-spending | Your FIRE Number #2 | Find your real spending (widget spendAverager)
Summary: Average a full year, including the costs that only show up now and then.
Idea: Your FIRE number is built on spending, so use a full year of real numbers, including bills that come once a year or once a decade.

### Why a typical month lies
Most months look calm. Then the car needs tires, the insurance renews, December arrives and the water heater dies. A budget built from a typical month can easily miss 10% to 25% of what you really spend.

### Three ways to find your number

- Look back 12 months. Download a year of bank and card transactions and add them up. This is the most honest number.
- Track forward. Use an app or a spreadsheet for three months, then add the yearly costs you know are coming.
- Build it up. Use the calculator above: monthly costs plus every irregular cost divided by how often it happens.Use the look-back if you can. See tracking your spending for tools.

### Annualize the lumpy costs
Divide each irregular cost by how many years it lasts. A $28,000 car every 10 years is $2,800 a year. A $5,000 round of appliances and electronics every 5 years is $1,000 a year. Move that money aside monthly into a sinking fund so the bill never becomes a crisis.

### Adjust for retirement

- Goes away: retirement contributions, commuting, work clothes, and the mortgage payment if it will be paid off.
- Stays: property tax, home insurance and repairs, even with no mortgage.
- Goes up: health insurance (often the biggest new cost before Medicare at 65), travel and hobbies, and taxes on withdrawals.
- Changes over time: kids grow up and leave, and health costs rise with age.
### Then add a cushion
Many planners add about 10% for things nobody can list in advance. Watch for lifestyle creep, where spending rises with every raise: it quietly pushes your target further away.

## Worked example: The Family That Thought It Spent $5,000 a Month

1. A family adds up a typical month: housing $2,400, food $1,100, transport $500, utilities $350, health $400 and everything else $250. Total: $5,000 a month, or $60,000 a year.
2. Then the yearly bills: insurance $2,200, travel $4,000, holidays and gifts $1,500, car repairs $1,400 and home repairs $3,500. That adds $12,600.
3. Then the long-cycle costs: a $28,000 car every 10 years ($2,800 a year) and $5,000 of appliances and electronics every 5 years ($1,000 a year). That adds $3,800.
4. True yearly spending: $60,000 + $12,600 + $3,800 = $76,400. The typical-month budget missed $16,400, about 27%.
5. At 25 times spending, that gap is $16,400 × 25 = $410,000 of FIRE number they would have been missing.
Result: Count the whole year. A number built on a typical month can leave you hundreds of thousands short.

## Mistakes
- Using a calm month as your budget and forgetting yearly and once-a-decade bills.
- Dropping the mortgage from retirement spending but also forgetting the property tax, insurance and repairs that stay.
- Leaving out health insurance because an employer pays most of it today.
- Counting retirement contributions as spending. They stop when you stop working.
## Takeaways
- Use 12 months of real transactions when you can.
- Annualize lumpy costs: divide each by how many years it lasts, and save for it monthly.
- Adjust for retirement: some costs end, health insurance and taxes begin.
- Every $1,000 a year of spending adds about $25,000 to your FIRE number.
## Quiz
Q: You replace a $30,000 car every 10 years. How much should your yearly spending include for it?
  Nothing, it is not a monthly bill
* $3,000
  $30,000, but only in the year you buy
Why: $30,000 ÷ 10 years = $3,000 a year, set aside monthly in a sinking fund.

========== l-fire-number | Your FIRE Number #3 | Your FIRE number (widget fireNumber)
Summary: Spending, withdrawal rate, other income and your whole net worth in one number.
Idea: Your FIRE number is the invested money that can pay your yearly spending, after other income, for the rest of your life.

### The formula
FIRE number = (yearly spending − other yearly income) ÷ withdrawal rate. At a 4% withdrawal rate that is 25 times; at 3.5% about 28.6 times; at 3.25% about 30.8 times.

### What goes into spending

- Your real yearly spending from the last lesson.
- Health insurance and care. Retire before 65 and you buy your own coverage. For 2026, marketplace subsidies stop completely above 400% of the federal poverty level: about $62,600 of income for one person, $84,600 for two and $128,600 for a family of four. Below that line, a modest taxable income can mean a much smaller premium; see health insurance before 65.
- Taxes on withdrawals. Often low in early retirement, but rarely zero; see how withdrawals are taxed.
### Pick a withdrawal rate
The 4% rule comes from William Bengen's 1994 study of 30-year retirements: the worst historical start still lasted 30 years at about 4.15%. In 2025 he raised his figure to 4.7% for a more diversified portfolio, while Morningstar's estimate for 2026 is 3.9% (30 years, 90% success). If you stop at 40, your money may need to last 50 years or more, and research on 40- to 60-year retirements points to roughly 3.25% to 3.5%. Being willing to cut spending in bad years lets you start higher. See the 4% rule and its limits.

### Other income lowers the number
Rent from a property or steady part-time work reduces what your portfolio must pay. Social Security helps too, but benefits start at 62 at the earliest, so you need a bridge: money set aside to cover the years until they begin. The calculator adds that bridge for you. Retiring early also lowers your benefit, because Social Security averages your 35 highest-earning years (see why).

### Count your net worth carefully

- Count fully: taxable brokerage, Roth accounts and HSA.
- Count with care: pre-tax 401(k) and IRA money is taxed when it comes out, so some people count it at about 85%.
- Usually leave out: home equity, unless you will sell or downsize, and the emergency cash you want to keep.Counting the house is the most common way people fool themselves: you cannot buy groceries with a living room.

## Worked example: A Couple at 38
All amounts in today's dollars, with a 5% return after inflation. It matches the Couple preset above.
1. Living spending is $65,000, health insurance $14,000 and taxes $3,000: $82,000 a year.
2. At a 4% withdrawal rate the FIRE number is $82,000 ÷ 0.04 = $2,050,000.
3. They count $160,000 taxable, $210,000 pre-tax, $45,000 Roth, $12,000 HSA and $30,000 cash: $457,000. They leave out $180,000 of home equity. That is about 22% of the way.
4. Saving $45,000 a year, they reach $2,050,000 in about 16 years, around age 54.
5. Now add Social Security of $38,000 a year from 67, an estimate from SSA. The portfolio only needs ($82,000 − $38,000) ÷ 0.04 = $1,100,000 for the long run, plus a bridge for the years before 67.
6. Retiring around 51 means about 16 bridge years, roughly $600,000, so the target is about $1.7 million, reached in about 13 years.
Result: Spending sets the target, other income shrinks it, and only money you can actually spend counts toward it.

## Mistakes
- Counting the equity in a home you plan to keep living in.
- Using 4% for a 50-year retirement with no plan to cut spending in bad years.
- Forgetting the gap between retiring and Social Security, which starts at 62 at the earliest.
- Leaving health insurance and taxes out of retirement spending.
## Takeaways
- FIRE number = (spending − other income) ÷ withdrawal rate.
- For long retirements, 3.25% to 3.5% is the cautious range; 4% works better with flexible spending.
- Social Security lowers the number but needs a bridge until it starts.
- Count invested money you can spend; treat pre-tax money as partly taxed and leave out the house.
## Quiz
Q: You spend $60,000 a year and earn $12,000 a year from a small rental. At a 4% withdrawal rate, what is your FIRE number?
  $1,500,000
* $1,200,000
  $1,800,000
Why: ($60,000 − $12,000) ÷ 0.04 = $1,200,000. The rental covers part of the spending.
## Lesson glossary
- Social Security bridge: Money set aside to cover the spending Social Security will later pay, for the years between retiring and the first benefit check.

========== l-savings-rate | Your FIRE Number #4 | Savings rate is the lever (widget savingsRate)
Summary: Why the share of income you save decides how many years you work.
Idea: A higher savings rate works twice: more money invested each year, and a smaller target because you live on less.

### The shockingly simple math
In 2012 the blogger Mr. Money Mustache showed that, starting from zero, the years until financial independence depend almost entirely on one number: your savings rate, the share of take-home pay you save. Income barely matters, because a high earner who spends it all is no closer than anyone else.

### Why it works twice
Saving more puts more money to work each year, and it proves you can live on less, which shrinks your target. At 10% you save $1 for every $9 you spend, so piling up 25 years of spending takes most of a career. At 50% you save $1 for every $1 you spend, so each working year funds a year of freedom before any growth.

### The table
With a 5% return after inflation, a 4% withdrawal rate and nothing saved to start: 10% takes about 51 years, 25% about 32, 50% roughly 16 to 17 and 75% about 7. The chart above runs the same math with your numbers.

### How to raise it

- The big three: housing, transport and food are most of most budgets. One change there beats a hundred small cuts, though small habits add up too.
- Save your raises. Send most of every raise to savings before it turns into lifestyle creep.
- Automate. Pay yourself first with automatic transfers on payday.
- Use the tax breaks. A 401(k) match and an HSA make each dollar saved go further.
### How to measure it
Simple version: money saved ÷ take-home pay. Fuller version: add your pre-tax 401(k) contributions and the employer match to both the top and the bottom. Pick one method and stick with it so you can see progress month to month.

## Worked example: Two Ways to Use a Raise
A 5% return after inflation and a 4% withdrawal rate, starting from zero.
1. Jo takes home $80,000 and saves $16,000, a 20% savings rate. She needs about 37 years.
2. Her target is her spending times 25: $64,000 × 25 = $1,600,000.
3. She gets a $20,000 raise and saves all of it. Take-home pay is now $100,000 and saving is $36,000, a 36% rate, while spending stays at $64,000.
4. At 36% she needs about 24 years. Keeping spending flat cut about 13 years of work.
5. Had she spent the raise and kept saving $16,000, her rate would fall to 16%, her target would rise to $84,000 × 25 = $2,100,000 and the road would stretch to about 41 years.
Result: Same raise, a 17-year difference. What you do with extra income matters more than the income itself.

## Mistakes
- Chasing a higher salary while spending rises with it, so the savings rate never moves.
- Cutting tiny costs while ignoring housing and cars, the biggest lines in most budgets.
- Measuring the savings rate a different way each month, so you cannot see progress.
- Treating the table as exact. Returns vary, and the order of good and bad years matters.
## Takeaways
- Years to financial independence depend mostly on your savings rate.
- Saving more both adds money and shrinks the target.
- From zero, about 17 years at a 50% rate and about 51 years at 10% (5% real return, 4% withdrawal).
- Raises that are saved, not spent, are the fastest lever most people have.
## Quiz
Q: Two people both take home $100,000. One saves $50,000 a year, the other $20,000. With a 5% real return and the 4% rule, who reaches FI sooner, and by about how much?
  Neither, their incomes are equal
* The 50% saver, about 20 years sooner
  The 50% saver, about 2 years sooner
Why: About 17 years at 50% versus about 37 years at 20%. The savings rate sets the pace, not the income.
## Sources
- Mr. Money Mustache: the shockingly simple math behind early retirement (2012) — https://www.mrmoneymustache.com/2012/01/13/the-shockingly-simple-math-behind-early-retirement/

========== l-coast-fire | Your FIRE Number #5 | Coast FIRE and Barista FIRE (widget coastFire)
Summary: Save hard early, then let compounding finish the job while work only covers today.
Idea: Once your investments can grow into your number on their own, you only need to earn what you spend today.

### Coast FIRE
You reach Coast FIRE when the money you already have will grow into your FIRE number by your chosen retirement age, with no more saving. From then on you only need to earn enough to pay your bills. That can mean a lower-stress job, fewer hours or a career change.
Coast number = FIRE number ÷ (1 + real return)years to retirement. At a 5% return after inflation, money roughly doubles every 14 years (the rule of 72), so time does the heavy lifting.

### Barista FIRE
Barista FIRE means leaving full-time work while part-time income covers part of your spending and your portfolio covers the rest. The name comes from the idea of a part-time coffee-shop job with health benefits. Every $1,000 a year of work income cuts $25,000 from a 4% target, so $25,000 a year cuts $625,000.

### Health insurance is the catch
A part-time job with health insurance is gold before 65. Without one you buy marketplace coverage, where your income sets the subsidy. See health insurance before 65 and bridging the gap to Medicare.

### Risks to know

- Growth is not guaranteed. A coast plan assumes years of returns, and a weak decade can leave you short. Recheck every year.
- Spending creeps. If retirement spending ends up higher than planned, the coast number was too low.
- Fewer earning years can lower Social Security (why).
- Keep a margin. Many people keep saving a little even after crossing the line.

## Worked example: Coasting From 35
A 5% return after inflation and a 4% withdrawal rate.
1. Sam is 35, expects to spend $50,000 a year and wants to fully retire at 60. FIRE number: $50,000 × 25 = $1,250,000.
2. Years to 60: 25. Coast number: $1,250,000 ÷ 1.05^25 = $1,250,000 ÷ 3.386 ≈ $369,000.
3. Sam has $380,000 invested, a little above the coast number. With no new saving it should grow to about $1,287,000 by 60.
4. Sam moves to a 30-hour job paying $52,000 a year that covers the $50,000 of spending, with a small margin.
5. Barista FIRE instead: $380,000 supports about $15,200 a year at 4%, so part-time work would have to cover the other $34,800 for as long as it lasts. Coasting asks for more work income now but leads to full retirement at 60.
Result: Once you cross the coast number, work only has to pay today's bills.

## Mistakes
- Using an optimistic return. At 7% instead of 5%, the coast number in the example looks about 38% smaller.
- Mixing nominal and real numbers. If your FIRE number is in today's dollars, use a return after inflation.
- Stopping all saving the day you cross the line, with no margin for a bad decade.
- Cutting back to part-time work without a plan for health insurance.
## Takeaways
- Coast number = FIRE number ÷ (1 + real return) ^ years to retirement.
- After Coast FIRE, work only has to cover today's spending.
- Barista FIRE lets part-time income pay part of the bills, shrinking the number.
- Recheck every year and keep a margin: growth is not guaranteed.
## Quiz
Q: Your FIRE number is $1,000,000 and you plan to fully retire in 28 years. With a 5% return after inflation, roughly what coast number do you need today?
  $1,000,000
* About $255,000
  About $500,000
Why: $1,000,000 ÷ 1.05^28 ≈ $1,000,000 ÷ 3.92 ≈ $255,000.

- The Motley Fool: types of FIRE and which one is right for you (2025) — https://www.fool.com/retirement/2025/07/26/types-of-fire-which-one-is-right-for-you/

========== l-geo-arbitrage | Where Will You Live? #1 | Where your money goes furthest (widget cityCompare)
Summary: The same life costs very different amounts in different places.
Idea: The same savings buy a very different life in different places. A cheaper place shrinks your FIRE number.

### What geographic arbitrage means
Geographic arbitrage means earning or saving in a high-cost place and living in a cheaper one. Your FIRE number is roughly 25 times your yearly spending at a 4% withdrawal rate. So every $1,000 a month you cut from living costs lowers the number by about $300,000.

### What really drives the bill

- Housing is the biggest line almost everywhere, and the one that changes most between places.
- Health cover is the American wildcard. Before 65, a full-price marketplace plan for two people in their 50s can cost around $2,000 a month, while low taxable income can bring it to a few hundred (see Health insurance before 65). Abroad, residents often use cheaper public systems or local private plans.
- Flights home for holidays, weddings and aging parents.
- Visas and admin: renewals, translations, lawyers and residence cards.
- Currency: if the dollar falls 20%, a life priced in euros costs 25% more in dollars.
### What cost-of-living sites miss
Survey sites average what locals and foreigners report. They miss the expat premium in popular neighborhoods, furnished short-term rentals that cost far more than a local one-year lease, imported goods, international schools, a car where you need one, and air conditioning in hot places. Use them to compare places, then build your own budget.

### Taxes travel with you
Moving abroad does not end US taxes: as a citizen you file a US return wherever you live, and your new country may tax you as a resident too. Read US taxes follow you abroad and the France example. Inside the US, moving to a state with no income tax is a smaller but simpler win.

### Try before you move
Rent for one to three months, ideally in the less pleasant season, and track every dollar. Check the visa rules (Visas for early retirees), health care and how far you will be from family. Many people split the year, or keep a small base at home for the first year.

## Worked example: One couple, three places
A couple in their early 50s compares the same comfortable life in three places, at a 4% withdrawal rate.
1. In a US big city the life costs about $11,350 a month, including about $2,000 for full-price health insurance. That is $136,200 a year, so they would need about $3.4 million invested.
2. In a Midwest city it costs about $6,400 a month ($76,800 a year), a FIRE number of $1.92 million. If their taxable income is low enough for a marketplace subsidy, health cover falls to about $230 a month and the number to about $1.39 million.
3. In Valencia it costs about $3,760 a month, including Spanish private health insurance and flights home: $45,120 a year, a FIRE number of about $1.13 million.
4. They test currency risk. If the dollar falls 20% against the euro, everything priced in euros costs 25% more in dollars: about $4,610 a month, and a number of about $1.38 million.
5. They have $1.25 million invested. That covers Valencia today but not the weak-dollar case, so they plan a three-month trial stay and one more year of saving as a cushion.
Result: Same life, three places: about $3.4 million, $1.9 million or $1.1 million. Housing and health cover make most of the difference.

## Mistakes
- Budgeting from a two-week holiday. Tourist prices and short-term rentals cost far more than a local one-year lease, and a holiday skips the boring bills.
- Leaving out the lines that cost-of-living sites skip: health insurance, flights home, visa renewals and a buffer for currency swings.
- Assuming a cheaper country means lower taxes. US citizens file US taxes everywhere, and the new country may tax worldwide income too.
- Selling everything before a trial stay. Family, health care, language and loneliness send many people home within a few years.
## Takeaways
- Every $1,000 a month you cut from spending lowers your FIRE number by about $300,000 at a 4% withdrawal rate.
- Housing and health cover decide most of the gap between places.
- Keep a cushion of 10% to 20% of spending for currency moves, and budget flights home and visa costs.
- Rent first, for a few months, before you commit.
## Quiz
Q: At a 4% withdrawal rate, how much does cutting spending by $1,000 a month lower your FIRE number?
  About $12,000
* About $300,000
  About $48,000
Why: $1,000 a month is $12,000 a year, and 25 times that is $300,000.

========== l-van-life | Where Will You Live? #2 | Van, RV, trailer or house? (widget nomadCost)
Summary: What a home on wheels really costs, and where you legally live.
Idea: A home on wheels swaps rent for fuel, campsites, repairs and lost value. It is cheaper only if you buy used and drive slowly.

### The hidden rent: lost value
A van or motorhome loses value every year you own it. That drop is a real cost, even though no bill arrives. A $60,000 van that loses about 10% a year is worth about $39,400 after four years: roughly $430 a month. Motorhomes usually lose value faster. Buying used and keeping it longer are the biggest savings.

### Fuel and miles
Moving costs money. At 17 miles per gallon and $3.30 a gallon, 10,000 miles a year is about $160 a month of fuel; a motorhome at 8 mpg burns more than twice that. The cheapest nomads move slowly and stay in one area for weeks.

### Where you sleep

- Paid campgrounds and RV parks charge by the night, with weekly and monthly rates that cost less per night.
- Free camping on public land (boondocking) costs little, but stays in one spot are usually limited to about two weeks, and you need water, a dump station and showers.
- Memberships and friends’ driveways fill the gaps.
### A home state on paper
Full-timers still need a legal home, called a domicile. Florida, South Dakota and Texas are the most popular choices: none has a state income tax, and mail-forwarding services there give you a street address. Your driver’s license, vehicle registration, insurance rates, voting and jury duty all follow that state.

### Health insurance on the road
Marketplace plans are sold by the area of your domicile, and many cover routine care only in a local network, with emergencies covered elsewhere. A plan bought in South Dakota may not cover a routine checkup in Arizona. Look for plans with national or PPO networks, or plan routine care for when you are home. See Health insurance before 65.

### Plan the exit
Many people road-trip for two to five years, then settle. Keep a repair fund (a roof leak or transmission can cost thousands and leave you without a home for a week), and think about resale before you buy.

## Worked example: Four years in a used van

1. A couple buys a used camper van for $60,000 and plans four years on the road, driving 10,000 miles a year.
2. If the van loses about 10% a year, it is worth about $39,400 after four years. That $20,600 drop is about $430 a month: their hidden rent.
3. Fuel at 17 mpg and $3.30 a gallon is about $160 a month. Paying for a campsite 40% of nights at $38 is about $480 a month, including small costs on free nights.
4. Insurance ($165), repairs and upkeep (about $175) and internet, mail and phone (about $175) bring the total to about $1,585 a month.
5. A small-town rental with one car would cost about $2,225 a month for the same things, so the van saves about $640 a month: $7,670 a year, or about $190,000 off a FIRE number at 4%.
Result: The van wins because it was bought used and moves slowly. A $110,000 motorhome would lose about $1,100 a month in value alone.

## Mistakes
- Counting fuel and campsites but forgetting that the vehicle loses thousands of dollars of value every year.
- Buying a new $150,000 build for a lifestyle you have not tried. Rent one for a few weeks first.
- Choosing a domicile state without checking how its health plans work when you are far from home.
- No repair fund. A breakdown can cost thousands and leave you paying for a hotel while you wait.
## Takeaways
- Lost value is the hidden rent of a vehicle home: buy used and keep it longer.

========== l-travel-budget | Where Will You Live? #3 | Budget for a life of travel (widget travelBudget)
Summary: Slow travel, flights home and insurance: what a travelling retirement costs.
Idea: Travel costs fall fast the longer you stay in each place. Budget travel as a yearly line, then multiply it by 25.

### Two ways to travel
Trips from a home base come on top of your normal costs: rent or a mortgage keeps running while you are away. Full-time travel replaces housing, so compare it with your whole home budget instead of adding it on.

### Why slow travel is cheaper
Flights are a fixed cost per move. Stay three days and each day carries a big share of the flight; stay a month and the flight almost disappears. Longer stays also unlock weekly and monthly rental discounts, let you cook, and are far less tiring.

### The lines to budget

- Flights, including trips home for family events.
- Lodging, nightly or monthly.
- Food, local transport and activities, often the biggest line.
- Insurance: trip insurance for trips; travel medical or international health insurance for full-timers.
- Admin: visas, phone plans, a storage unit or car insurance back home.
### Health cover when you are away
Medicare generally does not cover care outside the US, and many US plans cover only emergencies abroad. Travel medical plans for nomads are cheap but limited (they often exclude pre-existing conditions) and are not full health insurance. See Health care and insurance abroad.

### Travel in your FIRE number
Every $1,000 a year of travel needs about $25,000 invested at a 4% withdrawal rate. Many retirees travel most in their first active years and less later, so some keep a separate travel fund for the early years instead of raising their spending forever. Pay with a card with no foreign transaction fee and always pay in local currency (Travel money with no foreign fees).

## Worked example: Three short trips or one long stay

1. A couple plans three two-week trips a year, mid-range, with $700 round-trip flights each.
2. Flights: 3 trips × 2 people × $700 = $4,200.
3. Lodging at $95 a night with a weekly discount of about 15% comes to about $3,390. Food, transport and activities at $65 per person per day add $5,460, and trip insurance at 5% adds about $650. Total: about $13,700 a year.
4. At a 4% withdrawal rate that travel needs about $343,000 invested (25 × $13,700).
5. The same 42 days as one six-week stay needs one set of flights ($1,400) and gets a monthly rental rate about 40% off: about $9,700 a year in all.
Result: Staying longer in one place saves about $4,000 a year, or about $100,000 off the FIRE number, for the same number of days away.

## Mistakes
- Budgeting flights and hotels but not daily spending, which is often the biggest line.
- Assuming Medicare or a US health plan covers you abroad. It usually does not, so you need travel or international health insurance.
- Moving every few days for months. Costs and fatigue both climb.
- Forgetting the bills at home while you are away: storage, car insurance, a phone plan or rent you still pay.
## Takeaways
- Every $1,000 a year of travel adds about $25,000 to your FIRE number at 4%.
- Longer stays spread flight costs and unlock monthly discounts.
- Trips from home add to your budget; full-time travel replaces housing.
- Budget insurance and flights home as their own lines.

========== l-household | Where Will You Live? #4 | Partners, kids and your number (widget householdFire)
Summary: How a partner and children change spending, and the number, over time.
Idea: Children raise spending for about twenty years, then it falls. Plan that bump on its own instead of funding the peak forever.

### Two adults, one plan

- Count both partners’ net worth and plan one shared budget: two people living together spend less than two singles.
- A partner with little or no pay can still save: a spousal IRA lets a non-working spouse contribute based on the working spouse’s pay (2026 limit $7,500, or $8,600 at 50 and over). See When to open an IRA.

Kid costs last roughly 18 to 22 years. If you set your FIRE number at peak spending, you over-save for the decades after they leave. Instead, aim for your long-run spending times 25 and plan the kid years separately: from income while you still work, a 529 plan, or a side pot you spend down.

The College Board’s average full budget for 2025-26 was about $31,000 a year at a public in-state college and about $65,500 at a private one. A 529 plan grows tax-free for this. Local public schools abroad can be free but teach in a new language; international schools often charge as much as a US private school, so get real quotes before you plan. 1. A couple spends $58,000 a year on themselves and has two children, aged 1 and 3.
2. Today they spend about $114,400: $58,000 for the adults, $15,000 per child, and about $1,100 a month of childcare for each child under 5.
3. Childcare ends in four years and spending falls to $88,000. It rises again when college overlaps: about $120,000 in year 17, with both kids in public college at about $31,000 each.
4. From year 21 the kids are gone and spending settles at $58,000. Their long-run FIRE number is $1.45 million (25 × $58,000).
5. The kid years add about $807,000 of extra spending in total. Funding the $120,000 peak forever would need about $3 million; $1.45 million plus a separate $807,000 for the kid years needs about $2.26 million.
Result: Treating the kids as a 20-year bump, not a new forever budget, cuts the target by about $740,000. Work income, a 529 and steady saving can carry much of the bump.

## Mistakes
- Using today’s spending, with toddlers in daycare, as your forever number.
- Ignoring the partner with few work years: a spousal IRA and spousal Social Security benefits both help.
- Going without life or disability insurance while the whole plan rests on one income.
- Saving for college before your own retirement. College can be borrowed for; retirement cannot.
## Takeaways
- Plan as a household: one budget, both partners’ accounts and both Social Security records.
- Kid costs are a bump of about 20 years. Fund it separately from your long-run number.

- Protect the plan with insurance while children depend on your income.

========== l-visas | Where Will You Live? #5 | Visas for early retirees (widget visaFinder)
Summary: Passive income and retirement visas: who qualifies and what comes with them.
Idea: To live abroad longer than a tourist stay you need a residence visa. Most retiree visas ask for steady passive income or savings.

### A tourist stay is not residency
Americans can visit much of Europe’s Schengen area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, and other countries set their own tourist limits. Living somewhere longer needs a residence visa or permit. Overstaying can bring fines and bans.

### The visas early retirees use

- Passive income visas accept steady income from dividends, interest, rent or pensions (Portugal’s D7, Spain’s non-lucrative visa, France’s visitor visa).
- Pensioner visas need a lifetime pension, such as Social Security (Costa Rica, Panama, Colombia). If you stop at 45, you will not have one until 62 at the earliest.
- Savings or deposit routes accept money in the bank (Mexico at some consulates, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia).
- Digital nomad visas need income from remote work, so they usually do not fit a fully retired person.
### What every application asks for
Proof of income or savings (bank statements, often for six to twelve months), health insurance, a criminal record check, a place to live, and documents with official stamps (apostilles) and translations. Most residence permits must be renewed, and many require you to spend a minimum time in the country each year.

### Residency usually means tax residency
Living somewhere most of the year usually makes you a tax resident there, while the US keeps taxing you as a citizen. Some countries offer special regimes for newcomers or pensioners; others tax worldwide income or wealth. Read US taxes follow you abroad and the France example before you choose.

### Plan your path
Visit first, read the official consulate page for your region (amounts change each year with minimum wages and exchange rates), and budget a few months for paperwork. For anything unusual, an immigration lawyer in the country is worth the fee. Think ahead too: permanent residency and citizenship rules differ a lot between countries.

## Worked example: A couple at 52 checks the doors
Figures marked as reported should be confirmed with the consulate.
1. A couple aged 52 lives on about $3,200 a month from dividends and a small rental. They have no pension yet and $70,000 in cash they could deposit.
2. Spain’s non-lucrative visa asks a couple for €36,000 a year, about $3,370 a month. They are just short, so they would need more income or another route.
3. Portugal’s D7 is reported at about the Portuguese minimum wage plus 50% for a spouse, roughly $1,550 a month, so they likely qualify; they confirm with the consulate.
4. Pensioner visas in Costa Rica, Panama and Colombia need a lifetime pension, so they will not qualify until Social Security starts at 62 or later.
5. Thailand’s retirement visa (age 50+) accepts 800,000 baht, about $23,800, in a Thai bank, and the Mexican consulate in San Diego accepts about $68,200 of average savings: both are within reach.
Result: Several doors are open, but each also needs health insurance, a background check and translated paperwork, and living there usually makes them tax residents.

## Mistakes
- Planning around a digital nomad visa after you stop working. Those visas need work income.
- Counting portfolio withdrawals as income when a visa wants a pension or regular payments. Read exactly what counts.
- Forgetting that becoming a resident usually makes you a tax resident too.
- Relying on blog figures. Amounts change with minimum wages and exchange rates, and each consulate can add its own rules.
## Takeaways
- Tourist stays are short; living abroad needs a residence visa.
- Passive income visas fit most early retirees; pensioner visas wait for Social Security.
- Savings and deposit routes can work when your income is low.
- Always confirm the current amount on the official consulate page.

  A savings-deposit route
Why: Pensioner visas need a lifetime pension such as Social Security, which starts at 62 at the earliest.

========== l-fire-tax | Taxes in Early Retirement #1 | How FIRE withdrawals are taxed (widget fireTaxCalc)
Summary: Which dollars count as income, and how gains stack on the 0% band.
Idea: Only the gain in a taxable sale counts as income, and a couple can realize about $131,000 of long-term gains at 0%.

### Three kinds of money come out
When you live off your portfolio, each dollar comes from one of three places, and the IRS treats them very differently.

- Taxable brokerage account. When you sell, only the gain counts as income. If you sell $10,000 of shares you bought for $6,000, $4,000 is gain and $6,000 is your own money coming back. Held more than a year, that gain is a long-term gain with its own low rates.
- Traditional 401(k) or IRA. Every dollar you take out is ordinary income, taxed like a paycheck. Before 59½ there can also be a 10% penalty (see money out before 59½).
- Roth IRA, HSA for medical bills, cash. Not income at all, if you follow the rules.
### The 2026 numbers that matter

- Standard deduction: $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household. That much income is taxed at 0%.
- Low ordinary brackets: 10% and 12% cover taxable income up to $50,400 single or $100,800 married filing jointly.
- Long-term gains and qualified dividends: 0% while your taxable income is up to $49,450 single, $98,900 married or $66,200 head of household. 15% above that, 20% above $545,500 single or $613,700 married.
- Net investment income tax: an extra 3.8% on investment income once income passes $200,000 single or $250,000 married. These lines are not raised for inflation.
### Gains stack on top
The IRS lines up your income in a set order. Ordinary income (IRA withdrawals, interest, part-time pay) fills the standard deduction and the low brackets first. Long-term gains sit on top. Whatever part of your gains lands below the 0% line is taxed at 0%.
So a married couple with no other income can realize $98,900 + $32,200 = $131,100 of long-term gains in 2026 and owe no federal income tax. A single person can realize $65,550. Every dollar of IRA income you add pushes a dollar of gains up toward the 15% band.

### What a 4% withdrawal really costs
Married filing jointly, no other income, 2026 rules:

- $80,000 from a taxable account where half of each sale is gain: $40,000 counts, $7,800 is taxable after the deduction, all at 0%. $0 federal tax.
- $120,000 from a taxable account that is all gain: $87,800 taxable, still under the 0% line. $0.
- $120,000 all from a traditional IRA: $87,800 of ordinary taxable income. $10,040, about 8.4%.
- A $40,000 Roth conversion plus $80,000 of sales with $40,000 of gain: $780.Many early retirees pay little or no federal income tax. Your account mix matters more than your portfolio size.

### Control the gain you realize
Most brokers let you choose which share lots you sell (specific identification). Selling high-cost lots realizes less gain; selling low-cost lots in a 0% year raises your cost basis for free. Hold tax-efficient index ETFs in the taxable account: actively managed mutual funds can pay out capital gains every December even when you sell nothing, and dividends count as income even when reinvested.

### States and the hidden tax
States with no broad income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas and Wyoming. Washington has no wage tax but taxes large long-term gains. Most other states tax gains like ordinary income, so a 4-6% state tax can be the biggest tax bill an early retiree pays.
The other hidden tax is health insurance: marketplace subsidies depend on the same income, and in 2026 they stop completely above 400% of the poverty line. See health insurance before 65. Rules get detailed fast; a CPA or enrolled agent can check your own numbers.

## Worked example: A couple spends $90,000 and pays $0
Married filing jointly, both 48, 2026 rules, no paycheck.
1. They hold $1.4 million in a taxable account with a $700,000 cost basis, so about half of every sale is long-term gain. They sell $90,000 of shares.
2. Only the gain counts: $45,000. The other $45,000 is their own money coming back.
3. Income the IRS counts is $45,000. After the $32,200 standard deduction, taxable income is $12,800, all long-term gain and far below the $98,900 0% line. Federal tax: $0.
4. They also convert $32,200 from a traditional IRA to Roth. That ordinary income uses up the standard deduction, which pushes the $45,000 of gains up to $45,000 of taxable income: still inside the 0% band. Federal tax: still $0.
5. Their state taxes income at a flat 5% above a deduction like the federal one. Without the conversion they owe about 5% × $12,800 = $640; with it, about 5% × $45,000 = $2,250.
Result: They spend $90,000 and move $32,200 into Roth for $0 federal tax. The state bill is the one to plan for.

## Mistakes
- Treating the whole sale as income. Only the gain part of a taxable sale counts; the rest is your own money coming back.
- Taking big traditional IRA withdrawals first. They are ordinary income, push your gains out of the 0% band, and before 59½ can add a 10% penalty.
- Ignoring fund payouts. Mutual funds can pay out capital gains every December even if you sell nothing, and reinvested dividends still count as income.
- Looking only at federal tax. State income tax and lost health-insurance subsidies can cost more than the federal bill.
## Takeaways
- Taxable sales: only the gain counts. Traditional 401(k) and IRA: all of it counts. Roth and HSA for medical: none of it counts.
- In 2026 a married couple with no other income can realize $131,100 of long-term gains at 0% federal tax; a single person $65,550.
- Ordinary income fills the deduction and brackets first, and gains stack on top of it.
- Many FIRE households pay little or no federal tax; state tax and health subsidies often matter more.
## Quiz
Q: A married couple with no other income sells $100,000 of shares they bought for $40,000. About how much federal income tax do they owe in 2026?
* $0
  $9,000 (15% of the gain)
  $15,000 (15% of the sale)
Why: Only the $60,000 gain counts. After the $32,200 deduction, $27,800 of taxable income sits inside the 0% band.

========== l-zero-bracket | Taxes in Early Retirement #2 | The 0% capital gains bracket (widget gainHarvest)
Summary: Fill low-income years with 0% gains or cheap Roth conversions.
Idea: Years with little income are a tax gift. Fill them on purpose with 0% gains or Roth conversions taxed at 10-12%.

### Why the early years are special
Between your last paycheck and the age when Social Security and required withdrawals start, your income can be very low. The standard deduction and the low brackets reset every January. Room you do not use this year is gone.

### Gain harvesting
Sell investments that have gone up in your taxable account and buy them straight back. The wash-sale rule only blocks losses, so you can rebuy the same fund the same day. You pay 0% on the gain if it fits in the band, and your cost basis resets to today’s price, so a future sale has less gain to tax.
Example: a single person with $30,000 of taxable income can realize about $19,450 of gains at 0% in 2026 ($49,450 − $30,000). Do not harvest losses in a year your gains are taxed 0% anyway: the loss is wasted.

### Roth conversions
Moving money from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA is taxed as ordinary income now, so the money grows tax-free afterwards and never faces required withdrawals. In a low-income year that tax can be small. Married filing jointly with no other income in 2026:

- The first $32,200 converted is tax-free (the standard deduction).
- Up to $57,000 stays in the 10% bracket: $2,480 of tax.
- Up to $133,000 stays in the 12% bracket: $11,600 of tax, 8.7% on average.Paying 10-12% now beats 22% or more later if large required withdrawals would push you higher. Conversions also feed the Roth ladder.

### They compete for the same room
Conversions are ordinary income, so they sit under your gains. Each dollar converted pushes a dollar of gains from the 0% band into 15%. Once that happens, an extra dollar of conversion costs 12% plus 15%: about 27%. The widget shows this as the cost of the next $1,000.
A common rule of thumb: with a large pre-tax balance, conversions in the 10-12% brackets usually come first; with a small one, harvest gains.

### Watch the side effects

- Health insurance. The enhanced marketplace subsidies ended after 2025. For 2026 coverage the subsidy stops completely above 400% of the poverty line: $62,600 for one person, $84,600 for two, $128,600 for four. Conversions and harvested gains count toward that income, and from 2026 any extra advance subsidy must be paid back in full.
- Medicare premiums. From about 63, income counts toward Medicare premium surcharges two years later.
- State tax may apply even when federal tax is $0.For your own plan, a CPA or enrolled agent can model several years at once.

## Worked example: Dana and Lee fill an empty year
Married filing jointly, 52 and 50, 2026 rules. They buy health insurance on the marketplace for two.
1. They spend $60,000 from their taxable account. 40% of each sale is gain, so $24,000 counts. With $4,000 of interest, their income is $28,000: below the $32,200 deduction. Tax $0, and most of the year’s 0% room is unused.
2. They convert $20,000 from a traditional IRA to Roth. Ordinary income becomes $24,000, still under the deduction. Tax still $0.
3. They sell and immediately rebuy a fund with $30,000 of gain. Income the IRS counts is now $78,000 and taxable income $45,800, all gains inside the 0% band. Tax still $0.
4. Room left in the 0% band: $131,100 − $78,000 = $53,100.
5. Their marketplace income is $78,000, under the $84,600 line for two. Another $10,000 of conversion would cross the line and cost them the whole health subsidy.
Result: $20,000 moved to Roth and $30,000 of cost basis raised, for $0 federal tax and the health subsidy kept.

## Mistakes
- Harvesting losses in a year when your gains are taxed 0% anyway. The loss is wasted; keep it for a year it saves real tax.
- Converting so much that gains spill into the 15% band. Past that point each extra conversion dollar costs about 27%.
- Crossing the marketplace subsidy line by a few dollars. In 2026 the subsidy ends completely above 400% of the poverty line.
- Forgetting the state. Some states tax harvested gains and conversions even when your federal bill is $0.
## Takeaways
- Low-income years are a use-it-or-lose-it chance to pay 0-12%.
- Gain harvesting: sell and rebuy right away. Wash sales only apply to losses.
- Conversions and gains share the same room; conversions sit underneath and push gains up.
- Check health subsidies, Medicare premiums and state tax before you fill the bands.
## Quiz
Q: Why can you sell a fund at a gain and buy it back the same day?
* The wash-sale rule only applies to sales at a loss
  Gains under $10,000 are exempt
  Brokers waive the rule for index funds
Why: The wash-sale rule disallows losses. A gain is taxed at whatever rate applies, 0% here, and your new cost basis is the higher price.

========== l-early-access | Taxes in Early Retirement #3 | Money out before 59½ (widget accessLadder)
Summary: Roth ladder, 72(t) and the Rule of 55: reach retirement money early.
Idea: Retirement money is not locked until 59½. Build a bridge from taxable savings, Roth conversions, 72(t) payments or the Rule of 55.

### The 10% penalty and its exits
Money taken from a traditional 401(k) or IRA before 59½ is taxed as income plus a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies. The IRS keeps a long list. The ones early retirees use most are below. Others include disability, medical bills above 7.5% of income, one $1,000 emergency withdrawal a year, and (IRA only) $10,000 lifetime for a first home, college costs and health premiums while unemployed.

### Money that is always open

- Taxable account: no age rules. Only the gain in each sale is taxed, often at 0% (see how FIRE withdrawals are taxed).
- Roth IRA contributions: the money you put in comes out first, any time, with no tax and no penalty.
- HSA receipts: you can reimburse yourself tax-free, with no time limit, for medical bills paid after you opened the HSA. Keep the receipts. Non-medical HSA withdrawals before 65 are taxed plus 20%.
### The Roth conversion ladder
Each year, convert some traditional IRA money to Roth. You pay income tax on it that year, ideally at 0-12%. Each conversion then has its own five-year clock that starts on January 1 of the year you convert. After that, the converted amount (not its earnings) comes out without the 10% penalty. A $40,000 conversion in December 2026 is open from January 1, 2031.
The catch: you need five years of other money while the first rung seasons, so start converting the year you stop working, or earlier. Withdrawals come out in a set order: contributions first, then conversions oldest first, then earnings. Conversions count as income for health subsidies.

### 72(t): substantially equal periodic payments
You can take fixed yearly payments from an IRA at any age if you follow an IRS formula: the RMD method, fixed amortization or fixed annuitization. The interest rate can be up to the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate. Example: a $500,000 IRA at 50, fixed amortization at 5%, life expectancy 36.2 years, pays about $30,200 a year.
Payments must continue until the later of five years or age 59½. Change them and the 10% penalty comes back on every past payment, plus interest. You may switch once to the RMD method. Many people split their IRA first so only the money they need is locked in.

### The Rule of 55
Leave your job in or after the calendar year you turn 55 and that employer’s 401(k) or 403(b) is open with no 10% penalty (50 for many public-safety workers). It does not cover IRAs or plans from old jobs, and rolling the money to an IRA loses it. If your plan allows, roll old 401(k)s into your current plan before you leave. Governmental 457(b) plans have no 10% penalty at all.

### Build the bridge
Common combinations: taxable savings for the first five years and a Roth ladder after; 72(t) payments when most money is pre-tax and you stop in your 40s; the Rule of 55 if you leave at 55 or later. Mistakes here are costly and hard to undo, so a CPA or enrolled agent should check a 72(t) setup.

## Worked example: Retire at 45 with a Roth ladder
Married filing jointly, both 45, today’s dollars with a 4% real return.
1. Sam and Ali spend $50,000 a year. They have $250,000 in a taxable account, $60,000 of Roth IRA contributions and $900,000 in old 401(k)s rolled into IRAs.
2. Each January they convert $50,000 from the IRA to Roth. With no other ordinary income, $32,200 is covered by the standard deduction and $17,800 is taxed at 10%: $1,780 a year.
3. Ages 45 to 49 are paid from the taxable account. At 50 and 51 the Roth contributions fill in.
4. From age 50 the first conversion, made at 45, has seasoned five tax years. From 51 on, each year’s spending comes from the conversion made five years earlier.
5. They reach 59½ with no 10% penalty and about $580,000 still growing in the IRA.
Result: A five-year runway plus yearly conversions bridges 15 years, for about $1,780 of federal tax a year on the conversions.

## Mistakes
- Starting a Roth ladder without five years of other money. Conversions are not penalty-free until their five-year clock runs out.
- Changing 72(t) payments early. Any change before the end date brings back the 10% on every past payment, plus interest.
- Rolling your 401(k) into an IRA after leaving at 55. The Rule of 55 only covers the employer plan, so the rollover loses it.
- Forgetting that conversions count as income for marketplace health subsidies. A big conversion can cost you the subsidy.
## Takeaways
- Taxable savings and Roth contributions are open at any age.
- Each Roth conversion becomes penalty-free after its own five-tax-year clock.
- 72(t) payments avoid the penalty, but must not change until the later of five years or 59½.
- The Rule of 55 opens the 401(k) of the job you leave in or after the year you turn 55.
## Quiz
Q: You are 50 and convert $40,000 to a Roth IRA in December 2026. When can you take that $40,000 out without the 10% penalty?
* January 1, 2031
  December 2031
  Only at 59½
Why: Each conversion’s five-year clock starts on January 1 of the year you convert, so a December 2026 conversion is open from January 1, 2031.

========== l-aca | Health Care & Social Security #1 | Health insurance before 65 (widget acaSubsidy)
Summary: Marketplace tax credits, the 400% cliff and how to steer your income.
Idea: Before 65, what you pay for health insurance depends on your income, not your savings. Steer your income and you steer the premium.

### Income, not wealth
If you retire before 65 you buy your own health insurance, usually on the ACA marketplace (HealthCare.gov or your state’s site). The premium tax credit that lowers the price depends on your household’s MAGI compared with the federal poverty level (FPL). Your savings do not count. A couple with $2 million invested and $60,000 of income is treated as a $60,000 household.

### The 2026 rules: the cliff is back
The extra-generous credits of 2021 to 2025 expired at the end of 2025 and were not renewed. The House passed a three-year extension in January 2026, but the Senate has not voted on it as of October 2026, so check HealthCare.gov before you plan. For 2026 coverage the original rules apply again:

- The credit is available from 100% to 400% of the poverty level, using the 2025 guidelines: $15,650 for one person, $21,150 for two, $32,150 for four.
- At 400% help stops completely: $62,600 single, $84,600 for a couple, $128,600 for a family of four. That is the subsidy cliff.
- Below it you pay a set share of income for the benchmark (second-cheapest silver) plan: 2.10% under 133% of FPL, rising to 9.96% from 300% to 400%. The credit covers the rest.
- For 2027 the cliff still applies under current law and the top share rises to 10.22%. Open enrollment for 2027 runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027 on HealthCare.gov (December 15 for coverage from January 1).
### What counts as MAGI
MAGI for the marketplace is your adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest, untaxed foreign income and the untaxed part of Social Security. In practice:

- Counts: wages, traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, Roth conversions, interest and dividends, realized capital gains, all of your Social Security benefit.
- Does not count: withdrawals of your own Roth IRA contributions, cash from savings, the cost-basis part of shares you sell, loans.
- Lowers it: HSA contributions and deductible traditional IRA contributions (you need earned income for an IRA).
### Medicaid and the coverage gap
In the 41 states (with DC) that expanded Medicaid, adults under about 138% of FPL usually get Medicaid instead of a credit. It is based on income, not assets. In the 10 states that did not expand, adults under 100% of FPL often get neither: the coverage gap.
Some early retirees with large savings but low income have used Medicaid as their health plan. That gets much harder from January 1, 2027 (states may start earlier and some may get extensions to the end of 2028): expansion adults aged 19 to 64 must show 80 hours a month of work, community service, a work program or half-time school, or earnings of at least 80 times the federal minimum wage, unless exempt (for example medically frail people and some caregivers). Coverage is also rechecked every six months. A retiree living on savings usually meets none of these, so plan on the marketplace instead.

### Reconciliation: the repayment trap
The credit is usually paid in advance, based on your income estimate. At tax time Form 8962 compares it with your real income. From tax year 2026 the cap on repaying extra credit is gone. If your final MAGI lands one dollar over 400%, you repay every dollar of credit you received.

### How early retirees steer MAGI

- Choose what you sell: high-cost-basis shares create little gain.
- Keep a cash or Roth-contribution bucket for years when you need low income.
- Do Roth conversions and gain harvesting in years when you are not on the marketplace, or only up to your target.
- Bronze and catastrophic plans count as HSA-eligible from 2026, so HSA contributions can trim MAGI.
- Check your total in November, before year-end fund distributions surprise you. Report changes to the marketplace during the year.Taxes and coverage interact in ways that are easy to miss: the 0% capital gains lesson and the bridge to Medicare build on this. A CPA or enrolled agent can check your plan.

## Worked example: A couple at 58, close to the line
Made-up numbers for 2026 coverage. Check your own area’s premium on HealthCare.gov.
1. Household of two: the 2025 poverty guideline is $21,150, so 400% is $84,600.
2. The benchmark silver plan for both of them costs $2,400 a month, or $28,800 a year.
3. They plan MAGI of $80,000, which is 378% of FPL. Their share is 9.96%: 0.0996 × $80,000 = $7,968 a year. The credit covers $28,800 − $7,968 = $20,832.
4. In December a fund pays out a surprise $5,000 gain. MAGI becomes $85,000, or 402% of FPL. No credit at all, and with no repayment cap they owe back the full $20,832 at tax time.
5. The fix: spend from cash or Roth contributions and sell high-basis shares, keeping MAGI at about $84,000, and check it again before year-end.
Result: Here $5,000 of extra income would have cost $20,832. Plan withdrawals around the line.

## Mistakes
- Assuming your savings rule you out. The marketplace looks at income, not assets.
- Forgetting that Roth conversions, realized gains and your whole Social Security benefit count in MAGI.
- Estimating income too low. From 2026 there is no cap on paying back extra credit.
- Planning on the 2021-2025 credits. They expired, and the 400% cliff is back.
## Takeaways
- Marketplace credits depend on MAGI and household size, measured against the poverty level.
- For 2026 coverage help stops above 400% of FPL: $62,600 single, $84,600 for two, $128,600 for four.
- Below about 138% of FPL in an expansion state, adults usually get Medicaid instead.
- Choosing which money you spend (cash, Roth contributions, high-basis shares) is how early retirees control MAGI.
## Quiz
Q: A retired couple has $2 million invested and plans $60,000 of MAGI in 2026. What decides their marketplace credit?
  Their $2 million of savings
* Their income compared with the poverty level, plus the local benchmark price
  Nothing: retirees cannot get credits before 65
Why: Credits use MAGI and household size, not assets. $60,000 is under the $84,600 line for two.
## Lesson glossary
- Benchmark plan: The second-lowest-cost silver plan in your area. The tax credit is sized against its price, but you can use the credit on any metal level.
- Reconciliation: Comparing the advance credit you received with the credit your real income allows, on Form 8962 with your tax return.

========== l-health-bridge | Health Care & Social Security #2 | Bridging the gap to Medicare (widget healthBridge)
Summary: COBRA, the marketplace, a spouse’s plan or a job: covering every year before 65.
Idea: Every year before 65 needs a health plan. Price the whole bridge before you pick your retirement date.

### Your options before 65

- A spouse’s workplace plan. Often the cheapest bridge if your spouse keeps working.
- COBRA. Keep your old employer plan for up to 18 months after leaving, at up to 102% of its full cost. You have at least 60 days to elect it. It keeps your doctors and this year’s deductible progress.
- The ACA marketplace. Pick a plan within 60 days of losing job coverage. With income under the 400% line, the price is capped as a share of income (see health insurance before 65). If you drop COBRA early on purpose, you usually wait until open enrollment.
- Medicaid if your income is low enough in your state.
- A part-time job with benefits, the idea behind Barista FIRE.
- Living abroad with a national system or an expat plan (see health care abroad).
### Health care sharing ministries are not insurance
Sharing plans can look cheap, but state regulators warn that they are not insurance, are not required to pay your bills and do not have to cover pre-existing conditions. Treat them as a risk, not as a bridge.

### Age drives the full price
Marketplace premiums can be up to three times higher at 64 than at 21. The national average benchmark plan for a 40-year-old was $625 a month in 2026, from about $401 in New Hampshire to $1,299 in Vermont, so a 60-year-old in an average area faces about $1,330 a month at full price. Under the 400% line the credit absorbs that age jump; above it you pay it all.

### Where the HSA fits
HSA money cannot pay regular marketplace premiums, but it can pay COBRA premiums, coverage while you receive unemployment benefits, long-term care insurance and, from 65, Medicare premiums. From 2026 bronze and catastrophic marketplace plans count as HSA-eligible, so you can keep contributing before Medicare. Contributions must stop once you enroll in Medicare.

### Medicare at 65 and the two-year lookback
Sign up in the seven-month window around your 65th birthday; late Part B enrollment adds 10% to the premium for each full year of delay, for life. In 2026 Part B is $202.90 a month, but higher incomes pay a surcharge called IRMAA, based on your tax return from two years earlier. A big Roth conversion or home sale at 63 raises your premiums at 65. If you stopped working, Form SSA-44 lets you ask Social Security to use your newer, lower income.

## Worked example: Maya retires at 60
Single, $50,000 of planned MAGI a year, an expansion state. Her COBRA price of $800 a month is an example.
1. COBRA for the full 18 months costs 18 × $800 = $14,400.
2. At $50,000 she is at 319% of the poverty level, so the benchmark plan costs her 9.96% of income: $4,980 a year, or $415 a month, whatever her age.
3. Marketplace from 61½ to 65 (42 months): 42 × $415 = $17,430. COBRA first totals $14,400 + $17,430 = $31,830.
4. Marketplace from day one: 60 months × $415 = $24,900, which is $6,930 less.
5. At 63 she keeps MAGI under $109,000, so her first Medicare year has no surcharge: Part B is $202.90 a month in 2026.
Result: Going straight to the marketplace saves about $6,900. COBRA makes sense mainly mid-treatment or to keep the same doctors.

## Mistakes
- Choosing COBRA by default without pricing the marketplace at your new, lower retirement income.
- Dropping COBRA midyear and expecting to switch: ending it early on purpose usually means waiting for open enrollment.
- Relying on a sharing ministry as your only coverage. It is not insurance and payment is not guaranteed.
- Doing a large Roth conversion or sale at 63 and paying higher Medicare premiums at 65.
## Takeaways
- List every year from your retirement date to 65 and give each one a health plan and a price.
- Under the 400% line the marketplace caps the benchmark premium as a share of income, so age matters less.
- COBRA lasts up to 18 months at up to 102% of the plan’s full cost, and HSA money can pay it.
- Medicare premiums at 65 depend on your income at 63: plan conversions with that in mind.
## Quiz
Q: Which premium can you pay with HSA money before 65?
  A regular marketplace plan premium
* COBRA premiums
  Any premium once you are retired
Why: IRS rules allow HSA money for COBRA, coverage while on unemployment, long-term care insurance and, from 65, Medicare premiums.
## Lesson glossary
- Health care sharing ministry: A group whose members share medical bills, often faith-based. Not insurance: payment is voluntary and pre-existing conditions can be excluded.
- Special enrollment period: A window outside open enrollment, often 60 days after losing job coverage, when you can buy a marketplace plan.

========== l-ss-early | Health Care & Social Security #3 | Social Security when you stop early (widget ssEarly)
Summary: Zero years, the 40 credits, claiming age and what an early stop really costs.
Idea: Social Security averages your best 35 years. Stopping early adds zeros, but the formula softens the blow more than most people expect.

### How the benefit is figured
Social Security takes your 35 highest-earning years (adjusted for wage growth), adds them up and divides by 420 months. That is your average indexed monthly earnings. A formula with two bend points then gives your benefit at full retirement age, the PIA: for people first eligible in 2027 it is 90% of the first $1,340, 32% of the amount up to $8,077 and 15% above that. The first dollars count most, which is why an early stop hurts less than you might fear.

### The 10-year minimum
You need 40 credits to qualify. You earn up to four a year, one for each $1,890 of earnings in 2026. Ten years of real work is enough to get a benefit for life.

### Zero years
If you stop after 23 years of work, the 35-year average includes 12 zeros. Your statement at ssa.gov assumes you keep earning until you claim, so it overstates the benefit for an early retiree. Recalculate with your planned stop age, or use the calculator here as a rough guide.

### When to claim

- Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
- Claiming at 62 cuts your benefit by 30% for life.
- Waiting to 70 adds 8% a year, 24% in total.
- A spouse with little or no work record can get up to 50% of your PIA at their full retirement age (35% less at 62). Delayed credits do not raise it.
- When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the larger of the two benefits, which is a strong reason for the higher earner to wait.
### The earnings test
If you claim before full retirement age and still work, Social Security holds back $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 (2026). In the year you reach full retirement age it is $1 for every $3 above $65,160, counting only months before your birthday. Withheld money is not lost: your benefit is recalculated at full retirement age.

### Will it be there?
The 2026 Trustees Report projects the combined trust funds run out in 2034, after which incoming taxes pay about 83% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts. A cautious plan uses that 83% figure; the chip in the calculator shows it.

### Taxes and living abroad
Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable once your provisional income passes $25,000 single or $32,000 married; those thresholds have never been raised. US citizens can usually receive benefits abroad; Treasury cannot send payments to Cuba or North Korea, and a few other countries are restricted. Agreements with countries such as France stop you paying into two systems and let you combine credits: see US taxes follow you abroad. The Social Security Fairness Act of January 2025 ended the rules that cut benefits for people with some public or foreign pensions.

## Worked example: Sam stops at 45
Sam earns about $90,000 a year in today’s dollars from age 22. Simplified: wages already in today’s dollars, full retirement age 67.
1. Working until at least 57 fills 35 years. Average monthly earnings: $90,000 ÷ 12 = $7,500.
2. Formula: 90% of the first $1,340 is $1,206, plus 32% of the next $6,160 is $1,971.20. Benefit at 67: about $3,177 a month.
3. Stopping at 45 leaves 23 working years and 12 zeros: $90,000 × 23 ÷ 420 = $4,929 a month on average.
4. Formula: $1,206 + 32% of $3,589 ($1,148) = about $2,354 a month at 67, roughly 26% less.
5. Claiming age moves it more: $2,354 × 0.70 = $1,648 at 62, or $2,354 × 1.24 = $2,919 at 70.
Result: Twelve fewer working years cut the benefit by about 26%. Claiming at 70 instead of 62 raises it by 77%.

## Mistakes
- Thinking an early stop wipes out Social Security. Forty credits qualify you, and the formula favors the first dollars.
- Planning with the number on your statement, which assumes you keep working until you claim.
- Claiming at 62 by default when savings could bridge the gap, and locking in a 30% cut for life, which can also shrink a surviving spouse’s check.
- Claiming early while still earning, then being surprised when the earnings test holds back payments.
## Takeaways
- Your benefit uses your 35 best years; missing years count as zeros.
- You need 40 credits, about ten years of work, to qualify at all.
- Because the formula pays 90% on the first dollars, stopping in your 40s often cuts the benefit by only a quarter or a third.
- Claiming age matters as much as working years: 62 pays 30% less than 67, and 70 pays 24% more.
- Plan conservatively for about 83% of scheduled benefits after 2034.
## Quiz
Q: You worked 20 years and stop at 42. What happens to your Social Security?
  You get nothing unless you work 35 years
* You qualify, and the benefit averages 20 working years with 15 zero years
  Your benefit stays at the amount on today’s statement
Why: Twenty years gives far more than 40 credits. The 35-year average simply includes 15 zeros.
## Lesson glossary
- AIME: Your 35 best years of wage-indexed earnings divided by 420 months. The benefit formula starts from it.
- Bend points: The two dollar amounts in the benefit formula where the rate drops from 90% to 32% and from 32% to 15%.

========== l-us-tax-abroad | Retiring Abroad #1 | US taxes follow you abroad (widget expatTax)
Summary: Americans file US taxes wherever they live. What the FEIE, credits and treaties do, plus the forms.
Idea: The US taxes its citizens on worldwide income wherever they live. Exclusions and credits stop most double tax, but the filing never stops.

### Citizens file wherever they live
The US is one of very few countries that taxes by citizenship. Move to Lisbon or Bangkok and you still file a Form 1040 every year your income is over the filing threshold, reporting income from everywhere. Your new country usually taxes you too, because you live there. Two returns a year is normal for an American abroad.
If you live abroad on April 15 you get an automatic extension to June 15 to file, but interest on any tax due still runs from April 15. Form 4868 extends the deadline to October 15.

### The foreign earned income exclusion (FEIE)
The FEIE lets you leave up to $132,900 of foreign earned income off your US return in 2026, per person, on Form 2555. Earned means wages and self-employment income. To claim it you need a tax home abroad plus either 330 full days outside the US in any 12-month period or bona fide residence abroad for a full tax year.

- It does nothing for dividends, interest, capital gains, IRA or 401(k) withdrawals, pensions or Social Security. A retiree living on a portfolio gets no help from it.
- It does not reduce self-employment tax.
- Income above the exclusion is taxed at the higher rates it would have faced with the excluded pay included (the stacking rule).
- Excluded pay does not count as compensation for IRA contributions, and it still counts as income for marketplace health subsidies if you ever come back.
### The foreign tax credit
The other tool is a dollar-for-dollar credit for income tax you pay abroad, on Form 1116. In a country with higher tax than the US, the credit often wipes out the US tax on your foreign pay, keeps that pay available for IRA contributions, and leaves unused credit you can carry forward for up to 10 years. In a low-tax country the FEIE usually wins. The widget compares both.
A credit only helps when there is US tax to cancel. If your US tax is already $0, because you live in the 0% capital gains band (see the 0% bracket), there is nothing to offset.

### Treaties and the saving clause
Tax treaties decide which country taxes which income first and who gives the credit. But almost every US treaty has a saving clause: the US keeps taxing its own citizens as if the treaty did not exist, apart from listed exceptions. So a treaty rarely removes your US return; it mostly decides where the credit comes from. The next lesson walks through France as a full example.

### Report your foreign accounts

- FBAR (FinCEN 114): required when all your foreign financial accounts added together top $10,000 at any moment of the year. It is filed online with FinCEN, separately from your tax return, due April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15.
- Form 8938 (FATCA): filed with your return. Living abroad, the thresholds are $200,000 at year end or $300,000 at any time for single filers, and $400,000 or $600,000 for married couples filing jointly. Missing it can cost a $10,000 penalty, plus up to $50,000 more if you keep ignoring IRS notices.
### Social security taxes and your last state
Self-employed Americans abroad owe US self-employment tax even when the FEIE covers their income tax, unless a totalization agreement assigns them to the other country’s system. The US has 31 of these agreements; the one with France has been in force since July 1, 1988.
Your last US state may also keep taxing you if it still sees you as living there. Change your domicile, driver’s license and voter registration, and keep proof of the move. Cross-border rules get complicated fast, so a CPA or enrolled agent who works with expats is worth the fee in your first year abroad.

## Worked example: A retired couple abroad
Made-up couple, 2026 US federal rules, married filing jointly.
1. They live on $40,000 of dividends and long-term gains from US funds, $30,000 of IRA withdrawals and $38,000 of Social Security. None of it is earned income, so the FEIE is zero.
2. Provisional income is $30,000 + $40,000 + half of $38,000 = $89,000. That is above $44,000, so the maximum 85% of Social Security is taxable: $32,300.
3. AGI is $30,000 + $40,000 + $32,300 = $102,300. After the $32,200 standard deduction, taxable income is $70,100.
4. The $30,100 of ordinary income costs $2,480 (10% of $24,800) plus $636 (12% of $5,300) = $3,116. The $40,000 of gains and dividends sits under the $98,900 line, so it is taxed at 0%.
5. They keep $60,000 in a local bank. All foreign accounts topped $10,000, so they file an FBAR. They stay under the $400,000 Form 8938 line.
Result: About $3,100 of US tax, an FBAR, and a tax return in their new country too. The FEIE did nothing for them.

## Mistakes
- Thinking you stop filing US taxes once you move. Citizens file every year their income is over the threshold, wherever they live.
- Expecting the FEIE to shelter retirement income. It covers only wages and self-employment income.
- Forgetting the FBAR. It is a separate online filing, and the trigger is all foreign accounts added together topping $10,000 at any moment.
- Claiming the FEIE by habit in a high-tax country. The foreign tax credit can leave less US tax, keeps IRA eligibility and builds credits you can carry forward.
## Takeaways
- US citizens file a 1040 wherever they live, and usually a return in their new country too.
- The FEIE ($132,900 in 2026) covers earned income only, and not self-employment tax.
- The foreign tax credit is the main tool for retirees and people in high-tax countries.
- Treaties decide who gives the credit; the saving clause keeps your US return.
- FBAR above $10,000 in foreign accounts; Form 8938 above $200,000 or $300,000 (single, abroad).
## Quiz
Q: You retire abroad and live on dividends and IRA withdrawals. What does the FEIE do for you?
  Excludes the first $132,900
* Nothing: it covers only earned income
  Excludes half of it
Why: The FEIE applies to wages and self-employment income only. Investment income and retirement withdrawals stay fully reportable.
## Lesson glossary
- Stacking rule: With the FEIE, the income you still report is taxed at the rates it would face if the excluded pay were included.
- Tax home: The place where you mainly work or do business. For the FEIE it must be outside the US.

========== l-treaty-france | Retiring Abroad #2 | Tax treaties: the France example (widget franceTax)
Summary: What really happens to a US citizen, or a US-French dual citizen, who lives in France.
Idea: France taxes you because you live there. The US taxes you because you are a citizen. The treaty decides who gives the credit, not whether you file.

### Two countries, two reasons to tax
France taxes its residents on income from everywhere. Nationality does not matter: live in France and you are in. The US taxes its citizens wherever they live. A US citizen living in France therefore files in both countries, plus an FBAR and possibly Form 8938. Being a French citizen as well changes none of this, because France already taxes you as a resident.

### The treaty in plain words
The US-France tax treaty was signed in 1994, took effect in 1996 and was updated in 2004 and 2009. Its saving clause lets the US tax its citizens as if the treaty did not exist, with exceptions. The parts that matter most for a retiree:

- US Social Security paid to a French resident is taxable only in the US. This exception survives the saving clause. France still counts it when setting the rate on your other income.
- 401(k) and IRA withdrawals are taxed by France as the country where you live. The US also taxes you as a citizen and gives a credit for the French tax.
- Dividends and interest from publicly traded US companies and the US government go into your French return, but US citizens get a French credit equal to the French income tax on them, which removes that tax.
- Gains from selling shares are generally taxed by the country where you live, so France taxes them at its flat tax, and the US credits the French income tax.
- A Roth IRA is a question mark: its French treatment is uncertain. Assume Roth earnings could be taxed in France until an adviser tells you otherwise.
### French income tax: the 0% band and household parts
The 2026 scale (on 2025 income) is applied per part: 0% up to €11,600, 11% to €29,579, 30% to €84,577, 41% to €181,917 and 45% above. A couple has 2 parts, each of the first two children adds half a part and each later child a full part (the saving from children is capped at €1,807 per half-part). A further reduction, the décote, helps low tax bills. Together they mean €0 income tax up to about €17,200 of taxable income for a single person and about €32,500 for a couple.

### Social charges: the part people forget
On top of income tax, France levies social charges on investment income. In 2026 they are 18.6% on most of it (CSG 10.6%, CRDS 0.5% and a 7.5% solidarity levy), so the flat tax on dividends and gains, the PFU, is 31.4%: 12.8% income tax plus 18.6% social charges. Life insurance (assurance-vie), rental income and real-estate gains stay at 17.2%. A reduced 7.5% rate exists only for people covered by an EU, EEA or Swiss social security system, so someone covered by US Social Security appears to owe the full rate. Whether the treaty credit also removes social charges on US dividends is unsettled; the widget lets you see both cases.

### So do you pay nothing under a certain amount?
As a blanket rule, no. There is no special exemption for dual citizens. In a narrow sense, yes: a low income can mean €0 French income tax (the 0% band plus the décote) and $0 US federal tax (the standard deduction plus the 0% capital gains band up to $98,900 of taxable income for a married couple). But French social charges start at the first euro of investment income: €10,000 of dividends or gains can cost €1,860 even when income tax is zero. And US Social Security is taxed only by the US, where at low incomes little or none of it may be taxable.

### Other French rules to know

- IFI, a wealth tax on real estate only, applies when your net real estate tops €1.3 million on January 1. Financial assets are not in it.
- The US-France totalization agreement (in force since July 1, 1988) stops you paying both countries’ social security taxes on work.
- French funds and many assurance-vie contracts can be PFICs for Americans (see banks, brokers and PFICs).This is one of the hardest areas of personal tax. Before you move, have a tax professional who handles both US and French returns run your real numbers.

## Worked example: A couple of 62 in Bordeaux
Made-up US citizens, married, $95,000 of income a year, $1.12 per euro. Same numbers as the “Couple at 62” preset.
1. They take $15,000 of dividends from US funds, sell $20,000 of fund gains, withdraw $30,000 from a 401(k) and get $30,000 of Social Security.
2. France puts the 401(k) money and Social Security on its scale: about €53,600 over 2 parts, or €3,340 of tax. Social Security gets a credit for its half, so they owe about €1,670, all on the 401(k) money.
3. The gains pay the flat tax: €2,290 of income tax plus €3,320 of social charges on about €17,860. The dividends’ income tax is credited away, but in this estimate their €2,490 of social charges remains.
4. The US taxes everything: $25,500 of the Social Security is taxable, so taxable income is $58,300. Dividends and gains fall in the 0% band, and the $23,300 of ordinary income costs $2,330.
5. The US credits $1,259 of French tax against the 401(k) part, leaving $1,071 of US tax, which is the tax on Social Security.
Result: About €9,770 in France (≈ $10,940) plus $1,071 in the US: roughly $12,000, or 12.6% of their income. Social charges (€5,810) are the biggest piece. Living in a US state with no income tax, the same income would have cost $2,330 of federal tax.

## Mistakes
- Believing dual citizenship removes a tax. France taxes residents and the US taxes citizens, so a dual citizen living in France gets both.
- Looking only at income tax. Social charges of 18.6% hit most investment income from the first euro, even when income tax is zero.
- Assuming a Roth IRA stays tax-free in France. Its French treatment is uncertain.
- Opening French funds or an assurance-vie without US advice. They can be PFICs, with punishing US tax and extra forms.
## Takeaways
- France taxes residents on worldwide income; the US taxes citizens. A dual citizen in France files in both.
- US Social Security is taxed only by the US under the treaty; 401(k) and IRA withdrawals are taxed by France first.
- French income tax can be €0 below about €17,200 (single) or €32,500 (couple) of taxable income.
- Social charges of 18.6% (2026) apply to most investment income from the first euro.
- Get a US-French tax professional to check your numbers before you move.
## Quiz
Q: A US citizen living in France receives US Social Security. Under the treaty, who taxes it?

Why: The treaty makes social security taxable only by the paying country, and this exception survives the saving clause. France can still count it when setting the rate on other income.

- Quotient familial: France’s household parts system: income is divided by the number of parts, taxed, then multiplied back, so families pay less.
- Décote: A French reduction that shrinks or cancels small income tax bills.
- PFU: France’s flat tax on investment income: 12.8% income tax plus social charges, 31.4% in total for most income in 2026.
- IFI: A French wealth tax on real estate only, when net real estate tops €1.3 million.

========== l-expat-money | Retiring Abroad #3 | Banks, brokers and PFICs abroad (widget expatChecklist)
Summary: Keep your accounts working, avoid the PFIC trap and set up Social Security before you move.
Idea: Sort out your broker, bank and investments before you move. The wrong local fund can cost far more than any bank fee.

### Your US broker after you move
Some US brokers limit what you can do once you have a foreign address: no new fund purchases, restricted trading, or a request to move the account. Others run international or expat account programs. Ask before you move: can I keep buying funds, trade options, get statements and sign in from abroad? Tell the truth about where you live. Keeping a US address you do not really use can break the account terms.

### Banking where you live
You will need a local account for rent, utilities and usually your visa. Banks abroad must report American customers to the IRS under FATCA, so expect extra forms. Move money at a fair exchange rate: compare what you are offered with the mid-market rate, because bank wires often hide a markup. Carry a card with no foreign transaction fee (see travel money).

### The PFIC trap
To US tax law, most non-US mutual funds and ETFs are PFICs (passive foreign investment companies). That includes European UCITS funds run by US firms. Each one needs a Form 8621, and the default regime taxes gains at the top rate plus an interest charge, which can take most of the gain. Special elections exist but add work. The simple rule: keep investing through US-domiciled funds such as VT or VTI, or individual stocks and bonds. Your new country may tax US funds in its own way, so check that too.

### Currency risk
If your savings are in dollars and your life is in euros, a weaker dollar raises your costs. At $1.12 per euro, €3,000 a month costs about $3,360. At $1.25 per euro it costs $3,750; at $1.00, $3,000. A buffer of 10–20% of a year’s spending, or some savings in the local currency, softens the swings.

### Social Security and Medicare from abroad
US citizens can generally receive Social Security abroad. Payments cannot be sent to Cuba or North Korea, and a few countries have restrictions. Medicare generally does not pay for care outside the US, but signing up for Part B late adds 10% to the premium for each 12 months you could have had it. See health care abroad.

### If you ever renounce
Giving up US citizenship is permanent and done in person at a US embassy or consulate, for a fee (check travel.state.gov for today’s amount). You owe an exit tax as a covered expatriate if your net worth is $2 million or more, your average yearly income tax over the past five years tops $211,000 (2026), or you cannot certify five years of tax compliance on Form 8854. For covered expatriates, gains above $910,000 (2026) are taxed as if everything had been sold the day before. Never decide this without a specialist.

## Worked example: Ana moves to Porto
Made-up retiree with $600,000 at a US broker and $1.12 per euro.
1. Before moving she asks her broker about foreign addresses. It allows existing holdings but limits new fund purchases, so she opens an account with a broker that serves Americans abroad.
2. She keeps her money in VT, a US-domiciled world fund. It is not a PFIC, so there is no Form 8621.
3. A local bank suggests its own European fund. That would be a PFIC, so she says no.
4. She keeps €20,000 in a Portuguese bank account. Her foreign accounts top $10,000, so she files an FBAR each year.
5. She needs €3,000 a month, about $3,360. She keeps a buffer of 15% of a year’s spending, about $6,000, in euros.
Result: No PFICs, one FBAR, a working brokerage account and a currency cushion: the boring setup that avoids expensive surprises.

## Mistakes
- Buying a local fund because the bank suggests it. For an American it is almost always a PFIC.
- Keeping a fake US address so the broker does not notice the move.
- Ignoring currency risk because the exchange rate looks fine on moving day.
- Skipping Medicare Part B at 65 without knowing the late-enrollment penalty if you return.
## Takeaways
- Ask your broker about foreign addresses before you move.
- Stick to US-domiciled funds or individual stocks and bonds: most foreign funds are PFICs.
- Expect FATCA forms at local banks and file an FBAR when foreign accounts top $10,000.
- Keep a currency buffer of 10–20% of a year’s spending.
- Renouncing citizenship is permanent and can trigger an exit tax.
## Quiz
Q: Which of these is usually a PFIC for an American living abroad?

* A European UCITS ETF
  Shares of a US company
Why: Funds based outside the US, including European UCITS ETFs, are generally PFICs. US-listed funds and ordinary shares are not.
## Lesson glossary
- UCITS: The European rulebook for investment funds. Funds sold to Europeans usually follow it, and they are PFICs for Americans.
- Covered expatriate: Someone who gives up US citizenship and meets the net worth, tax or compliance tests, so owes the exit tax.

========== l-expat-health | Retiring Abroad #4 | Health care and insurance abroad (widget expatHealth)
Summary: Public systems, private and international plans, US trips and the Medicare question.
Idea: Abroad, health cover comes in layers: a main plan, cover for the gaps, a plan for US trips, and a Medicare decision at 65.

### Medicare stays home
Medicare generally does not pay for care outside the US. If you move abroad at 65 or later, your main cover must come from your new country or a private plan. Medicare Part B costs $202.90 a month in 2026. Dropping it saves that money, but if you return and sign up later, the premium rises 10% for each full 12 months you could have had it. Many people keep Part B to keep the door home open; others drop it on purpose. Decide before you go.

### Your main cover depends on the country and the visa

- France: stable residents can join public health insurance (PUMa) and usually add a top-up policy. The long-stay visitor visa asks for private insurance at the start.
- Spain: the non-lucrative visa requires Spanish private health insurance with no copays or deductibles; travel insurance is not accepted.
- Costa Rica: legal residents must join the national system, the CAJA.
- Thailand: the LTR “wealthy pensioner” visa needs at least $50,000 of health cover, or $100,000 in the bank.Visa rules change often: check the consulate’s current list before you buy anything.

### Travel medical is not health insurance
Nomad and travel medical plans are cheap for young travelers. One popular plan cost $62.72 per four weeks for ages 18 to 39 when checked, with a $250,000 overall limit. But they exclude pre-existing conditions and cancer treatment, cover the US only as an add-on, and limit time in your home country. They are fine for a healthy 30-year-old on the move, not as the only cover for a 60-year-old.

### International expat plans
International plans are built for people living abroad long term. Price depends on your age, the area of cover (worldwide excluding the US costs less), the deductible and your health history, and it rises as you age. Compare several insurers, and check whether the plan can be renewed for life and how it treats conditions you already have.

### Compare with staying in the US
Before 65, the alternative at home is a marketplace plan. In 2026 the national average benchmark silver plan for a 40-year-old costs about $625 a month with no subsidy, and older buyers pay much more. Moving back to the US usually opens a special enrollment window for marketplace coverage. See health insurance before 65 and bridging to Medicare.

## Worked example: Pat retires to Valencia at 62
Made-up retiree; check the consulate and insurers for real terms.
1. For Spain’s non-lucrative visa she buys Spanish private health insurance with no copays or deductibles. A travel policy would not be accepted.
2. She visits family in the US three weeks a year, so she buys travel medical cover for those trips. Her Spanish plan does not cover the US.
3. At 65 she must decide on Medicare Part B: $202.90 a month in 2026, or a 10% higher premium for each full year without it if she moves back later.
4. She plans to stay for good, so she drops Part B and puts the $2,435 a year toward her Spanish plan.
Result: Three layers, chosen on purpose: a plan her visa accepts, cover for US trips, and a Medicare decision made with the penalty in mind.

## Mistakes
- Assuming Medicare will pay for care abroad. It generally does not.
- Relying on a nomad plan as full health insurance in your 50s or 60s.
- Buying a policy before checking what your visa requires.
- Ignoring the Part B late-enrollment penalty when you might move back.
## Takeaways
- Medicare generally does not cover care outside the US.
- Your visa often decides your main cover: Spain wants private insurance with no copays; Costa Rica requires the CAJA.
- Travel medical plans exclude pre-existing conditions and are not full health insurance.
- Decide on Part B before 65: $202.90 a month in 2026, or a 10% penalty per year if you return late.

========== l-swr | Stress-Test Your Plan #1 | The 4% rule and its limits (widget swrSim)
Summary: Where the 4% rule comes from, and why early retirees often use less.
Idea: Spending 4% in year one, raised with inflation, survived 30 years in US history. Longer retirements need a little less.

### Where 4% comes from
In 1994 a financial planner named Bill Bengen tested every 30-year retirement in US history since 1926. He asked: what is the most you could withdraw in year one, then raise with inflation every year, without running out? For a mix of 60% US stocks and 40% Treasury bonds, the worst period still allowed about 4.15%. That got rounded to the 4% rule. In 1998 three professors at Trinity University ran a similar test (the Trinity study) and found 3% to 4% held up well for stock-heavy portfolios.
Read the rule carefully. You withdraw 4% of your savings in the first year only. After that you take the same dollars plus inflation, whatever the market does. With $1M you take $40,000 in year one; if prices rise 3%, you take $41,200 in year two.

### What “safe” means

- Success means money was left at the end of the period. It says nothing about how much.
- The rule is built on the worst historical stretch. In most periods retirees following it ended with more than they started with.
- Bengen’s research assumed tax-deferred accounts. Taxes, fund fees and advice fees come out of the same 4%, so count them in your spending.
- Bengen calls inflation retirees’ greatest enemy: high inflation early in retirement and expensive stock markets both lower the safe rate.
### Why early retirees use less
A 40-year-old may need money for 50 or 60 years. The research blog Early Retirement Now found that 4% with a 50/50 mix succeeded about 95% of the time over 30 years, but only about 65% of the time over 60 years. Its summary for 40 to 60 year retirements: about 3.25% to 3.5%. Looking forward instead of back, Morningstar puts the safe starting rate for 2026 at 3.9% for 30 years with 30% to 50% in stocks. Bengen himself raised his figure to 4.7% in a 2025 book by adding small-company and international stocks.

### Turn a rate into your number

- 4% = 25 times your yearly spending
- 3.5% = 28.6 times
- 3.25% = 30.8 times
- 3% = 33.3 timesFor $60,000 a year that is $1.5M, $1.71M, $1.85M or $2M. Build yours in Your FIRE number.

### Ways to safely spend more
The rates above assume you never change your spending. Real people do. Morningstar finds flexible methods can support starting rates approaching 6% if your spending can move with markets. Other levers: a few years of part-time income, a later and bigger Social Security check, and low fees. See sequence risk and guardrails next.

## Worked example: A 50-year plan for $60,000 a year
A couple aged 45 spends $60,000 a year and wants the money to last to 95.
1. They need 50 years of withdrawals, not the 30 years the 4% rule was built for.
2. At 4% they would need $60,000 × 25 = $1.5M. Over 60-year periods, 4% held up only about 65% of the time in Early Retirement Now’s tests.
3. At 3.5% they need $60,000 ÷ 0.035 = $1.71M (28.6 times). At 3.25%, $60,000 ÷ 0.0325 = $1.85M (30.8 times).
4. They pick 3.5%. Year one they withdraw $60,000. Inflation runs 3%, so year two they withdraw $61,800, whatever the market did.
5. They also agree to trim travel after a bad year, which gives them a cushion if the first decade goes badly.
Result: Planning at 3.5% means saving about $214,000 more than the 4% rule, in exchange for a plan that held up even over 60-year stretches.

## Mistakes
- Reading the rule as 4% of each year’s balance. That is a different method, and its spending swings up and down with the market.
- Using a 30-year rule for a 50- or 60-year retirement without lowering the rate or adding flexibility.
- Leaving taxes, fund fees and health insurance out of the spending number the rate is applied to.
- Treating 90% success as a guarantee, or a failed path as going broke overnight. Failing paths show warning signs for years, and you can react.
## Takeaways
- The 4% rule: withdraw 4% in year one, then raise that dollar amount with inflation.
- It survived the worst 30-year period in Bengen’s US data; it is a floor from history, not a forecast.
- For 40 to 60 years, research points to roughly 3.25% to 3.5%, or about 29 to 31 times spending.
- Flexible spending, part-time income and low fees let you safely spend more.
## Quiz
Q: Under the 4% rule, a retiree with $1M withdraws $40,000 in year one. Inflation is 3%. What do they withdraw in year two?
  4% of whatever the balance is now
* $41,200: the same spending raised for inflation
  $40,000 again, whatever happens
Why: The rule sets a dollar amount in year one and raises it with inflation each year, whatever the market does.
## Lesson glossary
- Monte Carlo simulation: Running thousands of random “what if” futures to see how often a plan works.
- Success rate: The share of past periods or simulated futures in which the money lasted the whole time.
- Trinity study: A 1998 study by three Trinity University professors that tested withdrawal rates on historical US returns.

========== l-sequence | Stress-Test Your Plan #2 | Sequence of returns risk (widget sequenceDemo)
Summary: Why bad years at the start of retirement hurt far more than the same years later.
Idea: Once you are withdrawing, the same returns in a different order can mean money for life or running out.

### Order only matters when money moves
Without withdrawals, the order of returns does not matter: a 20% loss then a 25% gain ends in the same place as the gain then the loss. Switch the widget to No withdrawals and both lines finish together.
Withdrawals change that. When you sell shares to pay for groceries during a crash, you sell more shares at low prices. Those shares are not there when the market recovers. A temporary drop becomes a permanent hole. That is sequence of returns risk.

### The danger zone
The first five to ten years of retirement matter most, because the portfolio is at its biggest and has the longest time left to fund. Schwab’s example: after early losses, a portfolio paying out 2% a year needed about 11.5 years to recover, while one paying out 4% needed about 28.

### How to defend yourself

- A cash and bond buffer. Schwab suggests about a year of spending (after Social Security or other income) in cash, plus two to four years in short-term high-quality bonds, so you do not have to sell stocks in a downturn. This is the bucket strategy.
- Flexible spending. Cut the extras after a bad year. See guardrails.
- Part-time income early on. Even a small income in the first years means smaller withdrawals when they hurt most.
- A lower starting rate. See the 4% rule and its limits.
- Rebalance. Selling bonds to buy stocks after a crash keeps your mix steady and spends the safer money first.
### Watch the right number
A bad year is not failure. Track your current withdrawal rate: this year’s spending divided by today’s balance. If it climbs well above where you started, it is time to act, years before the money is in danger. Stress-test your own plan in What if?.

## Worked example: Same returns, six years apart
Two retirees each start with $1M and withdraw $50,000 in year one, raising it 2% a year. Every year earns +6% except two years that lose 15%.
1. Alex gets the two bad years first. Year 1: $1,000,000 − $50,000 = $950,000, then × 0.85 = $807,500.
2. Year 2: $807,500 − $51,000 = $756,500, then × 0.85 = $643,025. A third of the money is gone in two years.
3. Blair earns +6% in those years: $1,007,000 after year 1 and about $1.03M by year 9. The same two −15% years arrive in years 10 and 11, leaving $652,386.
4. After 18 years Alex has about $24,000 left and runs out in year 19. Blair still has about $396,000 and lasts until year 25.
Result: Same returns, same spending, same average. Only the order changed, and it cost Alex six years of income.

## Mistakes
- Judging a retirement plan only by its average return.
- Having no cash or short-term bond buffer, so a crash forces you to sell stocks at low prices to pay the bills.
- Keeping spending fixed through a deep drop in the first years of retirement.
- Panicking after one bad year instead of checking your current withdrawal rate against your plan.
## Takeaways
- With no withdrawals, the order of returns does not matter. With withdrawals, it matters a lot.
- The first five to ten years of retirement carry the most risk.
- A cash and bond buffer, flexible spending and early part-time income are the main defenses.
- Track this year’s spending as a share of today’s balance to spot trouble early.
## Quiz
Q: Two retirees get exactly the same yearly returns in a different order and withdraw the same amounts. Why can one run out of money?
  Their average returns are different
* Early losses force them to sell more shares at low prices, leaving less to recover
  Taxes are always higher for the one who runs out
Why: The averages are identical. Withdrawing during early losses sells shares cheaply, so less money is left to benefit from the recovery.
## Lesson glossary
- Current withdrawal rate: This year’s spending from the portfolio divided by its balance today.

========== l-guardrails | Stress-Test Your Plan #3 | Flexible spending with guardrails (widget guardrailsSim)
Summary: Spend a bit more in good years and cut a little in bad ones so the money lasts.
Idea: A plan that can bend rarely breaks: small, rule-based cuts after bad years let you start higher and still last.

### Fixed spending is rigid
The 4% rule assumes you never change your spending, even in a crash. Real people trim travel or delay a new car when markets fall. Building that flexibility into written rules lets you start with a higher withdrawal and still sleep at night.

### Guyton-Klinger guardrails
Planners Jonathan Guyton and William Klinger published the best-known rules in 2006:

- Start around 5.2% to 5.6% with at least 65% in stocks, using all the rules.
- Capital preservation: if your current withdrawal rate rises more than 20% above where you started, cut spending by 10%.
- Prosperity: if it falls more than 20% below where you started, give yourself a 10% raise.
- The cut rule stops applying in the last 15 years of the plan.Start at 5% on $1M ($50,000) and the rails sit at 6% and 4%. If the portfolio falls to $800,000, $50,000 is now 6.25%, so spending drops to $45,000.

### Variable percentage withdrawal
The Bogleheads VPW method withdraws a percentage of whatever you have each year, and the percentage rises with age. For a 60/40 mix their table starts at about 4.1% at age 40, 4.3% at 50, 4.7% at 60 and 5.0% at 65. You can never run out early, but your spending moves with the market, so plan a floor for the essentials. Bogleheads suggests considering an income annuity around age 80.

### How much can flexibility buy?
Morningstar finds that flexible methods (guardrails, a constant percentage, endowment-style rules) can support starting rates approaching 6%, if your spending can really move. The price is variable income: the widget shows guardrails cutting deeply on a rough path.

### Make your spending bendable

- Split spending into needs (housing, food, insurance) and wants (travel, gifts, upgrades). Only wants should flex.
- Keep a few years of part-time or Barista FIRE income as a buffer for a bad start.
- Delay big one-off purchases after a down year.
- Write the rules down before you retire, so the cut is a plan, not a panic.
### One-more-year syndrome
Many people hit their number and keep working “just one more year”, then another, out of fear of a crash. Each extra year buys a little safety and costs a year of freedom. Flexible rules, a cash buffer and part-time income are cheaper insurance than years of your life. Set a date and test it in Your full FIRE plan.

## Worked example: Guardrails through a bear market
A retiree starts with $1M and follows Guyton-Klinger-style rails of ±20%.
1. Start at 5%: $50,000 in year one. Upper rail: 5% × 1.2 = 6%. Lower rail: 5% × 0.8 = 4%.
2. A bear market drops the portfolio to $800,000. $50,000 ÷ $800,000 = 6.25%, above the 6% rail.
3. Cut spending 10%: $50,000 × 0.9 = $45,000. New rate: $45,000 ÷ $800,000 = 5.6%, back inside the rails.
4. Years later the portfolio reaches $1,200,000. $45,000 ÷ $1.2M = 3.75%, below the 4% rail, so spending rises 10% to $49,500.
5. In the last 15 years of the plan the cut rule no longer applies, so spending only rises with good years.
Result: One $5,000 cut during a bad stretch kept the plan on track, instead of locking in a much lower fixed rate for life.

## Mistakes
- Setting guardrails and then ignoring them when the cut arrives.
- Starting at a guardrail rate when almost all of your spending is essential and nothing can be cut.
- Mistaking VPW’s lower spending in bad years for failure, instead of planning a floor for the essentials.
- Working “one more year” again and again out of fear, instead of building a flexible plan.
## Takeaways
- Rules that cut spending a little after bad years let you start higher and still last.
- Guyton-Klinger: cut 10% if your rate rises 20% above the start, raise 10% if it falls 20% below.
- Variable percentage never runs out early, but your income moves with the market.
- Only flexible spending should flex: cover essentials with steady income or a buffer.
- Part-time income in the early years is one of the strongest protections there is.
## Quiz
Q: You started at 5% ($50,000 of $1M) with ±20% guardrails. Your portfolio falls to $780,000. What does the rule say?
  Nothing: stay at $50,000
* Cut spending 10% to $45,000, because 6.4% is above the 6% rail
  Stop withdrawals until the market recovers
Why: $50,000 ÷ $780,000 = 6.4%, more than 20% above the 5% start, so the rule cuts spending by 10%.
## Lesson glossary
- Variable percentage withdrawal: Withdrawing a set percentage of the current balance each year, with the percentage rising as you age.
- Capital preservation rule: The Guyton-Klinger guardrail that cuts spending 10% when your withdrawal rate rises more than 20% above where it started.
- One-more-year syndrome: Putting off retirement again and again, even after you have enough, out of fear.

========== l-fire-planner | Stress-Test Your Plan #4 | Your full FIRE plan (widget firePlanner)
Summary: Every account, cost and rule in one year-by-year plan, and the age you can stop.
Idea: A FIRE plan is a yearly budget for your money: what you save, what you spend, which account pays and what tax you owe.

Your FIRE number tells you roughly how much you need. A plan tells you when you can stop and how the money comes out, year by year, until 95. The planner works in today’s dollars: every amount is in what money buys now, and returns are after inflation. That keeps the numbers readable over 50 years.

### What goes in

- You: your age and household. Couples are taxed as married filing jointly.
- Money now: each bucket separately, because each is taxed and unlocked differently: taxable brokerage (and how much of it is your own money, the cost basis), pre-tax 401(k) and IRA, Roth, HSA and cash. Plus what you save each year.
- Life after: living costs, where you will live, travel, health insurance before 65 and your Social Security estimate.
- Assumptions: return, inflation, how markets behave and how flexible your spending is.
### The order the money comes out
The planner follows the order many early retirees use:

- Cash and the taxable account first. You only pay tax on the gain part of each sale, and in 2026 long-term gains are taxed at 0% while taxable income stays under $98,900 for a married couple ($49,450 single). See the 0% bracket.
- A Roth conversion ladder at the same time. Each year it moves some pre-tax money into Roth, often just up to the standard deduction ($32,200 for a couple in 2026) so the conversion costs no tax. Five years later that money can come out tax- and penalty-free. See money out before 59½.
- Roth contributions and seasoned conversions next, then pre-tax money from 59½ (or from 55 under the Rule of 55 if you leave your job that year or later).
- Social Security from your claim age, and the HSA for medical costs from 65.
### Taxes can be tiny
Because only gains are taxed and the first $98,900 of a couple’s taxable income from long-term gains is taxed at 0% in 2026, many early retirees owe little or no federal income tax while they live on a taxable account. The planner estimates each year’s federal tax with the 2026 brackets, the standard deduction, the 0% gains band and the rules for taxing Social Security. State tax is not included: check your state, or pick one with no income tax (FIRE taxes).

### Health insurance before 65
In 2026 the extra marketplace subsidies of 2021-2025 have expired. Subsidies stop completely once household income passes 400% of the poverty line: $62,600 for one person, $84,600 for two and $128,600 for four for 2026 coverage. From 2026 you also repay any excess advance credit in full at tax time. Roth conversions and realized gains count toward that income, so the planner warns you when a year goes over the line. See health insurance before 65.

### Social Security in the plan
Use an estimate that assumes you stop working at your stop age (your online my Social Security account at ssa.gov can show this), because your benefit is based on your 35 highest years and early retirement adds zero years. With a full retirement age of 67, claiming at 62 pays 70% of the full benefit and waiting to 70 pays 124%. See Social Security when you stop early.

### Reading the result
The earliest age is the first stop age where the plan lasts past 95. An average return is a smooth line that real markets never follow, so check the Cautious and Crash at start paths in step 4 and give yourself a few years of slack. Flexible spending helps a lot (guardrails), and the next lesson breaks the plan on purpose (what if). Like any planner, this one is a simplified model, not advice; a fee-only fiduciary adviser can review a real plan.

## Worked example: A couple at 47 who pays no federal tax
A married couple stops at 47 with $2.1M invested: $1.2M taxable (they paid in $700K), $700K pre-tax and $200K Roth. They need $70,000 a year for living plus $10,000 for health insurance. Figures use 2026 rules.
1. Year-one spending is $70,000 + $10,000 = $80,000, about 3.8% of $2.1M.
2. They sell $80,000 from the taxable account. Their basis is 700 ÷ 1,200 = 58.3% of the account, so the gain part is $80,000 × (1 − 0.583) = $33,333.
3. They also convert $32,200 from pre-tax to Roth. That exactly fills the 2026 standard deduction for a couple, so the conversion is taxed at 0%.
4. Taxable income is the $33,333 gain, all inside the 0% band that runs to $98,900. Federal income tax: $0.
5. Their income for health subsidies is $32,200 + $33,333 = $65,533, about 310% of the poverty line for two ($21,150 for 2026 coverage). They pay at most 9.96% of income for the benchmark plan, about $6,527 a year, and the credit covers the rest.
6. At 52 the first $32,200 conversion has seasoned for five years and can come out tax- and penalty-free. The ladder keeps them going until pre-tax money opens at 59½.
Result: $80,000 of spending, $0 of federal income tax and a capped health premium: the order of withdrawals did that, not a bigger portfolio.

## Mistakes
- Planning with the average return only. A plan that just works on a smooth line can fail when a crash comes in year one; test the Cautious and Crash paths.
- Leaving out health insurance before 65, or assuming the bigger 2021-2025 subsidies. In 2026 the 400% cliff is back and excess advance credits must be repaid in full.
- Counting all your money as reachable. Pre-tax money is locked until 59½ (55 with the Rule of 55), so a plan can have enough in total and still run out of money you can use.
- Using a Social Security estimate that assumes you work until 67. Stopping at 45 puts zero years into your 35-year average.
## Takeaways
- Plan in today’s dollars with returns after inflation; it keeps 50 years of numbers readable.
- Spend cash and taxable money first while a Roth ladder converts pre-tax money at 0% or 10-12%.
- In 2026 a couple can have up to $98,900 of taxable income from long-term gains taxed at 0%.
- Your earliest stop age is where the plan lasts past 95. Give it slack and redo the plan every year.
## Quiz
Q: Why does the planner spend the taxable account first in early retirement?
  Money in a taxable account is never taxed
* Only the gain part of each sale is taxed, often at 0%, while pre-tax money is converted to Roth at low rates
  Pre-tax money can never be taken out before 65
Why: Selling shares you already paid tax on only taxes the gain, and in 2026 a couple’s long-term gains are taxed at 0% up to $98,900 of taxable income, leaving room for cheap Roth conversions.
## Lesson glossary
- Today's dollars: Amounts adjusted for inflation so they show what money buys now. A plan in today’s dollars uses returns after inflation.

========== l-fire-whatif | Stress-Test Your Plan #5 | What if? Stress-test the plan (widget fireWhatIf)
Summary: Crashes, inflation, big bills and a Social Security cut: see what breaks and what fixes it.
Idea: A plan that only works if nothing goes wrong is a hope. Break it on purpose, then find the cheapest fix.

Every FIRE plan rests on guesses about markets, prices, health and law. Stress-testing means changing one guess at a time to its bad version and watching what happens. If the plan only works on the average path, you know how much slack to add before you quit.

### The stresses in this tool

- A crash in year one. The worst time for a big loss is right after you stop, because you sell shares at low prices to live (sequence risk).
- A high-inflation decade. Prices rise faster than your investments for years, so your returns after inflation shrink.
- A big medical bill. One-off costs are easy to forget: cars, roofs, weddings, surgery.
- A child. USDA’s middle-income estimate is about $285,000 to raise a child to 17, before college.
- A Social Security cut. See below.
### Social Security’s trust fund
The 2026 Trustees Report projects that the retirement trust fund runs out late in 2032. After that, incoming payroll taxes would still pay about 78% of scheduled benefits (about 83% if the retirement and disability funds are combined, in 2034). Congress has acted before, so a cut is a risk to plan for, not a certainty. Planning on 0% is as wrong as planning on 100% forever.

### The cheapest fixes

- Flexible spending. Trimming travel and extras after a bad year does more than you would think (guardrails).
- A little income. Part-time or seasonal work in the first ten years covers spending exactly when sequence risk is highest. Even modest pay can replace a lot of savings (Barista FIRE).
- One more year. It works, but it is the most expensive fix: you pay for it with a year of your life. Decide your finish line in advance so “one more year” does not repeat forever.
- A cheaper place. Moving abroad or to a low-cost town shrinks the number (where your money goes furthest).
- A cash buffer. A year or two of spending in cash or bonds lets you avoid selling stocks in a crash.
### FIRE mistakes to avoid

- Forgetting health insurance before 65, and the 2026 subsidy cliff at 400% of the poverty line.
- Forgetting taxes, or paying far more than needed by drawing from the wrong account first.
- Ignoring lumpy costs: a car every 8-10 years, home repairs, a new roof, help for family.
- Underestimating home costs. Maintenance, insurance and property tax rise over time.
- Sequence risk: a 100% stock portfolio with no cash buffer the year you quit.
- Identity and boredom. Work gives structure and friends. Plan what you are retiring to, not only what you are leaving.
- A partner who is not on board with the budget, the place or the date.

## Worked example: One crash, three fixes
A plan has $1.5M invested at 50 and spends $60,000 a year, a 4% withdrawal rate. In year one stocks fall 30%.
1. They take $60,000 at the start of the year, leaving $1,440,000. The crash cuts it by 30%: $1,440,000 × 0.70 = $1,008,000.
2. Keeping the same $60,000 is now $60,000 ÷ $1,008,000 = 5.95% of the portfolio, well above a safe rate.
3. Fix 1, spend 10% less for a few years: $54,000 ÷ $1,008,000 = 5.36%. Better, but still high.
4. Fix 2, earn $20,000 a year part-time: the portfolio only pays $40,000, or $40,000 ÷ $1,008,000 = 3.97%.
5. Fix 3, both: $54,000 − $20,000 = $34,000, or $34,000 ÷ $1,008,000 = 3.37%.
Result: A little income does more than a 10% spending cut, and stacking both brings the withdrawal rate back under 3.5%.

## Mistakes
- Testing only the average case, then quitting on a plan with no slack.
- Treating one more year as free. It costs a year of your life; set a finish line before you start.
- Assuming Social Security pays 100% forever, or nothing at all. The projected gap is about a fifth.
- Keeping no cash buffer, so a crash forces you to sell stocks near the bottom.
## Takeaways
- Stress-test the first ten years of retirement hardest: that is where crashes and inflation do the most damage.
- Flexible spending and a little income are usually the cheapest fixes.
- Plan for a Social Security cut of about a fifth as a stress case, not as the base case.
- Write down the fix in advance, so a bad year triggers a plan instead of panic.
## Quiz
Q: In the example, which single change lowered the withdrawal rate most after the crash?
  Spending 10% less
* Earning $20,000 a year part-time
  Waiting for the market to recover before deciding
Why: Part-time pay cut the portfolio’s draw from $60,000 to $40,000 (3.97%), while a 10% spending cut only reached 5.36%.
