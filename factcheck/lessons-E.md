

========== l-macro-intro | The Fed & Interest Rates #1 | How the economy moves stocks (widget macroMap)
Summary: The chain from jobs, prices and interest rates to profits and stock prices.
Idea: Stock prices follow two things: expected profits and interest rates. The economy moves both.

### Two numbers drive every stock
A share is a claim on a company’s future profits. What it is worth today depends on how big those profits will be and how much the future is worth today, which is set by interest rates. The economy pushes on both. Busy shoppers and full employment lift profits. Inflation pushes rates up, and higher rates shrink what far-off profits are worth now.

### The dials to watch

- Growth: GDP, retail sales and business surveys (PMI).

- Jobs: the monthly jobs report and weekly jobless claims (the jobs lesson).

- Prices: CPI and PCE inflation (the inflation lesson).

- Rates: the Fed’s target rate and Treasury yields (the Fed, yields).

- Commodities and the dollar: oil, metals and the dollar’s value (oil, the dollar).


### Two levers: monetary and fiscal policy
Monetary policy is the Federal Reserve: short-term interest rates and its bond holdings. Fiscal policy is Congress and the President: taxes, spending and deficits. They can push in the same direction, as in 2020 when both poured money into the economy, or against each other, as when the government spends heavily while the Fed tries to cool inflation.

### Markets trade surprises, not news
Before every report, economists publish a forecast called the consensus. Prices already reflect it. If inflation comes in exactly as expected, little happens. The gap between actual and expected is what moves markets, often within seconds of 8:30 am Eastern time.
That is why good news can be bad news. A very strong jobs report can push stocks down if it means the Fed will keep rates higher for longer.

### What tends to move together

- Risk-on days: stocks, copper and riskier bonds rise together.

- Risk-off days: money runs to Treasuries, the dollar and gold.

- Inflation scares are different: stocks and bonds can fall at the same time, as they did in 2022. That is when a stock and bond mix protects you least.


### How to use this
As a trader: know the calendar. Cut size or stay flat into big 8:30 am releases, and let the first few minutes settle before you trade. As an investor: macro explains why markets move, but it rarely tells you when. Keep your mix, rebalance on a schedule and avoid rebuilding a portfolio around one month’s data.

## Worked example: A hotter-than-expected inflation morning
Illustrative numbers, to show how the chain plays out in one morning.
1. Economists expect core CPI to rise 0.3% for the month. At 8:30 am ET the report shows 0.5%.
2. Within a minute the 2-year Treasury yield jumps from 4.70% to 4.85% as traders price in fewer rate cuts.
3. Stock index futures fall 1.2%. A fast-growing tech stock drops 3% before the open while a large bank barely moves.
4. A day trader whose plan says "no trades in the first 15 minutes on CPI day" waits, then trades a clean retest of the premarket low with normal size.
5. A long-term investor with 70% stocks and 30% bonds does nothing: the mix is still inside the 5-point band that would trigger a rebalance.
Result: Same news, two right answers: the trader manages the event risk, the investor ignores it.

## Mistakes
- Reacting to the headline number instead of the surprise against the consensus forecast.
- Assuming good economic news is always good for stocks. If it means higher rates for longer, stocks can fall.
- Holding a large or leveraged position into an 8:30 am CPI or jobs release without a plan.
- Rebuilding a long-term portfolio around one month of data or one scary headline.
## Takeaways
- Stock prices follow expected profits and interest rates; the economy moves both.
- Monetary policy is the Fed; fiscal policy is taxes and spending.
- Markets move on surprises against the consensus, not on the news itself.
- Inflation scares can hit stocks and bonds at the same time.
- Traders plan around release times; investors keep their mix and rebalance.
## Quiz
Q: Monthly CPI comes in at 0.3%, exactly what economists expected. What usually happens to stocks?
  They jump, because inflation is positive
* Little, because the number was already expected
  They crash, because any inflation is bad
Why: Prices already reflect the consensus forecast. Markets move on the surprise, the gap between actual and expected.

========== l-fed | The Fed & Interest Rates #2 | How the Federal Reserve works (widget fedSim)
Summary: Who sets interest rates, how a meeting works and how a decision reaches your wallet.
Idea: Twelve people vote eight times a year on one short-term rate, and that rate ripples into every loan and stock price.

### What the Fed is
The Federal Reserve is the US central bank. It has three parts:

- The Board of Governors in Washington: seven governors nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate.

- Twelve regional Reserve Banks, from Boston to San Francisco, each with its own president.

- The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which sets interest rates. It has 12 voters: the 7 governors, the president of the New York Fed and 4 of the other 11 regional presidents, who rotate each year. The non-voting presidents still join the debate.

In October 2026 the Board lists Kevin Warsh as Chairman. Jerome Powell, the previous chair, remains a governor.

### The dual mandate
Congress gave the Fed two goals: maximum employment and stable prices. The Fed defines stable prices as 2% inflation a year, measured by the PCE price index (see inflation). When the two goals pull apart, as in stagflation, the Fed has to choose which miss to fight first.

### How a meeting works

- Eight scheduled meetings a year, most over two days.

- 2:00 pm ET: the statement with the decision and the vote.

- 2:30 pm ET: the chair’s press conference, where traders listen for hints about the next move.

- Four meetings a year (March, June, September and December) also publish the Summary of Economic Projections with the dot plot: each official’s guess for rates at future year ends.

- Minutes come out three weeks later and can move markets again.

The remaining 2026 meetings are October 27–28 and December 8–9 (with projections). The full list is on the calendar.

### The tools

- The target range for the federal funds rate, the overnight rate banks charge each other. It anchors every short-term rate.

- Interest on reserve balances and the overnight reverse repo rate, which keep market rates inside that range.

- The discount window, emergency loans to banks.

- The balance sheet: buying bonds (quantitative easing) pushes long-term rates down; letting them run off (quantitative tightening) does the reverse.

- Forward guidance: words about the future path, which move markets before any action.


### From the Fed to your wallet

- Credit cards and HELOCs: tied to the prime rate, which banks set at the top of the Fed’s range plus 3 points, so they move within a billing cycle or two.

- Savings and money market funds: drift the same way, often more slowly.

- Mortgages: follow the 10-year Treasury yield, which the market sets (see yields). Mortgage rates can even rise after a Fed cut.

- Stocks: react to the expected path of rates more than to any single move.


### Where things stand in October 2026
On September 16, 2026 the FOMC raised the target range by 0.25 points to 3.75% to 4.00%, by a 12–0 vote, saying inflation "remains elevated". The latest readings: CPI inflation 3.7% over the year to September (core CPI 2.6%), core PCE inflation 3.0% in August, and unemployment 4.2% in September with job growth of only 29,000. The next decision is October 28 at 2:00 pm ET.

## Worked example: From a Fed hike to your credit card
The September 16, 2026 hike, followed through one household. The card margin and savings rate are illustrative.
1. The FOMC raises the target range by 0.25 points, from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00%.
2. Banks’ prime rate follows the top of the range plus 3 points: from 6.75% to 7.00%.
3. A variable card APR set at prime + 15 points moves from 21.75% to 22.00%.
4. On a $5,000 balance carried for a year, that adds about $12.50 of interest ($5,000 × 0.25%).
5. A savings account paying 4.00% may drift toward 4.25% if the bank passes the hike on: about $25 more a year on $10,000.
Result: Small per dollar, but it reaches millions of loans and accounts at once. That is how the Fed cools or warms the economy.

## Mistakes
- Thinking the Fed sets mortgage rates. They follow the 10-year Treasury yield, which the market sets.
- Watching only the decision and missing the statement, the dot plot and the press conference, which often move markets more.
- Treating the dot plot as a promise. It is a forecast that changes with the data.
- Betting heavily on rate cuts while the Fed is still saying inflation is too high.
## Takeaways
- The FOMC has 12 voters: 7 governors, the New York Fed president and 4 rotating presidents.
- Its goals are maximum employment and 2% inflation measured by PCE.
- Eight meetings a year; statement at 2:00 pm ET, press conference at 2:30.
- Card and HELOC rates follow the Fed quickly; mortgages follow the 10-year yield.
- In September 2026 the Fed hiked to 3.75–4.00% because inflation was still above goal.
## Quiz
Q: Who votes on interest rates at an FOMC meeting?

* The 7 governors, the New York Fed president and 4 rotating regional presidents
  Only the Fed chair
Why: Twelve members vote. All 12 regional presidents attend and speak, but only New York plus four others vote in a given year.

========== l-rates | The Fed & Interest Rates #3 | Rates, yields and the yield curve (widget rateValuation)
Summary: Why the 10-year yield matters, how rates move stocks and bonds, and what the curve says.
Idea: Interest rates are the price of time. When they rise, money in the future is worth less today, so stocks and bonds fall.

### Short rates and long rates
The Fed sets only the overnight rate. Everything longer is set by trading in Treasury bonds, based on where investors think inflation, growth and the Fed are heading, plus extra pay for tying money up longer. The 10-year Treasury yield is the benchmark that mortgages and stock valuations watch. On October 20, 2026 the curve read: 3-month 4.26%, 2-year 4.79%, 10-year 5.12%, 30-year 5.42%.

### Why rates move stock prices
A stock is worth its future profits discounted back to today. A higher rate means a bigger discount. Companies whose profits are mostly far in the future, the fast growers, are hit hardest, a bit like long-term bonds. That is why tech stocks fell hardest when yields jumped in 2022. Steady companies paying profits now are hit less.

### Bonds: the seesaw
When yields rise, existing bonds with lower coupons are worth less, so their prices fall. Duration tells you by how much: a bond fund with a duration of 6 loses about 6% if yields rise 1 point. Long bonds swing the most. In 2022 a US total bond market index fund lost about 13% and an S&P 500 index fund about 18%: inflation hit both at once. More on bonds in bonds and interest rates.

### Real yields
A real yield is the yield minus expected inflation. It is the true reward for lending. Rising real yields tend to pressure growth stocks and gold, which pays no interest. Treasury inflation-protected bonds (TIPS) show the real yield directly.

### The yield curve

- Normal: long yields above short ones. The usual shape in an expansion. In October 2026 the 10-year sat about 0.33 points above the 2-year.

- Flat: little extra pay for lending longer, common after the Fed has hiked.

- Inverted: short yields above long ones. Investors expect cuts, usually because they expect growth to weaken. Inversions have come before most US recessions, but with long and uneven delays, and the most recent long inversion was not followed by a quick recession.


### Mortgage rates
Mortgage rates track the 10-year yield plus a spread for risk and costs. In mid-October 2026 the average 30-year fixed rate was 7.40% while the 10-year yielded about 5.12%, a spread of about 2.3 points. Use the Mortgage tab, then the mortgage lesson.

## Worked example: One point higher, two companies
The Stocks tab model: required return = 10-year yield + 4 points of extra return for owning stocks.
1. Start with the 10-year yield at 5.12%. The steady company (3% growth, pays out 70%) is worth about 11.8 times earnings.
2. The fast-grower (14% growth for ten years, then 3.5%) is worth about 22.5 times earnings.
3. Yields rise 1 point to 6.12%. The steady company’s fair P/E falls to about 10.1, a drop of about 14%.
4. The fast-grower’s falls to about 17.9, a drop of about 21%, because more of its value is far in the future.
5. On the Bonds tab, the same 1-point rise cuts a 30-year bond by about 13.6% and a 2-year by only about 1.8%.
Result: Long-dated assets, whether bonds or growth stocks, feel rate moves the most.

## Mistakes
- Thinking the Fed controls long-term rates. It sets the overnight rate; markets set the rest.
- Calling long-term bonds safe without checking duration. A 30-year bond can lose more than 10% when yields jump.
- Treating an inverted curve as a signal to sell everything. The delay before past recessions has been long and uneven.
- Forgetting that stocks and bonds can fall together when inflation is the problem.
## Takeaways
- The 10-year Treasury yield is the benchmark for mortgages and stock valuations.
- Higher rates lower what future profits are worth; growth stocks feel it most.
- Bond prices move opposite to yields; duration says by how much.
- Normal, flat and inverted curves each tell a story about expected growth and Fed moves.
- Mortgage rates follow the 10-year plus a spread, about 2.3 points in October 2026.
## Quiz
Q: The 10-year yield jumps from 4% to 5%. Which usually falls the most?
  A steady utility with slow, reliable profits
* A fast-growing company whose profits are mostly years away
  Cash in a money market fund
Why: Most of a fast-grower’s value is in distant profits, which a higher discount rate shrinks the most. Money market funds actually earn more when rates rise.

========== l-inflation | Economic Data & the Calendar #1 | Inflation: CPI, PCE and PPI (widget inflationCalc)
Summary: How inflation is measured, why the Fed aims for 2% and how hot or cool reports move markets.
Idea: Inflation is how fast prices rise. Hot readings push interest rates and bond yields up, which usually pulls stock prices down.

Inflation is the speed at which prices rise. At 3% a year, something that costs $100 today costs about $134 in ten years. The same dollars buy less, so money that earns less than inflation is quietly shrinking.

### The three reports traders watch

- CPI (Consumer Price Index) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics: prices consumers pay, out around the middle of each month at 8:30 am Eastern (for example October 14 and November 10, 2026). It is the headline number on the news.

- PCE (personal consumption expenditures price index) from the Bureau of Economic Analysis: a wider measure of spending, out near the end of the month at 8:30 am with the personal income report. This is the one the Federal Reserve uses for its 2% goal. Since 2000, CPI has run about half a point above PCE on average, mostly because the two weigh housing differently.

- PPI (Producer Price Index): prices businesses receive. Rising costs here can show up in consumer prices a few months later.


### Headline, core and the shelter effect
Headline inflation includes everything. Core leaves out food and energy, which jump around with weather, wars and oil supply, so core shows the slower trend underneath. Housing costs (shelter) are about 36% of CPI but closer to 15% of PCE, which is one reason the two measures often differ. Shelter also moves slowly, because it tracks rents people already pay, not just new leases.

### How inflation moves markets

- Hotter than expected: traders expect higher interest rates for longer. Bond yields rise, the dollar usually firms, and stocks tend to fall, growth stocks most, because their profits sit far in the future and are worth less when rates are high.

- Cooler than expected: the opposite. Rate cuts look closer, yields fall and stocks often rally.

- What matters is the surprise: markets price in the forecast before the report. A 3% reading can lift stocks if 3.3% was expected. See trading data-release days.


### Lessons from the past
In the 1970s inflation climbed into double digits while growth stalled, a mix called stagflation. CPI inflation peaked near 15% in 1980, and Fed chair Paul Volcker pushed short-term rates close to 20% to break it, causing a deep recession. In June 2022 CPI inflation reached 9.1%, the highest in about 40 years. The Fed raised rates at the fastest pace in decades, and both stocks and bonds fell that year: an S&P 500 index fund lost about 18%. On September 13, 2022 alone, a hotter-than-expected CPI report knocked the S&P 500 down 4.3%.

### Where inflation stands now
CPI rose 3.7% over the year to September 2026, a touch above the 3.6% forecast, while core CPI was 2.6%. The gap is energy: energy prices were up 20.4% on the year. PCE inflation for August was 3.4%, with core PCE at 3.0%, unusually above core CPI after a change in how the government measures some services. Producer prices (PPI) were up 6.3%, a warning that costs are still flowing through. After the CPI report, traders raised the odds of another Fed rate hike. The next CPI comes out on November 10, 2026 at 8:30 am ET; every date is on the calendar.
Data can have gaps. During the 43-day government shutdown in late 2025, the October 2025 CPI was never published, which is why some year-over-year comparisons for that month are missing.

### What it means for your money

- Keep cash in places that pay close to inflation, such as high-yield savings, Treasury bills or a money market fund (see where to park cash).

- Over decades, owning businesses through index funds has beaten inflation, with big swings along the way.

- Long-term bonds suffer most when inflation surprises upward; see rates and the yield curve.

## Worked example: The same $50,000, two homes for it
Inflation averages 3.7% a year (the CPI rate for September 2026). Taxes are ignored.
1. Maya leaves $50,000 in a savings account paying 0.6%. After 10 years the balance reads $53,082.
2. Prices rose 1.037 to the 10th power, or 43.8% in total, so each of her dollars buys less.
3. In today’s dollars her account is worth $53,082 ÷ 1.438 = $36,912. She lost about $13,100 of buying power while the balance went up.
4. Her brother puts the same $50,000 in Treasury bills paying 4.2%. After 10 years he has $75,448, or $52,464 in today’s dollars.
5. Both took almost no risk, but one choice kept up with inflation and the other did not.
Result: Same money, same safety, about $15,600 apart in buying power after ten years.

## Mistakes
- Comparing your savings rate with zero instead of with inflation. A 0.6% account loses ground when prices rise 3.7%.
- Reading only the headline number. Energy can swing it by a point or more; core shows the trend the Fed cares about.
- Treating CPI and PCE as the same number. The Fed’s 2% goal is for PCE, which usually runs a little below CPI.
- Trading the first seconds after 8:30 am. Spreads widen and the first move often reverses once traders read the details.
## Takeaways
- Inflation is the speed prices rise; money earning less than inflation loses buying power.
- CPI is the headline consumer measure, PCE is the Fed’s 2% target, PPI shows business costs.
- Core inflation leaves out food and energy to show the underlying trend.
- Hotter-than-expected inflation usually means higher yields and lower stock prices, especially for growth stocks.
- Markets react to the surprise against the forecast, not to the number alone.
## Quiz
Q: Which inflation measure does the Federal Reserve use for its 2% goal?
  CPI
* PCE
  PPI
Why: The Fed’s 2% target is set in terms of the PCE price index, which covers a wider range of spending than CPI.
## Lesson glossary
- Shelter: Housing costs in CPI: rent and the estimated rent owners would pay. About a third of the index.

========== l-jobs | Economic Data & the Calendar #2 | The jobs report and jobless claims (widget jobsReport)
Summary: Payrolls, unemployment, wages and weekly claims, and why good news can be bad for stocks.
Idea: The jobs report shows how fast the economy is hiring. Markets react to the surprise against the forecast, filtered through what the Fed is worried about.

The Employment Situation report from the Bureau of Labor Statistics comes out at 8:30 am Eastern, usually on the first Friday of the month (the next ones are November 6 and December 4, 2026). Traders call it jobs Friday. It packs several numbers into one release.

### What is in the report

- Nonfarm payrolls: how many jobs employers added or cut last month, from a survey of businesses.

- Unemployment rate: the share of people looking for work who cannot find it, from a separate survey of households. The two surveys can disagree in any one month.

- Average hourly earnings: wage growth. Fast wage gains can feed inflation, especially in services.

- Revisions: the two previous months are updated. A big downward revision can matter as much as the new number.


### Why good news can be bad news
When the Fed is fighting inflation, a hot jobs report means rates stay high for longer. Bond yields jump and stocks can fall on “good” news. When the Fed is more worried about a weakening job market, the same strong report is simply good news. Always ask which problem the Fed is focused on; the Fed lesson explains how it decides.

### The early warnings: weekly claims and JOLTS

- Initial jobless claims come out every Thursday at 8:30 am. They count new filings for unemployment benefits, so they turn before the monthly report does. Watch the 4-week average to smooth out holidays and storms.

- JOLTS (job openings) comes out at 10:00 am about a month after the month it covers, usually on a Tuesday. Openings per unemployed person show how tight the job market is.

- A steady rise in claims together with a rising unemployment rate is a classic recession warning; see the business cycle.


### Where jobs stand now
Employers added only 29,000 jobs in September 2026, and July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000. Yet the unemployment rate held at 4.2%, wages grew 3.0% over the year and weekly jobless claims were low, near 199,000 in mid-October. Job openings were 7.1 million in August. Hiring is slow, but layoffs are not rising: a soft job market, not a breaking one. The widget starts from these levels.
Gaps happen: the October 2025 jobs report was cancelled during the 43-day government shutdown, and the household survey for that month was never collected.

### How to use it as a trader

- Know the forecast before the release. The surprise, including revisions, drives the first move.

- Size down or stay flat into 8:30 am; see trading data-release days.

- Watch the 2-year Treasury yield. It reacts fastest to what the report means for the Fed.

## Worked example: A soft jobs Friday
Made-up numbers. The Fed has said it is watching the job market closely.
1. Economists expect +110,000 jobs. The report shows +45,000.
2. The two previous months are revised down by a combined 40,000, so the total surprise is 45,000 − 110,000 − 40,000 = −105,000 jobs.
3. The unemployment rate rises from 4.3% to 4.5%, and wage growth slows to 3.4% a year.
4. Traders read this as a cooling job market. The 2-year yield falls and the odds of a rate cut jump within minutes.
5. With the Fed worried about jobs, stocks often fall too, because a weak report raises recession fears. In the widget, pick Soft and the weak-job-market worry to see it.
Result: The headline miss was 65,000 jobs, but revisions made the real surprise about 105,000.

## Mistakes
- Ignoring revisions. A good headline can hide big downward changes to earlier months.
- Reading payrolls and the unemployment rate as one survey. They come from different surveys and can point different ways.
- Assuming a strong report is always good for stocks. It depends on whether the Fed is fighting inflation or a slowdown.
- Treating one week of jobless claims as a trend. Use the 4-week average.
## Takeaways
- Jobs reports usually land on a Friday at 8:30 am ET: payrolls, unemployment rate, wages and revisions.
- The market moves on the surprise against the forecast, including revisions.
- When the Fed fights inflation, strong jobs can push yields up and stocks down.
- Weekly claims (Thursdays) and JOLTS are earlier and slower clues about the job market.
## Quiz
Q: The Fed is fighting inflation and payrolls beat the forecast by 150,000. What often happens first?
  Bond yields fall and stocks jump
* Bond yields rise and stocks dip
  Nothing, jobs data does not move markets
Why: A strong report makes higher rates for longer more likely, so yields rise and stocks often dip at first.
## Lesson glossary
- Average hourly earnings: Wage growth from the jobs report. Watched for signs of wage-driven inflation.
- Revision: An update to an earlier month’s number when more survey answers come in.

========== l-pmi | Economic Data & the Calendar #3 | PMI, GDP and retail sales (widget pmiGauge)
Summary: The 50 line in PMI surveys, how GDP is built and what consumer spending reports say about stocks.
Idea: PMI surveys are the fastest read on business: above 50 means growing. GDP is the slower, fuller picture, and consumers drive most of it.

A PMI (purchasing managers’ index) asks purchasing managers whether things got better, worse or stayed the same this month. The answers are turned into an index where 50 means no change: above 50, more firms say growing; below 50, more say shrinking.

### The ISM reports

- ISM Manufacturing PMI: out at 10:00 am Eastern on the first business day of the month. The headline is the average of five parts: new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries and inventories.

- ISM Services PMI: out at 10:00 am on the third business day. Four parts: business activity, new orders, employment and supplier deliveries. Services are most of the US economy.

- Prices paid is published with each report but is not part of the headline. A jump there often comes before higher inflation readings.

- S&P Global also publishes US PMIs at 9:45 am, with an early “flash” estimate around the third or fourth week of the month (October 23, 2026 for October).


### Reading the parts

- New orders are future work, so they tend to lead. New orders above inventories is a sign production will pick up.

- Employment gives a hint before the jobs report.

- One month below 50 is common. A run of readings in the low or mid-40s has often come with recessions.


### GDP: the full picture, later
GDP (gross domestic product) adds up everything the economy produces. The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes an advance estimate about a month after each quarter, then two revisions. US growth is quoted at an annual rate: a quarter that grows about 0.5% is reported as about 2%. Consumer spending is the biggest part, roughly two thirds. Inventories and net exports can swing the headline a lot from quarter to quarter, so traders watch consumer spending plus business investment for the real demand underneath.

### Retail sales and confidence
The Census Bureau’s advance retail sales report comes out around the middle of the month at 8:30 am. It is an early read on consumers. Retail sales are in dollars, so part of any rise can just be higher prices; the “control group” (which leaves out cars, gas, building materials and restaurants) is the part that feeds GDP. Consumer confidence surveys from the University of Michigan (a preliminary reading mid-month and a final one late in the month) and the Conference Board show how people feel, which does not always match how they spend.

### Where things stand now
In September 2026 the ISM Manufacturing PMI was 54.5 and the Services PMI 54.9: both comfortably above 50. But prices paid hit 77.9 in manufacturing and about 74 in services, a sign that cost pressure is building. GDP grew at a 2.2% annual rate in the second quarter; the third-quarter advance estimate is due October 29, 2026. Retail sales jumped 0.8% in September, though much of that was gas prices; the control group rose only 0.1%. Meanwhile the University of Michigan sentiment index fell to 46.3 in its October preliminary reading, among the lowest in its history. Busy businesses, gloomy households: a good reminder that each report shows only part of the picture.

### Which stocks react

- Strong manufacturing data tends to lift industrials, materials, energy and transport.

- Strong consumer data helps retailers, travel and banks.

- Weak data sends money toward defensives such as utilities, staples and health care. See the business cycle.

## Worked example: Reading an ISM morning
Made-up numbers for one manufacturing report.
1. The parts come in at new orders 51.2, production 49.0, employment 46.8, supplier deliveries 50.6 and inventories 45.4.
2. The headline is their average: (51.2 + 49.0 + 46.8 + 50.6 + 45.4) ÷ 5 = 48.6, below 50.
3. But new orders minus inventories is 51.2 − 45.4 = +5.8. Orders are arriving faster than stock is piling up, an early sign of a turn.
4. Prices paid jumps to 62. That warns that costs are rising and could show up in inflation later.
5. Industrial stocks may rise on the orders while bond yields tick up on the prices. One report, two stories.
Result: A sub-50 headline with strong new orders is often the first sign a manufacturing slump is ending.

## Mistakes
- Treating a PMI of 49 as a recession. Small dips below 50 are common; direction and new orders matter more.
- Watching only manufacturing. Services are the bigger part of the US economy.
- Forgetting that US GDP is annualized. A 2% headline is about 0.5% growth in that quarter.
- Reading retail sales without inflation in mind. Sales up 3% with prices up 3% means about the same amount of stuff.
## Takeaways
- PMI above 50 means growing, below 50 means shrinking; new orders lead.
- ISM Manufacturing comes out on the first business day, ISM Services on the third, both at 10:00 am ET.
- GDP is quoted at an annual rate and revised twice; consumers are about two thirds of it.
- Strong data helps cyclical stocks, weak data favors defensives.
## Quiz
Q: An ISM Manufacturing PMI of 48 means…
  Factory output fell 2%
* More firms reported shrinking than growing
  The economy is officially in recession
Why: PMI is a survey index: below 50 means more firms reported declines than gains. It is not a percent change and not a recession call.
## Lesson glossary
- Prices paid: The PMI part that tracks what firms pay for inputs. An early inflation clue.
- Annual rate: Quarterly growth scaled up to a full year, the way US GDP is reported.

========== l-data-day | Economic Data & the Calendar #4 | Trading data-release days (widget dataDayMove)
Summary: Why CPI, jobs and Fed days move markets, and how to size around the release.
Idea: Markets move on the surprise, not the number. Options show how big a move is priced in; size so a gap cannot break your plan.

### The events that move everything
A handful of scheduled reports can move the whole market in seconds. They come out on dates set long in advance, so you can plan around them (see the calendar):

- CPI inflation: 8:30 am ET, usually around the middle of the month (the September 2026 report came out on Wednesday, October 14). See inflation.

- The jobs report: 8:30 am ET, usually on the first Friday of the month (the September report came out on Friday, October 2). See the jobs report.

- Fed decisions: eight scheduled meetings a year, with the statement at 2:00 pm ET on the second day and the chair’s press conference at 2:30 pm. The next decisions are due October 28 and December 9, 2026. See how the Fed works.

- Also watched: PCE inflation, PPI, retail sales, the ISM PMIs (10:00 am ET), GDP, and results from the largest companies.


### The surprise is what moves prices
Before each report, economists publish forecasts. Their average is the consensus. Traders position for it ahead of time, so the consensus is already in the price. What moves markets is the surprise: the gap between the actual number and the consensus, plus details such as revisions to earlier months, the core number without food and energy, or wage growth.
The same surprise can push stocks either way. When the market fears inflation, a strong jobs report lifts yields because the Fed may keep rates high, and stocks can fall: good news is bad news. When the market fears a slowdown, the same report eases recession worries and stocks rise.

### How big a move is priced in
The options market tells you. Add the prices of the at-the-money call and put that expire just after the event: that pair is a straddle. Its price is roughly the size of the move traders expect. If an index fund trades at $500 and the straddle costs $5, the market is pricing about ±$5, or 1%. A smaller move happens a bit more than half the time; a move twice as big is rarer, but it happens. For the S&P 500, options have priced roughly 0.6% to 1% for recent CPI days and about 1.2% for a jobs report in May 2024. Real moves can be far bigger: on September 13, 2022, a hot CPI report sent the S&P 500 down 4.3% in one day. More in volatility and the expected move.

### What reacts first

- The 2-year Treasury yield: the most sensitive to what the Fed will do next. See rates and yields.

- The dollar: higher US yields tend to lift it. See the dollar.

- Rate-sensitive stocks: fast-growing tech, small companies, homebuilders and real estate move most on rate surprises.

- Gold: often dips when inflation-adjusted yields jump. See gold and silver.


### A release-morning routine

- Check the calendar the night before and write down the time.

- Do not carry a full-size position with a tight stop into 8:30 am data. A stop becomes a market order: if the price gaps past it, you are filled at the gap.

- Size for a bad open, not just your stop. A move of twice the priced-in move should still fit inside your normal risk (see position size and the 1% rule), or wait until after the release.

- Let the first 15 minutes finish, then trade levels with confirmation (see timeframes and breakouts). The first reaction often reverses as people read the details.

- On Fed days the 2:30 pm press conference can undo the 2:00 pm move.

- Buying options just before an event? Implied volatility drops right after the news (IV crush), so you need a bigger move than priced in to profit.

## Worked example: Sizing a trade on CPI morning
You trade a $25,000 account and risk 1% ($250) per trade. CPI comes out at 8:30 am ET.
1. An index fund trades at $500 (example price). The at-the-money straddle costs $5.00, so the market is pricing about ±$5, or 1%.
2. Your plan: buy with a stop 0.4% ($2) below. Normal sizing gives $250 ÷ $2 = 125 shares.
3. Your stop is well inside the priced-in move. In a simple bell-curve model there is about a 37% chance the open jumps straight past it.
4. If the print is hot and the fund opens twice the priced-in move lower ($10 a share), 125 shares lose $1,250: 5R instead of 1R.
5. To keep even that bad open within $250, hold $250 ÷ $10 = 25 shares, or wait until after 9:45 am and trade the new levels.
Result: Same idea, sized for the data: a bad open now costs 1R, not 5R.

## Mistakes
- Holding a full-size position with a tight stop through 8:30 am data, as if the stop guaranteed the exit price.
- Trading the headline in the first seconds, when spreads are wide and the first move often reverses.
- Buying options right before the event and losing money even when the direction was right, because volatility drops after the news.
- Assuming hot data is always bad for stocks, or strong jobs always good. The reaction depends on what the market fears that month.
## Takeaways
- Markets react to the surprise versus the consensus, not the raw number.
- The at-the-money straddle shows roughly how big a move is priced in.
- Size so an open of twice the priced-in move still fits your normal risk, or wait until after the release.
- Watch the 2-year yield and the dollar for the market’s read of the data.
- Let the first 15 minutes finish; on Fed days, wait for the press conference.
## Quiz
Q: An index fund trades at $400. Just before CPI, the at-the-money straddle costs $6. Roughly what move is priced in?
* About ±$6, or 1.5%
  About ±$12, or 3%
  About ±$3, or 0.75%
Why: The straddle’s price is roughly the size of the move the options market expects: $6 on $400 is 1.5%.
## Lesson glossary
- Straddle: Buying (or selling) a call and a put with the same strike and expiry. Its price shows how big a move the market expects.
- Hawkish and dovish: Hawkish means leaning toward higher rates to fight inflation; dovish means leaning toward lower rates to support jobs.

========== l-earnings-season | Economic Data & the Calendar #5 | Earnings season and the economy (widget earningsSeason)
Summary: When companies report, what really moves the stock, and how the economy shows up in results.
Idea: Four times a year companies report. The stock reacts to the surprise and the outlook, and the outlook carries the economy inside it.

### The rhythm of a season
Most US companies report within a few weeks after each quarter ends. The biggest banks usually open the season about two weeks after the quarter closes; the busiest weeks follow, including several of the largest tech companies; big retailers come last because their quarters end a month later. JPMorgan Chase, which traditionally opens the season, has set its Q4 2026 report for Thursday, January 14, 2027, and its 2027 reports for April 13, July 13 and October 12. Each company posts its date on its investor-relations page, and the calendar marks the season openings.

### What a report contains

- EPS (earnings per share) against the analysts’ consensus estimate.

- Revenue against its estimate. A beat built on sales is worth more than one built on cost cuts.

- Guidance: the company’s forecast for the next quarter or year. This is the future, and prices are built on the future.

- Margins and commentary: costs, demand, hiring and investment plans, mostly heard on the conference call.

Back to basics in earnings and the three statements.

### Why a beat is normal
Early in the Q3 2026 season, 84% of S&P 500 companies that had reported beat their EPS estimates, above the 5-year average of 78% and the 10-year average of 77% (FactSet, October 16, 2026). But the average beat was only 2.6%, and only 67% beat on revenue. Companies tend to guide cautiously and analysts trim estimates before reports, so a small beat is the baseline, not good news. What moves the stock is the size of the beat, whether revenue beat too, and whether guidance went up or down, compared with what the stock’s run-up already assumed.

### Where the economy shows up

- Interest rates: borrowing costs for companies, and lending income for banks. See rates.

- The dollar: a strong dollar shrinks foreign sales when they are converted back, a headwind for multinationals. See the dollar.

- The consumer: retailers, restaurants and card networks describe spending in real time.

- Costs: oil, metals, wages and tariffs squeeze or lift margins. See oil, copper and tariffs.

- The banks go first and act as a check-up on the economy: loan demand, defaults and consumer credit.


### The index view
Over long periods stock prices follow earnings. FactSet put blended growth for Q3 2026 at about 29% as of October 16, led by energy and semiconductors, with about 33% expected for all of 2026. The S&P 500 traded at about 19 times expected earnings for the next 12 months, close to its 10-year average of 19.1, down from 22 at the end of 2025 as bond yields rose. When stocks are priced at a high multiple of earnings, there is less room for disappointment.

### Trading and investing through reports

- Options price the expected move. Holding through a report means accepting a gap of that size or more, so keep the position small enough (see data-release days).

- After the report, implied volatility drops (IV crush). Option buyers need a move bigger than priced in.

- Big surprises have tended to keep drifting in the same direction for days or weeks after the report, a pattern researchers call post-earnings drift. It is a tendency, not a promise.

- Investors: judge the trend across several quarters (revenue, margins, guidance) rather than one headline.

## Worked example: A beat that still fell
DEMO is a made-up company. The numbers follow the widget’s first scenario.
1. DEMO runs up 12% in the weeks before its report, to $150. Options price a move of about ±7%.
2. After the close it reports EPS of $1.10 against a $1.00 estimate, a 10% beat. Revenue matches the estimate.
3. It lowers its outlook for next quarter below what analysts expected. In the model, the guidance cut is worth −7 points and the high expectations −3.6 points; the EPS beat adds only +2.5.
4. The stock opens about 8.1% lower, near $137.85: a bigger drop than the ±7% priced in.
5. An investor holding 100 shares is down about $1,215 overnight. A trader who bought calls for the beat loses on the price and on the drop in volatility.
Result: The beat was about the past; the guidance cut changed the future, and the run-up meant good news was already in the price.

## Mistakes
- Reading “beat” in a headline and buying, without checking revenue and guidance.
- Holding a large position through a report as if a stop protects you. The stock can open far past it.
- Ignoring how far the stock ran before the report. A big run-up raises the bar.
- Judging a company on one quarter instead of the trend in revenue, margins and guidance.
## Takeaways
- Seasons open about two weeks after each quarter ends, with the big banks; retailers report last.
- Most companies beat estimates, so the size of the beat, revenue and guidance matter more than the word “beat”.
- Guidance carries the economy inside it: rates, the dollar, consumer demand and costs.
- Options show the move priced in; size for a gap and remember IV crush.
## Quiz
Q: A company beats its EPS estimate by 8% but lowers next quarter’s guidance well below forecasts. What usually matters most for the next-day move?
  The 8% EPS beat
* The lower guidance
  The size of its dividend
Why: Prices are built on future profits. Guidance changes the future; the beat describes a quarter that is already over.
## Lesson glossary
- Beat and raise: Beating estimates for the quarter and raising guidance for the future. The combination markets like most.
- Conference call: The call after a report where executives explain results and answer analysts’ questions.
- Post-earnings drift: The tendency of stocks with big earnings surprises to keep moving the same way for a while afterward.

========== l-cycle | Economic Data & the Calendar #6 | The business cycle, sectors and recessions (widget cycleClock)
Summary: Expansion, peak, slowdown and recovery: the signals to watch and the sectors that tend to lead.
Idea: Economies move in cycles. A few leading indicators hint at the phase, and different sectors have tended to lead in each one.

### Four phases

- Early cycle: the economy climbs out of a recession, rates are low and profits rebound fast.

- Mid cycle: steady growth, usually the longest phase.

- Late cycle: growth slows, inflation and rates are high, margins get squeezed.

- Recession: output and jobs fall, and the Fed cuts rates.

In the US, the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) dates recessions, usually many months after they start. From 1945 to 2020 the average expansion lasted about 64 months and the average contraction about 10 months.

### Leading indicators worth watching

- The yield curve: when the 2-year Treasury yields more than the 10-year, the curve is inverted. Inversions came before most past recessions, but the long inversion that started in 2022 has been a false alarm so far. See rates and the yield curve.

- Weekly jobless claims: a steady rise means layoffs are spreading. See jobs.

- ISM PMI and new orders: below 50 means activity is shrinking. See PMI.

- The Sahm rule: when the 3-month average unemployment rate rises 0.5 points or more above its low of the past 12 months, past recessions had usually begun.

Readings in October 2026: the 10-year yield sat 0.33 points above the 2-year (positive but flat), weekly claims were about 199,000, the ISM manufacturing PMI was 54.5 for September, and the Sahm rule read 0.00. No single signal is perfect: the Sahm rule triggered in mid-2024 without a recession.

### Sector rotation
Fidelity’s study of US cycles since 1962 found these tendencies. Early cycle: consumer discretionary, real estate, industrials, financials, technology and materials led. Mid cycle: technology. Late cycle: energy (the most consistent), materials, health care, consumer staples and utilities. Recession: consumer staples, utilities and health care. The study covers only seven full cycles. It is a tendency drawn from past cycles, not a rule. Leadership can break the pattern, for example when a technology wave carries one sector through every phase.

### Stocks move before the economy
The market looks ahead, so it usually turns before the economic data does:

- 2007 to 2009: stocks peaked in October 2007, two months before the recession began, and bottomed in March 2009, three months before it ended in June.

- 2020: the recession ran from February to April 2020; stocks bottomed in March.

- 1990 to 1991: stocks bottomed in October 1990; the recession ended in March 1991.

- 2001: the exception. The recession ended in November 2001, but the dot-com bear market kept falling until October 2002.

Waiting for the economy to look healthy has often meant buying back much higher.

### How to use the cycle

- Do not bet everything on calling the phase. Single signals give false alarms, and the official recession call comes late.

- Investors: keep a long-term mix and rebalance back to it (see asset allocation); keep buying through downturns if your job and emergency fund allow.

- Traders: notice which sectors show relative strength, and cut position size when volatility rises.

- Watch the calendar for the reports that feed these indicators (see the calendar).

## Worked example: Reading a late-cycle dashboard
Illustrative readings, not today’s data.
1. Over three months the yield curve goes from −0.4 to +0.6 points as short-term rates fall: after being inverted, it is turning positive again.
2. Weekly jobless claims climb from 210K to 260K, and the ISM PMI slips to 48: factories are shrinking.
3. The Sahm rule reads 0.4, close to the 0.5 trigger. Three of the four lights are amber or red, pointing to late cycle.
4. In past late cycles, energy, health care, consumer staples and utilities tended to hold up better, and stocks often fell before a recession was declared.
5. Sam’s $100,000 portfolio has drifted to 68% stocks and 32% bonds. Instead of guessing, Sam rebalances to the 60/40 target: sell $8,000 of stocks and buy $8,000 of bonds.
Result: No forecast needed: the dashboard says “be ready”, and rebalancing puts the plan back in place whatever comes next.

## Mistakes
- Selling everything on one recession signal. Single signals, such as the 2022 to 2024 inversion, have given false alarms.
- Waiting for the official recession call. It comes months later, often after stocks have already bottomed.
- Chasing the last cycle’s winners without checking whether conditions still match.
- Treating sector rotation as a law. It is a tendency, and some cycles break it.
## Takeaways
- The cycle runs early, mid, late and recession; expansions have lasted far longer than contractions.
- Watch several leading indicators together: the yield curve, jobless claims, PMI and the Sahm rule.
- Different sectors have tended to lead in different phases, from cyclical early on to defensive late.
- Stocks usually turn before the economy does, so waiting for good news often means buying higher.
## Quiz
Q: In most past US recessions, when did stocks hit their low?
* Before the recession ended
  Exactly when the recession was declared over
  About a year after it ended
Why: In 2009 and 2020 stocks bottomed in March, before the June 2009 and April 2020 troughs. 2001 to 2002 was the exception.
## Lesson glossary
- Sahm rule: A recession signal: the 3-month average unemployment rate rising 0.5 points or more above its low of the previous 12 months.
- NBER: The National Bureau of Economic Research, the private nonprofit whose committee officially dates US recessions.

========== l-key-dates | Economic Data & the Calendar #7 | The investor’s calendar (widget keyDates)
Summary: The monthly rhythm of data, the Fed’s meetings and the tax year in dates.
Idea: Markets move on a schedule. Know which days carry big news so you can size, wait or act on purpose.

Markets run on a timetable. A handful of government reports, Fed meetings and expiration days cause most of the big scheduled moves, and almost all of them follow published rules. Put them in your calendar and you stop being surprised by the busiest minutes of the month. The full list, with export, is on the calendar page.

### The monthly rhythm

- First business day: ISM Manufacturing PMI at 10:00 am ET. Third business day: ISM Services PMI.

- Early in the month: JOLTS job openings (10:00 am ET), then the jobs report, usually the first Friday at 8:30 am ET.

- Around the middle: CPI and PPI inflation, then retail sales, all at 8:30 am ET.

- Third Friday: monthly options expiration.

- Late in the month: flash PMIs, consumer confidence, PCE inflation, and GDP in the month after each quarter ends.

- Every week: jobless claims on Thursday at 8:30 am ET and EIA oil inventories on Wednesday at 10:30 am ET, moved when a holiday gets in the way.

Most 8:30 am reports land an hour before the stock market opens at 9:30, so futures, bond yields and the dollar react first. See trading data-release days for how to handle them.

### The Fed’s eight meetings
The FOMC meets eight times a year, roughly every six weeks. Each meeting runs two days and ends with a statement at 2:00 pm ET and the chair’s press conference at 2:30 pm ET. The March, June, September and December meetings also publish the dot plot, each official’s rate forecast. Minutes follow three weeks after every meeting. More in how the Federal Reserve works.

### Expiration days and earnings seasons
Standard monthly options expire on the third Friday (the Thursday before if that Friday is a market holiday). In March, June, September and December that same day is quad witching, one of the heaviest-volume days of the year. Earnings seasons start about two weeks after each quarter ends, when the big banks report first, and run for five to six weeks: mid-January, mid-April, mid-July and mid-October.

### The tax year in dates

- Estimated taxes for income without withholding, such as trading gains: due around January 15, April 15, June 15 and September 15 (moved to the next business day when they fall on a weekend or holiday).

- W-2s and many 1099s arrive by the end of January; broker 1099-B forms by mid-February, often with corrections later. Heavy traders often wait before filing.

- Filing deadline: mid-April, or mid-October with an extension. An extension gives more time to file, not to pay.

- IRA and HSA contributions for last year are allowed until the April filing deadline, not the extension.

- December 31: last day to harvest losses or gains (the trade date counts), make Roth conversions, defer pay into a 401(k) and take required minimum distributions. Your very first RMD can wait until April 1 of the next year. See tax-loss harvesting.


### Benefits windows
Medicare open enrollment runs from October 15 to December 7. The ACA marketplace opens on November 1; pick a plan by December 15 for coverage from January 1. Most employers run their benefits enrollment in October and November, and that is often when you can start or change an ESPP, an HSA or your 401(k) rate. Social Security announces next year’s cost-of-living increase each October.

### How to use it

- Every weekend, look at the week ahead: which days carry CPI, jobs or the Fed?

- If you day trade, size down or stand aside for the first minutes after 8:30 am and 2:00 pm releases. Spreads widen and prices jump.

- If you hold options, remember implied volatility climbs before big events and drops after (IV crush).

- Do tax moves early in December, not on the last afternoon of the year.

- Export the dates you care about to your phone’s calendar so the reminders come to you.

## Worked example: Planning December 2026
Maya trades part time with a $25,000 account and also invests for retirement. On Sunday, November 29 she spends ten minutes with the calendar.
1. She filters Fed, Data, Markets and Tax and exports December to her phone. The busy stretch stands out: the jobs report on Friday, December 4, the Fed decision with a dot plot on Wednesday, December 9 at 2:00 pm ET, and CPI the next morning, December 10, at 8:30 am ET.
2. She normally risks 1% per trade: 1% of $25,000 is $250. On December 4, 9 and 10 she halves that to $125 and waits 15 minutes after each release before entering.
3. She does not open new option trades the day before December 9 or 10. Implied volatility usually rises into big events and drops right after, so buyers often lose to IV crush even when they guess the direction.
4. She sells a fund with a $4,000 loss on Monday, December 14, not on December 31, and buys a similar fund that tracks a different index. The wash-sale window runs 30 days each way, November 14 to January 13, so she checks that none of her accounts bought the original fund in that span.
5. She also notes the rest: Medicare open enrollment ends December 7 (for her parents), the marketplace deadline for January 1 coverage is December 15, quad witching is Friday, December 18, the market closes at 1:00 pm on December 24 and is shut on Christmas Day.
Result: Ten minutes of planning puts every big December date in front of her before it arrives, and the $4,000 loss can offset gains, then up to $3,000 of income, with any rest carried forward.

## Mistakes
- Opening a big position a few minutes before a CPI report or an FOMC statement, then getting whipsawed when prices jump both ways.
- Treating the release itself as the news. Markets move on the gap between the actual number and the forecast, and on the press conference, not the headline alone.
- Waiting until December 31 to harvest losses: rushed orders, thin holiday trading and automatic purchases that trigger wash sales.
- Forgetting estimated taxes after a big trading year, then owing an underpayment penalty on top of the tax.
## Takeaways
- Most market-moving data arrives at 8:30 am ET (CPI, jobs, PPI, retail sales, GDP, PCE) or 10:00 am ET (ISM, JOLTS, sentiment).
- The Fed decides eight times a year at 2:00 pm ET; four of those meetings come with a dot plot.
- Third Friday means options expiration; in March, June, September and December it is quad witching.
- The tax year runs on quarterly estimated payments, a mid-April filing deadline and a hard December 31 for year-end moves.
- Most dates follow published rules, so you can plan months ahead and export them to your own calendar.

  9:30 am Eastern, at the opening bell
  2:00 pm Eastern, with Fed decisions
Why: Both come out at 8:30 am ET, an hour before stocks open, so futures, yields and the dollar react first.

========== l-oil | Oil, Metals & Geopolitics #1 | Oil: prices, OPEC+ and the economy (widget oilShock)
Summary: Why oil swings, how it reaches your gas tank and inflation, and who wins or loses.
Idea: Oil is a cost inside almost everything. Why it moves, supply or demand, matters as much as how far it moves.

### Two prices, one barrel
Oil is priced per barrel, which holds 42 gallons. The US benchmark is WTI (West Texas Intermediate); the global one is Brent, from the North Sea. They usually sit a few dollars apart. On October 21, 2026, WTI futures were about $98 and Brent futures about $110. Both trade as futures contracts, agreements to buy or sell oil on a future date, so the price on the news is usually the nearest futures contract. Physical oil for immediate delivery can trade at a different price: the EIA's Brent spot price was $126.55 on October 14, 2026, far above futures, a sign of very tight near-term supply.

### Who controls supply and demand

- OPEC+: OPEC members plus allies such as Russia agree on how much to pump. Their meetings move prices (see the calendar). The United Arab Emirates left OPEC and OPEC+ effective May 1, 2026.

- US shale producers: they add or cut drilling as prices rise or fall. US crude output hit a record of about 14 million barrels a day in October 2025 (EIA).

- The Strategic Petroleum Reserve: the government's emergency stockpile, released in a crisis. After large releases it held about 283 million barrels in mid-October 2026, reportedly the lowest since 1982.

- Demand: global growth, China, the summer driving season and air travel.

Every Wednesday at 10:30 am ET the Energy Information Administration (EIA) reports US crude and gasoline inventories. A surprise build or draw can move oil a few percent in minutes.

### From the barrel to your tank to inflation
Crude was about 51% of the price of a gallon of regular gas in 2025; refining, distribution and taxes make up the rest (EIA). Because a barrel is 42 gallons, each $1 on a barrel adds roughly 2.4 cents a gallon once it passes through, usually within a few weeks. Gasoline is about 2.895% of the CPI basket (BLS, December 2025), so a 10% jump at the pump adds about 0.3 percentage points to headline inflation by itself. In September 2026, energy prices were up 20.4% from a year earlier and gasoline 34.2%, while regular gas averaged about $4.26 a gallon on October 21 (AAA). Core inflation leaves energy out, but expensive fuel leaks into airfares, shipping and plastics over the following months (see Inflation).

### Supply shock or demand shock?

- Supply shock up (war, OPEC+ cut): higher prices with the same or weaker economy. Consumers pay more, airlines and shops suffer, energy producers profit. Headline inflation rises while growth slows.

- Demand shock down (recession): oil falls because the world needs less. Cheap gas does not rescue the economy; energy stocks drop most and the broad market usually falls too.

- Demand boom: oil rises because the economy is strong. Energy and most cyclical stocks can rise together.

- Supply glut: too much oil; gas gets cheaper and inflation cools while energy stocks lag.


### The Fed's dilemma
A supply shock pushes inflation up and growth down at the same time, the recipe for stagflation. Raising rates fights the inflation but deepens the slowdown; cutting does the reverse. Central banks watch core inflation partly so they can see through short energy swings, but a long oil shock that feeds into wages and prices gets their attention. In September 2026, with energy prices up about 20% from a year earlier, the Fed raised rates instead of cutting (see How the Fed works).

### Big oil moves in history

- 1973: an Arab oil embargo began in October; oil rose from about $2.90 to $11.65 a barrel by January 1974, nearly four times higher (Federal Reserve History).

- 1990: after Iraq invaded Kuwait in August, oil climbed from about $17 to a peak near $46 in October and helped tip the US into recession.

- 2022: after Russia invaded Ukraine, Brent peaked at about $139 in March, the highest since 2008.

- 2026: from late February, fighting involving Iran disrupted tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Brent futures neared $120 in early March, touched about $126 on April 30, fell to about $74 by late June and were back above $100 in October.

A Goldman Sachs look at the 1973, 1979, 1990 and 2022 shocks found oil rose a median 169% and the S&P 500 fell a median 23% from peak to trough. Shocks that feed inflation and change the Fed's path do far more damage than brief scares (see Tension, war, sanctions and tariffs).

### Owning oil: why funds can trail the price
Most oil funds hold futures, not barrels. When later months cost more than the current month (contango), the fund keeps selling the expiring contract and buying a pricier one, losing a little each roll. When later months are cheaper (backwardation), rolling adds return. Energy company stocks are another route; their profits follow oil but also costs, debt and management choices. Try the curve below.

## Worked example: A $25 oil spike and your budget
A conflict cuts supply and WTI jumps from $98 to $123 a barrel. You buy about 600 gallons of gas a year.
1. Pass-through to the pump: $25 ÷ 42 gallons ≈ $0.60 a gallon, so regular gas rises from about $4.26 to about $4.86, a 14% jump.
2. Your fuel bill: 600 gallons × $0.60 ≈ $357 more a year, money that no longer goes to restaurants or shops.
3. Inflation: gasoline is 2.895% of the CPI basket, so 14% × 2.895% ≈ 0.40 percentage points added to headline inflation, before airfares and shipping catch up.
4. Sectors: oil producers' revenue rises with every barrel, while airlines (fuel is one of their biggest costs) and delivery companies see margins squeezed.
5. The Fed: headline inflation rises while consumers slow down. Central banks often look through a one-time energy jump as long as core inflation stays calm; if it spreads into other prices, they may raise rates instead.
Result: About $357 a year out of your pocket and about 0.40 points on headline inflation from one $25 move.

## Mistakes
- Treating every oil drop as good news. If oil falls because a recession is coming, stocks usually fall with it.
- Buying an oil fund and expecting it to match the oil price. Futures rolls in contango can leave it far behind over a year.
- Chasing energy stocks after a war headline. Spikes driven by fear often fade as supply finds new routes and buyers cut back.
- Forgetting the lags: gas follows crude within weeks, and the CPI shows it the next month.
## Takeaways
- A barrel holds 42 gallons: each $1 on a barrel is about 2.4 cents a gallon at the pump.
- Gasoline is about 2.895% of the CPI, so pump prices move headline inflation quickly.
- Ask why oil moved: supply shocks hurt consumers and help producers; demand slumps hurt almost everyone.
- Supply shocks put the Fed in a bind between inflation and growth.
- Oil funds that hold futures can lag spot in contango and beat it in backwardation.
## Quiz
Q: Oil falls 30% because a recession is starting. What usually happens to the broad stock market?
  It rises, because gas is cheaper
* It usually falls, because earnings are expected to drop
  Nothing, oil and stocks are unrelated
Why: A demand-driven drop signals weaker spending and profits; cheap gas does not offset that.

- Crack spread: The gap between the price of crude and the fuels refined from it. It is roughly a refiner’s profit margin.

========== l-gold-silver | Oil, Metals & Geopolitics #2 | Gold and silver (widget metalsLab)
Summary: What moves gold and silver, the gold-silver ratio, how to own them and the tax quirk.
Idea: Gold is money that pays no interest; it shines when real yields fall, the dollar weakens or fear rises. Silver adds a factory job on top.

### Why gold has a price at all
Gold pays no interest or dividend. People hold it because it is scarce, lasts forever, is owned by central banks and has been treated as money for thousands of years. Its main drivers:

- Real yields: when bonds pay less after inflation, the cost of holding a metal that pays nothing falls, and gold tends to rise (see Rates and yields).

- The dollar: gold is priced in dollars, so a weaker dollar usually lifts it (see The dollar).

- Fear: wars, bank failures and crashes send buyers to safe havens.

- Central banks: central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes a year in 2022–2024, 863 tonnes in 2025 and a record 289 tonnes in the second quarter of 2026 (World Gold Council).


### Where prices are
Gold first topped $4,000 an ounce in October 2025, rose about 67% that year and set a record of roughly $5,600 on January 28–29, 2026. By October 21, 2026 it was about $4,141, around a quarter below the high, as rising bond yields and bets on rate hikes pulled it down even while geopolitical risk stayed high. Silver set a record of about $121.60 on January 29, 2026 and was about $58 in October.

### Silver: part money, part factory metal
Silver follows gold on money and fear, but much of it is used in industry, including electronics and solar panels. That makes it swing harder than gold in both directions and gives it a link to factory demand. In the month to October 21, 2026 alone, silver fell about 14%.

### The gold-silver ratio
Divide the gold price by the silver price to see how many ounces of silver one ounce of gold buys. It hit a modern record of about 124 in March 2020, averaged 86 in 2019, and dropped to about 45 at the January 2026 peaks. In October 2026 it was about 71. A high ratio means silver is cheap relative to gold; some investors tilt toward silver then. It is a comparison, not a timing tool, and it can stay stretched for years.

### Ways to own gold and silver

- Coins and bars: you own the metal, but dealers charge a premium to buy and pay less when you sell, and you need safe storage and insurance.

- Physically backed ETFs: funds that hold bullion in vaults, bought like a stock. Yearly fees: IAU 0.25%, GLD 0.4%, SLV 0.5% (see sources).

- Futures: leverage and contract rolls; for experienced traders only.

- Mining stocks (such as the GDX fund, 0.51% fee): companies whose profits rise faster than gold when prices climb, and fall faster when they drop, with company risk on top.


### The tax quirk
The IRS treats physical gold and silver as collectibles. Sell coins, bars or a physically backed ETF after more than a year and the gain is taxed at your ordinary rate, capped at 28%, instead of the 0/15/20% rates for stocks (see Capital gains). Held a year or less, it is ordinary income like any short-term gain. Mining-stock funds are taxed like stocks, and futures-based funds get the 60/40 treatment of Section 1256 contracts. Holding a gold ETF inside an IRA sidesteps the collectibles rate.

### How much gold?
Gold can rise when stocks fall, which is why some investors keep a small slice as a diversifier. It has also gone through long stretches of falling or flat prices, and it produces no income. Size it as insurance, not as the core of a plan (see Stocks, bonds and your age).

## Worked example: Gold ETF vs a stock fund, after tax
You invest $10,000 in a physical gold ETF (IAU) or in a stock index fund. Both rise 60% over 5 years. You are in the 24% bracket.
1. Fees: IAU charges 0.25% a year, so $16,000 shrinks to about $15,801. A 0.03% index fund ends at about $15,976.
2. Gold ETF tax: the gain of about $5,801 is a collectible gain, taxed at your 24% bracket (capped at 28%): about $1,392.
3. Stock fund tax: the gain of about $5,976 is a normal long-term gain at 15%: about $896.
4. After tax you keep about $14,409 from gold and $15,080 from stocks.
5. In an IRA neither would be taxed on the sale, so the collectibles penalty disappears there.
Result: Same price gain, but about $671 less in your pocket from the gold ETF in a taxable account.

## Mistakes
- Buying coins at a large dealer markup and forgetting the lower price you will get when you sell.
- Assuming gold always rises with inflation or in a crisis. It has had long flat and falling stretches.
- Forgetting the 28% collectibles rate when holding gold or silver ETFs in a taxable account.
- Treating mining stocks as if they were the metal. They can fall harder and carry company risk.
## Takeaways
- Gold tends to rise when real yields fall, the dollar weakens, fear spikes or central banks buy.
- Silver follows gold but swings harder because industry uses so much of it.
- The gold-silver ratio compares the two; it is not a timing signal.
- Physically backed metal ETFs are taxed as collectibles: ordinary rates up to 28% on long-term gains.
- Gold pays no income; keep it as a small diversifier, not the core.
## Quiz
Q: You hold a physically backed gold ETF for three years in the 24% bracket and sell at a gain. What federal rate applies?
  15%, like stocks
* 24%, the collectibles rule (capped at 28%)
  0%, because it is gold
Why: Physical gold counts as a collectible: long-term gains are taxed at ordinary rates up to 28%.
## Lesson glossary
- Bullion: Gold or silver in bars or coins valued for their metal content.
- Collectibles rate: The tax rate on long-term gains from collectibles such as gold: your ordinary rate, capped at 28%.

========== l-copper | Oil, Metals & Geopolitics #3 | Copper and industrial metals (widget copperGauge)
Summary: Why copper is read as a sign of growth, when that signal works and when it lies.
Idea: Copper goes into buildings, grids and machines, so demand-driven moves often show where the economy is heading before the data does.

### Why copper is called Dr. Copper
Copper carries electricity and heat, so it goes into wiring, plumbing, motors, power lines and electronics. When factories and builders get busier, they buy more copper, often before official reports show the pickup. That is why traders say copper has a "PhD in economics."

### Where copper goes
Construction and electrical infrastructure each take roughly a quarter of world copper use; machinery, transport and consumer goods take most of the rest. China uses about half of all copper, so Chinese building and factory activity moves the price more than any other country. US copper futures (COMEX) were about $6.54 a pound on October 21, 2026, up about a third in a year, after a record near $6.85 in September 2026.

### Supply is slow
Most copper comes from a small number of very large mines. A new mine can take many years to plan and build, so supply cannot jump quickly when demand rises. Strikes, floods and political changes at a single big mine can move the world price.

### When the signal fails
Copper is only a growth signal when demand moves it. Supply problems and policy can move the price with no change in the economy:

- Mine disruptions push prices up even as the economy slows.

- Tariffs: a July 30, 2025 proclamation put a 50% tariff on the copper in semi-finished products such as pipes and wire, and in copper-heavy parts, from August 1. Refined copper cathodes were left out. US copper futures had climbed far above London prices as importers rushed to buy ahead of the tariff, then fell 22% in a day, their steepest one-day drop on record. A tariff on refined copper was floated for 2027 but had not been decided as of mid-October 2026.

- Long-term trends such as power grids, data centers and electric cars can lift demand even when factory surveys are flat.

Check the PMI and other data before reading copper as a forecast (see PMI, GDP and retail sales).

### Other industrial metals
Aluminum (cans, cars, planes), nickel and lithium (batteries) and iron ore (steel) follow the same logic: demand from building and manufacturing, supply from a handful of producing countries, and big swings when either changes.

### Owning copper
Most people get exposure through mining stocks or futures-based funds. Miners add company risk and debt; futures funds face the contract rolls explained in Oil. Copper is volatile and cyclical: it tends to fall hard in recessions (see The business cycle).

## Worked example: Two copper rallies, two meanings
Made-up example. Over three months copper rises 15% twice in different years.
1. Rally A: Chinese builders and power-grid projects order more. Over the next two months the factory PMI climbs from 48 to 51, above the 50 line between shrinking and growing.
2. Reading A: demand moved copper first and the data followed. Cyclical stocks such as industrials and materials often do well in this phase.
3. Rally B: a strike shuts one of the world’s largest mines. Copper jumps 15%, but the PMI stays at 48 and new orders keep falling.
4. Reading B: supply moved the price, not demand. The economy is still soft, and the rally fades once the mine restarts.
5. The check: before treating copper as a forecast, ask what moved it and whether surveys and orders agree.
Result: Same 15% move: Rally A was a growth signal, Rally B was noise.

## Mistakes
- Reading every copper rally as a sign of growth. Strikes, tariffs and stockpiling can move it with no change in demand.
- Ignoring China. About half of world demand comes from one country.
- Chasing tariff-driven price gaps between US and London copper; policy changes can close them overnight.
- Buying a single mining stock as a copper bet and taking on that company’s debt and mine risk.
## Takeaways
- Copper is used in building, power and machines, so demand-driven moves can lead the economic data.
- China uses about half the world’s copper.
- Supply is slow to change, so mine problems can cause big price swings.
- Copper is a growth signal only when demand moves it; check the PMI and orders.
## Quiz
Q: Copper jumps 20% after a strike shuts a giant mine. What does that tell you about the global economy?
  Growth is speeding up
* Very little: supply moved the price, not demand
  A recession is certain
Why: Copper is a growth signal only when demand drives it; a supply shock says nothing about demand.

- Cathode: Refined copper in plate form, the raw material that factories turn into wire, pipe and sheet.
- LME: The London Metal Exchange, the main global market for industrial metals.

========== l-geopolitics | Oil, Metals & Geopolitics #4 | Tension, war, sanctions and tariffs (widget shockRecovery)
Summary: How wars, sanctions, tariffs and shipping shocks move oil, stocks and inflation, and what to do.
Idea: Markets fall fast on frightening news and usually recover within weeks. The exceptions are shocks that change inflation, interest rates or profits for years.

### What markets are really pricing
A stock price reflects what investors expect a company to earn and the interest rate they use to value those earnings. A headline matters for stocks only if it changes one of those. Fear arrives first: oil, gold and the VIX “fear gauge” jump, investors buy Treasuries and dollars, and stocks dip. Traders call this risk-off. Then the real question takes over: how long will it last, and what will it cost the economy?

### History: mostly short dips, a few long ones
LPL Research looked at 27 geopolitical shocks since 1941. On average the S&P 500 fell 1.0% on the first day and 4.4% in total, bottomed after about 18 days and was back to its starting level after about 39 days. The median fall was only 2.9%. The two big outliers were Pearl Harbor (−19.8%, about 307 days to recover) and Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in 1990 (−16.9%, about 189 days).
Oil shocks are different. A Goldman Sachs study of 1973, 1979, 1990 and 2022 found oil rose a median 169% and the S&P 500’s median peak-to-trough fall was 23%, because expensive energy feeds inflation and forces central banks to act.

### Oil is the main channel
Conflict near oil producers or shipping lanes threatens supply, so prices jump. Fuel and freight costs rise, inflation follows, and the Fed may have to keep rates higher. 2026 is a live example. After US and Israeli strikes on Iran began on February 28, tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz nearly stopped. Brent crude touched $126 intraday on April 30 and was still near $110 in October. US gasoline averaged $4.26 a gallon on October 21 against $3.05 a year earlier, energy prices were up 20.4% over the year to September, and the Fed raised rates on September 16. Stocks still set records by mid-April because company earnings stayed strong, but bonds fell as the 10-year yield passed 5%. More in Oil, Inflation and the Fed.

### How sanctions work

- In the US, sanctions are run by the Treasury’s OFAC. Putting a person or company on the SDN list blocks its property under US jurisdiction and bars Americans from dealing with it.

- The 50% rule extends the block to companies a sanctioned party owns 50% or more of. Non-US firms that keep dealing with a target risk secondary sanctions.

- Example: on October 22, 2025, OFAC added Russia’s two largest oil companies, Rosneft and Lukoil, and 34 subsidiaries to the SDN list.

- The oil price cap works through services instead of a ban. The G7, the EU and Australia bar their shipping, insurance and finance firms from handling Russian oil unless it sells at or below the cap. G7 firms control about 90% of the relevant insurance market. The cap started at $60 in December 2022; the EU and UK later lowered theirs and moved to a cap that adjusts every six months.

For markets, sanctions on a big exporter can lift prices for the goods it sells, reroute trade and force companies with business there to write it off. Check whether your funds hold companies with large sales in the affected region.

### Tariffs: who pays and what changes
A tariff is a tax on imports, paid by the US importer at the border. The cost is then shared between the importer’s profit margin, the foreign supplier’s price and the final customer, and other countries may retaliate. When broad tariffs were announced on April 2, 2025, the S&P 500 fell 4.84% and 5.97% on the next two days and jumped 9.52% when a 90-day pause came on April 9. On February 20, 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the emergency powers law used for many of those tariffs (IEEPA) does not authorize tariffs; tariffs under other laws (Sections 232 and 301) stayed. A temporary 10% tariff under Section 122 ran from February 24 to July 24, 2026, and Section 301 tariffs of 10% or 12.5% on 60 economies replaced it. A Minneapolis Fed analysis found tariffs had added about 0.2 to 0.4 points to core inflation by July 2026, showing up late, for example in clothing prices. Copper got its own tariff: see Copper.

### Shipping and supply chains
A few narrow sea routes, such as the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea with the Suez Canal, carry a large share of world trade. When ships are attacked there, insurance and freight costs jump and many vessels sail the long way around Africa, adding time and cost. Lloyd’s List counted Suez transits in August 2026 still 39% below their level before the Red Sea attacks began. Delays raise prices for goods, hurt importers and retailers, and help shipping companies that can charge more.

### What to do

- Ask one question: does this change inflation, interest rates or profits for years? If not, the dip has usually been short.

- Keep a cash buffer and an asset mix you can live with, so you never sell at a low to pay bills.

- Rebalance on a schedule rather than on headlines.

- Traders: cut position size when headline risk is high, expect gaps over weekends, and avoid leverage on breaking news. See trading psychology.

- Mark known risk dates (elections, deadlines, central bank meetings) in the calendar.

## Worked example: A war headline and an 80/20 account
Made-up numbers. On a Monday, fighting breaks out near a major oil route.
1. Your $50,000 account holds $40,000 in an S&P 500 fund and $10,000 in bonds.
2. Oil jumps 40% that week and your stock fund falls 6%: $40,000 × 0.94 = $37,600, a $2,400 paper loss. Bonds stay flat at $10,000.
3. You ask the key question. Oil is up a lot, so inflation could rise and rates stay higher. That argues for caution, not panic: you keep the plan and stop adding risk for now.
4. Rebalancing check: stocks are $37,600 of $47,600, about 79%, still inside your 75–85% band. No trade is needed.
5. A friend held a 3× daily S&P fund instead: roughly 3 × 6% = 18% down, about $9,000 on $50,000, and daily compounding can make it worse in a choppy week.
Result: A written plan turned a frightening week into a $2,400 paper loss and no rushed decisions. Leverage would have roughly tripled the damage.

## Mistakes
- Selling everything on the first frightening headline. In LPL’s study most geopolitical dips were recovered within weeks, and selling locks in the loss.
- Assuming every shock is short. Oil shocks that push up inflation and interest rates, such as 1973 and 1990, did far more damage.
- Trading breaking news with leverage or short-dated options: prices gap, spreads widen and stop orders can fill far from where you set them.
- Forgetting hidden exposure: international funds, companies with big sales in the region, and higher fuel costs in your own budget.
## Takeaways
- Fear moves first: oil, gold, the VIX, Treasuries and the dollar often jump while stocks dip.
- Across 27 geopolitical events since 1941, the S&P 500 fell 4.4% on average and recovered in about 39 days.
- The shocks that last change inflation, interest rates or profits, usually through oil.
- Sanctions block trade and money flows; tariffs are taxes on imports paid by the importer and shared along the chain.
- Have the plan before the headline: cash buffer, rebalancing rules, smaller size, no leverage on news.
## Quiz
Q: In LPL Research’s study of 27 geopolitical events, about how long did the S&P 500 take on average to get back to its pre-event level?
* About 39 days
  About two years
  It usually never recovered
Why: The average fall was 4.4% and recovery took about 39 days. The big exceptions were shocks that changed inflation or the economy for longer.
## Lesson glossary
- OFAC: The US Treasury office that runs sanctions programs.
- SDN list: OFAC’s list of people and companies whose US property is blocked and that Americans may not deal with.
- Secondary sanctions: Penalties aimed at non-US companies that keep dealing with a sanctioned target.
- Oil price cap: A rule that Western shipping, insurance and finance firms may only handle Russian oil sold at or below a set price.
- Chokepoint: A narrow sea route, such as the Strait of Hormuz or the Suez Canal, that a large share of trade must pass through.

========== l-dollar | Oil, Metals & Geopolitics #5 | The dollar and global money (widget dollarLab)
Summary: Why the dollar rises and falls, and what that does to big US companies, commodities and your foreign funds.
Idea: A stronger dollar shrinks the dollar value of everything earned abroad. A weaker dollar does the opposite.

### What the dollar index measures
The US Dollar Index (DXY) tracks the dollar against six major currencies, including the euro, the yen and the pound. It was about 101.7 on October 21, 2026. In 2025 it fell about 9.5%, its worst year since 2017; reporting at the time pointed to Fed rate cuts, tariff and policy uncertainty, and the dollar coming down from very high levels. In 2026 it bottomed near 95.3 in late January and climbed back above 101 by October as US yields rose and the Fed raised rates.

### Why the dollar moves

- Interest-rate gaps. When US rates rise compared with other countries, global money earns more in dollars, so demand for dollars rises. See rates and yields.

- Fear. In a global panic, investors and companies want dollars and Treasuries, the world’s main safe haven. See tension and war.

- Growth. A US economy growing faster than others tends to attract capital.

- Policy uncertainty. Doubts about US policy can push the dollar down, as in 2025.


### A strong dollar: who wins and who loses
Big US companies sell abroad in euros, yen and pounds, then report in dollars. When the dollar rises, the same foreign sales convert into fewer dollars: the translation effect. Companies call this a currency headwind and often report constant-currency growth to show how the business really did. A strong dollar also makes imports and foreign trips cheaper for Americans, tends to weigh on commodities priced in dollars (gold pays no interest, so a stronger dollar and higher rates raise the cost of holding it), and squeezes emerging markets that borrowed in dollars. A weak dollar flips each of these. See gold and earnings season.

### Your international funds
An unhedged foreign fund earns two things: the return of its stocks in their own currency, and the change in that currency against the dollar. Your return in dollars is (1 + local return) ÷ (1 + dollar change) − 1. Hedged funds use contracts to cancel most of the currency move, for a small cost. Owning some international stocks spreads your risk across economies and currencies; see index funds and asset allocation.

### How traders use it

- Watch the dollar alongside the 2-year Treasury yield: both react to surprises about the Fed.

- Gold and the dollar often move in opposite directions, though not every day.

- In earnings season, listen for “currency headwind” or “tailwind” and compare reported growth with constant-currency growth.

## Worked example: A strong-dollar year for a US multinational
Made-up company, same customers and prices in local money all year.
1. The company sells $100 billion a year: $60 billion in the US and $40 billion abroad in euros, yen and pounds. Its profit margin is 20%, so profit is $20 billion.
2. The dollar rises 10%. Each unit of foreign currency now buys 1 ÷ 1.10 = 0.909 dollars, so the same foreign sales count as $40 billion × 0.909 ≈ $36.4 billion.
3. Total sales fall to about $96.4 billion, down 3.6%, without losing a single customer.
4. Half of the costs of those foreign sales are also paid abroad: $40 billion × 80% × 50% = $16 billion, which now counts as about $14.5 billion. That natural hedge softens the hit.
5. Profit = $60B + $36.4B − $64B of US costs − $14.5B ≈ $17.8 billion, down about 10.9%.
Result: A 10% stronger dollar cut reported profit by about 11% while the business itself did not change.

## Mistakes
- Reading a currency headwind as a weaker business. Check constant-currency growth in the earnings report.
- Forgetting that an unhedged international fund carries currency risk: a strong dollar can turn a good year abroad into a loss for you.
- Assuming the dollar always rises in a crisis. In 2025 it fell about 9.5% amid tariff and policy uncertainty.
- Betting heavily on currency forecasts. Exchange rates are hard to predict; diversify instead.
## Takeaways
- The dollar index (DXY) tracks the dollar against six major currencies; it was about 101.7 in October 2026.
- Higher US interest rates and global fear usually lift the dollar; policy doubts and Fed cuts can push it down.
- A strong dollar shrinks the dollar value of foreign sales and of unhedged foreign funds; a weak dollar boosts them.
- Commodities priced in dollars, such as gold, often move opposite to the dollar.
## Quiz
Q: The dollar rises 10% against the euro and a European stock fund gains 8% in euros. Roughly what does an unhedged US investor earn?
  About +18%
* About −2%
  Exactly +8%
Why: 1.08 ÷ 1.10 − 1 ≈ −1.8%: the stronger dollar more than wiped out the local gain.
## Lesson glossary
- Translation effect: The change in reported results when foreign-currency sales and costs are converted into dollars.
- Constant currency: Growth measured as if exchange rates had not changed, to show how the business itself did.
- Currency hedging: Using contracts to cancel out exchange-rate moves, so a fund’s return stays close to its local return.
