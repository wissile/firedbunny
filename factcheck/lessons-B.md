

========== l-401k-match | Company Benefits #1 | The 401(k) match is free money (widget matchCalc)
Summary: How employer matching works, how much you leave behind, and the traps.
Idea: Your employer adds money when you save. Not saving enough to get all of it is turning down part of your pay.

Most companies with a 401(k) add money to your account when you contribute. That is the employer match. It is part of your pay package, and the only way to collect it is to contribute yourself.

### How the common formulas work

- 100% up to 4%: put in 4% of pay and the company adds another 4%.
- 50% of 6%: put in 6% and the company adds 3%. Putting in 3% only gets you 1.5%.
- 100% of the first 3% plus 50% of the next 2%: a common "safe harbor" formula. Put in 5% and the company adds 4%.Your plan documents (the summary plan description) or HR spell out your formula. Find the contribution rate that captures all of it: that rate is your floor.

### The match is on top of your own limit
In 2026 you can defer up to $24,500 of your own pay. The match does not count against that number. It counts only toward a bigger total limit of $72,000 a year, which the next lesson covers.

### Vesting: when the match becomes yours
Your own contributions are always 100% yours. The employer money can come with a vesting schedule. The law allows at most a 3-year cliff (nothing until year 3, then everything) or a 6-year graded schedule (20% a year from year 2). Safe harbor matches vest right away. If you are thinking about leaving, check how close you are to the next vesting date.

### The front-loading trap
Many plans calculate the match each paycheck. If you contribute a big share of pay early and hit the yearly limit in, say, September, the paychecks after that get no match. Some plans pay a true-up at year end to fix it; many do not. If yours does not, spread your contributions so they last all 26 paychecks.

### Newer options worth asking about

- Roth match. You choose whether your own money goes in pre-tax or Roth (see Traditional or Roth?). Since SECURE 2.0, plans may also let you take the match as Roth. It is then fully vested and counts as taxable income that year.
- Student-loan match. Plans may treat your student-loan payments as if they were 401(k) contributions and match them, so paying off loans no longer means missing the match.
- High earners. Employers can only count the first $360,000 of pay in 2026 when figuring contributions.
### When you leave
Your contributions and the vested part of the match go with you. Roll the account into your new plan or an IRA rather than cashing out, which triggers income tax and usually a 10% penalty before 59½.

## Worked example: Half the match left behind
Maya earns $80,000. Her plan matches 50% of the first 6% of pay.
1. Maya contributes 3% of pay: $2,400 a year. The match is 50% of that, $1,200.
2. If she contributed 6% ($4,800), the match would be 50% of $4,800: $2,400.
3. At 3% she leaves $1,200 of free money behind every year.
4. Raising her rate costs $2,400 more of pay before tax, and earns $1,200 of match: an instant 50% return before any market growth.
5. Invested for 30 years at 7%, $1,200 a year grows to about $113,000 ($1,200 × 94.46).
Result: Moving from 3% to 6% is worth roughly $113,000 by retirement from the match alone.

## Mistakes
- Contributing less than the match formula needs, even for a year or two. Missed match can never be made up later.
- Maxing out early in a plan that matches per paycheck and has no true-up, so the last months get no match.
- Quitting a few weeks before a vesting date and forfeiting thousands of dollars of employer money.
- Assuming the match counts against your $24,500 limit and contributing less because of it.
## Takeaways
- Always contribute at least enough to get the full match. It is an instant 25% to 100% return.
- The match comes on top of your own deferral limit.
- Check vesting before you change jobs.
- If you plan to max out, ask HR whether the plan has a true-up, or spread your contributions across all paychecks.
## Quiz
Q: Your plan matches 50% of the first 6% of pay. You earn $60,000 and contribute 4%. What is the match?
* $1,200
  $1,800
  $2,400
Why: 4% of $60,000 is $2,400, and half of that is $1,200. Contributing 6% would raise it to $1,800.

========== l-401k-limits | Company Benefits #2 | Max your 401(k): the limits most people miss (widget limitStack)
Summary: Three 401(k) limits, and why your employer’s money sits on top of yours.
Idea: There is a limit on what you put in and a much bigger limit on what goes in from every source.

Most people think the 401(k) limit is one number. There are really three, and the biggest one is the one people miss.

### Limit 1: what you defer ($24,500 in 2026)
This is your elective deferral limit: the pre-tax and Roth 401(k) money you choose to take out of your pay. It is per person, not per plan, so two jobs in one year share it. It also covers 403(b) plans.

### Limit 2: catch-up contributions

- Age 50 or older by December 31: an extra $8,000 in 2026, so $32,500 of your own money.
- Ages 60 to 63: a bigger catch-up of $11,250 instead of $8,000, so $35,750.
- From 2026, if your Social Security wages from that employer (W-2 box 3) were above $150,000 the year before, your catch-ups must go in as Roth. Ask your plan how it handles this.
### Limit 3: everything together ($72,000 in 2026)
The annual additions limit (also called the 415(c) limit) caps your deferrals plus the employer match, profit sharing and any after-tax contributions. It is $72,000 for 2026, or 100% of your pay if that is lower. Catch-up contributions are allowed on top, so up to $80,000 at 50+ and $83,250 at 60 to 63.
So yes: you can max out your own $24,500 and still receive the full match. The match never takes room from you.

### Filling the rest of the room
Even with a generous match, most people leave tens of thousands of dollars of room unused. If your plan accepts after-tax contributions, you can fill it, then convert that money to Roth. That is the mega backdoor Roth. Not every plan offers it, and plans that do may limit how much highly paid employees can put in after tax.

### What order to fill things
A common order: get the full match, then fund an HSA if eligible, then an IRA, then max the 401(k), then after-tax money. When to open an IRA walks through it.

## Worked example: Room most people never use
Jordan is 45, earns $150,000, and the employer contributes 4% of pay.
1. Jordan defers the full $24,500 for 2026.
2. The employer adds 4% of $150,000: $6,000. That does not reduce Jordan’s $24,500.
3. Together that is $30,500 going in.
4. The total limit is $72,000, so $72,000 − $30,500 = $41,500 of room is still open.
5. Jordan’s plan allows after-tax contributions with in-plan Roth conversion, so Jordan adds $15,000 after tax and converts it.
Result: Jordan saves $45,500 a year in the plan, $15,000 more than the "max" most people stop at.

## Mistakes
- Thinking the employer match reduces how much you are allowed to defer.
- Over-contributing across two jobs in one year: the $24,500 deferral limit is per person, so the excess must be fixed before the tax deadline.
- Turning 50 (or 60) and not raising your contribution to use the catch-up.
- Assuming after-tax room is useful without checking that the plan allows conversions to Roth.
## Takeaways
- 2026: $24,500 of your own deferrals, plus $8,000 catch-up at 50+ or $11,250 at 60 to 63.
- The employer’s money comes on top of your deferral limit, up to $72,000 in total (or 100% of pay).
- Catch-ups sit on top of the $72,000.
- After-tax contributions can fill the leftover room if your plan allows them.
## Quiz
Q: You are 40, defer $24,500 and your employer adds $10,000. How much more could go in for 2026 if your plan allows after-tax contributions?
  $0, you are maxed out
* $37,500
  $47,500
Why: $72,000 − $24,500 − $10,000 = $37,500 of room left under the annual additions limit.

========== l-mega-backdoor | Company Benefits #3 | The mega backdoor Roth (widget megaBackdoor)
Summary: Turn leftover 401(k) room into tens of thousands of dollars a year of Roth money.
Idea: After-tax 401(k) money converted to Roth grows tax-free, and the room for it can be far bigger than an IRA.

A Roth IRA allows only $7,500 a year in 2026, and high earners cannot contribute directly. The mega backdoor Roth uses your 401(k) instead, and the room can be several times larger.

### What your plan needs

- After-tax contributions: a third type of contribution, separate from pre-tax and Roth deferrals.
- A way to move that money to Roth: an in-plan Roth conversion (to the Roth 401(k)) or in-service withdrawals (rolled to your Roth IRA while you still work there).Search your plan documents or benefits site for "after-tax" and "in-plan conversion", or ask HR. Many large employers offer it; many smaller plans do not.

### Step by step

- Max your normal deferral ($24,500 in 2026) and get the full match.
- Work out your room: $72,000 minus your deferrals minus all employer money.
- Set an after-tax contribution percentage that fills it by year end.
- Turn on automatic conversion if the plan offers it, or convert often.
- Keep records. The converted after-tax money was already taxed, so only earnings before conversion are taxable.
### Convert fast
After-tax money left in the plan grows, and that growth is taxable as income when it comes out. Converting every paycheck keeps the taxable part close to zero. Leaving it for years can cost you thousands.

### In-plan conversion or roll to a Roth IRA?
Both work. Rolling to a Roth IRA gives you more fund choices and avoids the plan’s rules. An in-plan conversion is often automatic and simpler. When you roll out, IRS rules let you send the after-tax part to a Roth IRA and any earnings to a traditional IRA, so you do not owe tax on them now. The 1099-R can be confusing, so keep your own records.

### Limits to know
Plans must pass nondiscrimination tests. If not enough lower-paid employees contribute, highly paid employees can have after-tax money refunded. Equity comp and bonuses do not change the $72,000 cap. This is a good topic for a CPA or fee-only adviser if your plan rules are unclear.

## Worked example: $20,000 a year into Roth
Priya maxes her deferral and match, leaving $35,000 of room. Her plan allows after-tax money with automatic in-plan Roth conversion.
1. She sets an after-tax contribution of $20,000 a year, about $769 per paycheck.
2. Each paycheck the plan converts it to Roth 401(k) the same day, so there are no taxable earnings.
3. After 20 years at 7%, $20,000 a year grows to about $819,900 ($20,000 × 40.995).
4. She contributed $400,000, so $419,900 is growth, and all of it comes out tax-free in retirement.
5. Left unconverted, that $419,900 of growth would be taxed as income: at 24% that is about $100,800.
Result: Converting turns about $100,000 of future tax into $0.

## Mistakes
- Contributing after-tax money to a plan that offers no conversion or in-service withdrawal, so earnings pile up taxable.
- Converting once a year or less, and paying tax on months of earnings.
- Confusing after-tax contributions with Roth 401(k) deferrals. Roth deferrals count toward the $24,500 limit; after-tax money does not.
- Forgetting the plan may refund after-tax money to highly paid employees if it fails nondiscrimination testing.
## Takeaways
- The mega backdoor fills the room between your deferrals plus employer money and the $72,000 total.
- Your plan must allow after-tax contributions and in-plan conversion or in-service withdrawals.
- Convert as often as you can so almost nothing is taxable.
- It can move several times more money into Roth each year than a Roth IRA allows.
## Quiz
Q: Why convert after-tax 401(k) money to Roth quickly?
  The contribution itself is taxed again if you wait
* Earnings before conversion are taxable, so waiting increases the tax
  Conversions are only allowed in January
Why: The after-tax dollars were already taxed. Only the growth before conversion is taxable, so converting fast keeps that near zero.
## Lesson glossary
- In-service withdrawal: Taking money out of your 401(k) while you still work for the employer, allowed only if the plan permits it.
## Sources
- IRS Notice 2025-67: 2026 retirement plan limits (deferral, catch-up, $72,000 total, $360,000 pay cap, Roth catch-up wage test) — https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf
- Journal of Accountancy: how the mega backdoor Roth works — https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2023/may/how-mega-backdoor-roth-works/

========== l-hsa | Company Benefits #4 | The HSA: a triple tax break (widget hsaCalc)
Summary: Tax-free in, tax-free growth, tax-free out: the HSA as a retirement account.
Idea: An HSA is the only account where money goes in, grows and comes out without tax, if you spend it on health care.

A health savings account (HSA) comes with a high-deductible health plan (HDHP). It has the best tax deal in the US system: no tax going in, no tax on growth, and no tax coming out for qualified medical costs.

### Who can open one
You need HSA-eligible coverage (an HDHP), no other non-HDHP health coverage, you cannot be enrolled in Medicare, and nobody can claim you as a dependent. For 2026 an HDHP must have a deductible of at least $1,700 (self-only) or $3,400 (family).

### Limits

- 2026: $4,400 for self-only coverage, $8,750 for family coverage.
- 2027 (already announced): $4,500 and $9,000.
- Age 55 and older: an extra $1,000.
- Employer deposits count toward the limit.Contributing through payroll also skips Social Security and Medicare tax (7.65%), which a 401(k) or IRA cannot do.

### New from 2026
The 2025 tax law widened who can use an HSA. Bronze and catastrophic plans now count as HSA-compatible, telehealth before the deductible no longer blocks eligibility, and people in a direct primary care arrangement (a flat monthly fee to a doctor, within a cap) can still contribute and can pay that fee from the HSA.

### Treat it like a retirement account

- Invest it. Many HSAs start in cash. Move most of it into low-cost index funds.
- Pay small bills out of pocket if you can and keep every receipt. There is no deadline: you can reimburse yourself tax-free years later, as long as the expense came after you opened the HSA.
- After 65, you can withdraw for anything. Non-medical withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA; medical ones stay tax-free. Before 65, non-medical withdrawals owe tax plus a 20% penalty.
### Watch-outs

- California and New Jersey do not follow the federal HSA rules, so state tax applies there.
- Stop contributing once you enroll in Medicare, and note that Part A coverage can be backdated up to six months when you sign up after 65.
- A health FSA is different: it is "use it or lose it" for the year (with a small carryover), and it does not grow.Whether the HDHP is the right plan for your family is the question in Open enrollment.

## Worked example: An HSA that funds retirement
Sam is 35, has self-only HDHP coverage, and is in the 22% bracket.
1. Sam contributes the 2026 maximum, $4,400, through payroll.
2. Tax saved: 22% federal plus 7.65% payroll tax is 29.65%, about $1,305 a year.
3. Sam pays about $1,000 of yearly medical bills out of pocket and saves the receipts.
4. Invested at 7% for 30 years, $4,400 a year grows to about $415,600 ($4,400 × 94.46).
5. By 65 Sam has $30,000 of saved receipts that can come out tax-free at any time, plus a big account for medical costs in retirement.
Result: About $1,300 a year of tax saved now, and a six-figure tax-free health fund later.

## Mistakes
- Leaving the HSA in cash for decades instead of investing it.
- Throwing away receipts: without them you cannot prove tax-free reimbursements later.
- Contributing while also covered by a non-HDHP plan, such as a spouse’s PPO or a general-purpose health FSA.
- Spending HSA money on non-medical costs before 65 and paying tax plus the 20% penalty.
## Takeaways
- No tax in, no tax on growth, no tax out for medical costs.
- 2026 limits: $4,400 self-only, $8,750 family, plus $1,000 at 55+.
- Invest it and pay small bills from cash if you can; save receipts forever.
- After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA.
## Quiz
Q: You paid a $900 medical bill out of pocket in 2026 and kept the receipt. When can you reimburse yourself tax-free from your HSA?
  Only in 2026
  Only after age 65
* Any time later, as long as you keep the receipt
Why: There is no deadline for reimbursing a qualified expense that came after the HSA was opened, so the money can grow in the meantime.

========== l-rsu | Company Benefits #5 | RSUs: taxes at vest and when to sell (widget rsuVest)
Summary: How vesting shares are taxed, the 22% withholding gap and a simple sell plan.
Idea: RSUs are pay that arrives as shares. They are taxed when they vest, and holding them is a fresh bet on one stock.

### What an RSU is
A restricted stock unit is a promise of company shares that arrive on set vesting dates, as long as you still work there. A common schedule is four years with a one-year cliff: nothing for 12 months, then a quarter of the grant, then the rest every quarter. Leave before a date and the unvested units are gone. At private companies, many RSUs are double-trigger: they need both time served and an event such as an IPO before they count.

### How RSUs are taxed

- At vest: the market value of the shares is taxed as pay, exactly like a cash bonus. It shows up on your W-2, with Social Security and Medicare taxes too.
- Your cost basis is the price on the vest date. Your holding period starts that day.
- When you sell: only the move after vest is a capital gain or loss. Sell within a year and it is short-term; after a year, long-term (see capital gains).Granting the RSUs creates no tax. Selling right at vest creates almost none, because the price barely moved since it was taxed. Check that your 1099-B shows the vest price as your basis: if it shows $0, the vest value gets taxed twice.

### The 22% withholding gap
Employers usually withhold federal tax on vesting shares at the flat supplemental rate: 22% on the first $1 million of supplemental pay in a year and 37% above that. Most plans do this by quietly selling some shares (sell to cover).
If your real top bracket is 24%, 32% or 35%, 22% is not enough, and the rest is due in April. A senior engineer with $60,000 of vesting shares in the 32% bracket can easily owe several thousand dollars more. Fixes: move the gap into savings at each vest, raise withholding on your W-4 (or pick a higher RSU withholding rate if your employer offers one), or make estimated payments. Paying enough during the year also avoids an underpayment penalty; the instructions for IRS Form 2210 explain how much is enough.

### Hold or sell? The bonus test
Ask yourself: if this vest were paid as a cash bonus, would I spend it all on my employer’s stock? If not, selling at vest is the same decision. Holding ties your savings to the company that already pays your salary, so one bad year can hit your job and your net worth at once. Many planners suggest keeping any single stock under about 10% of your net worth.

### A simple RSU plan

- Sell at vest, or set up automatic sales, and move the money into broad index funds (see index funds).
- Set aside the tax gap in a high-yield savings account until April (see where to park cash).
- Use the cash flow to max your 401(k) and HSA from your paycheck (see 401(k) limits).
- If you are an insider, sell during open trading windows or through a pre-set 10b5-1 plan.
- Selling company shares at a loss close to a vest or an ESPP purchase can run into the wash sale rule. Ask your broker or CPA how they treat it before you harvest a loss.Equity pay gets complicated at high incomes. A CPA or a fee-only adviser who works with tech employees can help you plan the year.

## Worked example: A $60,000 vest in the 32% bracket
Sam is single, earns a $220,000 salary and has 400 RSUs vesting this year at $150 a share. All numbers are 2026 federal only.
1. 400 shares × $150 = $60,000 is added to Sam’s W-2 pay, just like a bonus.
2. Taxable income before the vest is $220,000 − $16,100 standard deduction = $203,900, already in the 32% bracket that starts at $201,775.
3. The vest pushes taxable income to $263,900. $52,325 of it is taxed at 32% and $7,675 at 35%: $16,744 + $2,686 = $19,430 of federal tax.
4. Payroll withholds 22%: $13,200, done by selling 88 of the 400 shares. Sam receives 312 shares.
5. The gap is $19,430 − $13,200 = $6,230 due in April. Sam moves $6,230 to savings the day the shares land.
6. Sam sells the 312 shares at $150. Cost basis is $150, so there is no extra tax, and the $46,800 goes into a total market index fund.
Result: Sam keeps about $40,570 after federal tax with no surprise bill, and company stock stays a small slice of net worth.

## Mistakes
- Assuming the 22% withheld covers the tax. Above the 22% bracket you owe the difference in April.
- Holding every vest because selling “feels like losing money”. Selling at vest adds no tax; holding is a new bet on one stock.
- Thinking the shares are taxed only when sold. The value is taxed as pay on the vest date whether you sell or not.
- Ignoring a $0 cost basis on the 1099-B and paying tax on the vest value a second time.
## Takeaways
- RSUs are taxed as pay on the vest date; your cost basis is that day’s price.
- Withholding is a flat 22% (37% over $1 million), often too little at higher incomes.
- Selling right at vest costs almost no extra tax.
- Keep any one stock, especially your employer’s, to a small slice of net worth.
## Quiz
Q: Your RSUs vest at $100 a share and you sell them the same day at $100. What is taxed?
  Nothing, because you sold right away
* The $100 a share as pay; the sale adds about $0 of gain
  Only the sale, as a short-term capital gain
Why: The value at vest is taxed as pay; your basis is $100, so selling at $100 creates no extra gain.
## Lesson glossary
- 10b5-1 plan: A written plan that sets future sales in advance, so company insiders can sell shares without timing concerns.
- Double-trigger RSU: An RSU that needs two events, usually time served and an IPO or sale of the company, before it vests and is taxed.

========== l-espp | Company Benefits #6 | ESPP: the 15% discount (widget esppCalc)
Summary: Why enrolling usually pays, how the lookback works and how the sale is taxed.
Idea: An ESPP lets you buy company stock below market price. Buying and selling right away is one of the best low-risk returns at work.

### How an ESPP works
An employee stock purchase plan takes a slice of each paycheck, after tax, during an offering period (often six months). On the purchase date it buys company shares for you at a discount, up to 15% in a qualified (Section 423) plan. The IRS caps purchases at $25,000 of stock per calendar year, valued at the price on the first day of the offering.

### The lookback makes it better
Many plans add a lookback: the discount applies to the lower of the price on the first day or on the purchase day. If the stock went from $40 to $50, you pay 85% of $40 = $34 for shares worth $50, a 47% gain. If it fell to $35, you pay 85% of $35 = $29.75 and still start 17.6% ahead.

### Why most people should enroll

- A 15% discount is an instant 17.6% gain on your money (1 ÷ 0.85 − 1), before any price move.
- Your money is withheld bit by bit, so on average it is in the plan for only about three months of a six-month period. That 17.6% works out to about 38% a year counted over six months, and closer to 90% a year once you allow for the money going in gradually.
- If you sell on purchase day, you take very little stock risk.Enroll if you can live on the smaller paycheck for six months. If cash is tight, start at a lower percentage rather than skipping it.

### How the sale is taxed

- Disqualifying sale (before two years from the first day or one year from purchase, including selling right away): the discount at purchase (purchase-day price minus what you paid) is taxed as pay. It counts as income tax only: ESPP income is not hit by Social Security or Medicare tax. Any move after purchase is a capital gain or loss.
- Qualifying sale (after both holding periods): only the smaller of your actual gain or the discount on the first-day price is taxed as pay; the rest is a long-term gain.Holding for the qualifying sale saves some tax, but it means two years with a big piece of your savings in one stock. For most people, selling right away and investing the money broadly wins on risk.

### The cost basis trap
Your employer adds the taxable discount to your W-2. Your broker’s Form 1099-B often reports your basis as only what you paid. If you file using that number, you pay tax on the discount twice. Use Form 3922 and your broker’s supplemental statement to report the adjusted basis on Form 8949.

## Worked example: Six months, 10% of pay, sell on purchase day
Ana earns $100,000, is single and puts 10% of pay into an ESPP with a 15% discount and a lookback.
1. Over six months she contributes 10% × $100,000 ÷ 2 = $5,000.
2. The stock is $40 on the first day and $50 on purchase day. With the lookback she pays 85% × $40 = $34 a share.
3. $5,000 ÷ $34 buys 147 whole shares for $4,998; the leftover $2 is refunded.
4. The shares are worth 147 × $50 = $7,350, a $2,352 gain (47%), which she sells the same day.
5. Selling right away is disqualifying: $2,352 is taxed as pay. In her 22% bracket that is about $517 of federal tax.
6. Her after-tax profit is about $1,835 on $4,998, in six months, with almost no time holding the stock.
Result: She reports a cost basis of $50 a share (not $34) so the $2,352 is not taxed twice.

## Mistakes
- Skipping the plan because “I can’t pick stocks”. You are not picking: buying at a discount and selling right away is close to a sure gain.
- Holding every purchase for the qualifying sale and ending up with a big chunk of savings in one stock.
- Using the broker’s 1099-B basis and paying tax twice on the discount.
- Contributing so much that you run up credit card debt to cover the smaller paycheck.
## Takeaways
- A 15% discount is a 17.6% instant gain; a lookback can make it much bigger.
- Selling on purchase day keeps the stock risk small.
- Disqualifying sale: the discount is taxed as pay. Qualifying sale: more of the gain is long-term.
- Report the adjusted cost basis from Form 3922 to avoid double tax.
## Quiz
Q: With a 15% discount and a lookback, the stock is $40 on day one and $50 on purchase day. What do you pay per share?
  $42.50
* $34.00
  $40.00
Why: The lookback uses the lower price, $40, and 85% of $40 is $34.
## Lesson glossary
- Form 3922: The form your employer sends after ESPP purchases, with the dates and prices you need to report the sale correctly.
- Offering period: The stretch, often six months, during which an ESPP collects your contributions before buying shares.

========== l-stock-options | Company Benefits #7 | Stock options: ISOs and NSOs (widget equityOptions)
Summary: What exercising costs, how ISOs and NSOs are taxed, AMT and the 90-day trap.
Idea: An employee stock option lets you buy shares at a fixed strike price. When and how you exercise decides most of the tax.

### What you get
An employee stock option is the right to buy company shares at a fixed strike price, usually the share value on the grant date. Options vest over time like RSUs and typically expire 10 years after grant. They are worth something only when the share price is above the strike. To own the shares you must exercise: pay the strike price for each share.

### NSOs: taxed as pay when you exercise
With a non-qualified stock option, the gap between the share price and the strike at exercise (the spread) is taxed as pay, with Social Security and Medicare, the year you exercise. Your cost basis becomes the price at exercise, and later moves are capital gains or losses.

### ISOs: no regular tax at exercise, but watch AMT

- Exercising an incentive stock option creates no regular income tax.
- The spread does count for the alternative minimum tax. A big spread can mean a large AMT bill, even if you cannot sell the shares yet. Part of that AMT can come back as a credit in later years.
- Sell at least 2 years after grant and 1 year after exercise and the whole gain is long-term. Sell sooner and it is a disqualifying sale: the spread is taxed as pay (without Social Security or Medicare), and there is no AMT if you sell in the same year you exercised.
- Only $100,000 of ISOs (by grant-date value) can first become exercisable each year; the rest are treated as NSOs.
### Ways to exercise

- Exercise and sell (cashless) at a public company: simple, taxed as pay, no stock risk.
- Exercise and hold: you need cash for the strike and for any tax, and you carry the stock risk.
- Early exercise with an 83(b) election: some startups let you exercise before vesting. Filing an 83(b) (IRS Form 15620, which can now be filed online) within 30 days means you are taxed on today’s spread, often $0, instead of a bigger one at vesting. For ISOs the election only counts for AMT. Miss the 30 days and it is gone.
- At private companies you may pay tax on shares you cannot sell for years. Exercise only what you can afford to lose.
### Startup shares and QSBS
For shares of a qualifying small company acquired after July 4, 2025, the qualified small business stock (QSBS) rules can exclude 50% of the gain after 3 years, 75% after 4 and 100% after 5, up to $15 million (or 10 times your basis, if larger). The company must meet the tests (including gross assets of $75 million or less), so ask before you count on it.

### When you leave the company
Most plans give you a short window, often 90 days, to exercise vested options after you leave. ISOs exercised more than three months after you leave are taxed as NSOs. Unexercised options that are worth something simply disappear when the window closes, so check your plan before you resign. Equity decisions this size deserve a CPA who knows AMT.

## Worked example: Exercising 4,000 startup ISOs
Lee is single, earns $160,000 and holds 4,000 vested ISOs with a $5 strike. A recent valuation puts the shares at $30.
1. Exercising costs 4,000 × $5 = $20,000 of cash.
2. The spread is ($30 − $5) × 4,000 = $100,000. That is not regular income for an ISO.
3. For AMT, the $100,000 is added to income and the standard deduction is not allowed. On these numbers the simplified estimate is about $17,000 of AMT for the year, on top of about $27,100 of regular tax on the salary.
4. If Lee had NSOs instead, the $100,000 would be taxed as pay: about $27,400 of extra federal income tax plus about $3,500 of Social Security and Medicare.
5. Lee holds until 2 years after grant and 1 year after exercise, then sells at $40: the whole $140,000 gain over the strike is long-term.
Result: Qualifying turns most of the gain into long-term gain, but Lee needed $20,000 plus the AMT in cash and carried a year of single-stock risk.

## Mistakes
- Exercising a large batch of ISOs without checking AMT, then owing tax on paper gains you cannot sell.
- Letting vested, in-the-money options expire after leaving the job because the 90-day window passed.
- Missing the 30-day deadline for an 83(b) election after an early exercise.
- Holding a startup’s shares for the long-term rate while the company’s value falls, so the tax saved is far smaller than the loss.
## Takeaways
- NSO spread at exercise is taxed as pay; ISO spread can trigger AMT instead.
- ISOs held 2 years from grant and 1 year from exercise give an all long-term gain.
- Plan the cash for the strike and the tax before you exercise.
- Know your post-termination window, often 90 days.
## Quiz
Q: You exercise NSOs with a $10 strike when the stock is $25. What is taxed as pay that year?
  $25 a share
* $15 a share, the spread
  Nothing until you sell
Why: For NSOs the spread at exercise, $25 − $10, is ordinary income.
## Lesson glossary
- Post-termination exercise window: How long you have to exercise vested options after leaving a company, often 90 days.
- Strike price: The fixed price per share you pay when you exercise an employee stock option.
## Sources
- IRS Topic 427: Stock options — https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc427
- IRC Section 422: incentive stock options (holding periods, $100,000 limit, 3 months) — https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/422

========== l-open-enrollment | Company Benefits #8 | Open enrollment: pick the right plan (widget planPicker)
Summary: A yearly checklist: health plan, HSA or FSA, insurance, 401(k), ESPP and beneficiaries.
Idea: Once a year you choose benefits worth thousands of dollars. A one-hour review pays better than almost anything else you do.

Open enrollment is the yearly window, usually in the fall, to choose next year’s benefits. You can also enroll when you are hired and within a short window (often 30 days) after a life event such as marriage or a new baby. Outside those windows, most choices are locked for the year.
If you are enrolling in fall 2026, you are choosing 2027 benefits. The 2027 HSA limits are out ($4,500 self-only, $9,000 family); the IRS had not yet announced 2027 401(k) limits as of October 21, 2026.

### 1. Health plan: compare total cost, not premium
Add up your premiums, your expected share of bills, and the worst case (premiums plus the out-of-pocket maximum). Then subtract any employer HSA deposit and the tax you save paying bills with pre-tax money. A high-deductible plan with an HSA often wins for healthy years; a low-deductible PPO can win when you expect heavy use. Check that your doctors are in network.

### 2. Pre-tax accounts

- HSA (with an HDHP): $4,400 self-only or $8,750 family in 2026. Keeps growing. See the HSA lesson.
- Health FSA: up to $3,400 in 2026 for predictable costs. Mostly use it or lose it, though some plans let about $680 carry over.
- Dependent care FSA: up to $7,500 per household in 2026 for child care while you work, raised from $5,000 by the 2025 tax law.
- Commuter benefits: up to $340 a month each for transit and parking in 2026, pre-tax.
### 3. Insurance

- Life: free coverage is often one or two times salary. Group coverage above $50,000 creates a little taxable income. Families usually need more; cheap term coverage is covered in Insurance you actually need.
- Disability: replaces part of your pay if you cannot work. If you can pay the long-term disability premium with after-tax money, the benefit is usually tax-free when you need it.
### 4. Money benefits

- Check your 401(k) rate still gets the full match (the match lesson).
- Enroll in the ESPP if there is one; its enrollment windows often line up with open enrollment (ESPP: the 15% discount).
- Legal plans, student-loan help and tuition benefits can save real money.
### 5. Beneficiaries
Check who is named on your 401(k), HSA and life insurance. These forms override your will, so update them after a marriage, divorce or birth.

## Worked example: A family picks a plan
The Lees expect about $4,000 of medical bills next year. Their choices: an HDHP with $3,000 of premiums and a $1,000 employer HSA deposit, or a PPO with $6,500 of premiums.
1. HDHP share of bills: $3,400 deductible plus 20% of the next $600 = $3,520.
2. HDHP all-in before tax savings: $3,000 + $3,520 − $1,000 = $5,520.
3. They pay the other $2,520 of bills from their HSA with pre-tax money: at 22% + 7.65% that saves about $747, so about $4,770 all-in.
4. PPO share of bills: $1,000 deductible plus 20% of $3,000 = $1,600. All-in: $6,500 + $1,600 = $8,100, minus about $474 of FSA tax savings = about $7,630.
5. Worst case: HDHP $3,000 + $10,000 − $1,000 = $12,000; PPO $6,500 + $7,000 = $13,500.
Result: The HDHP costs about $2,850 less in their expected year and less in the worst case too.

## Mistakes
- Picking the plan with the lowest premium, or the lowest deductible, without adding up the total.
- Letting benefits roll over without checking: prices, plans and your family change every year.
- Putting more in a health FSA than you will spend and losing the rest.
- Never updating beneficiaries after a marriage, divorce or new child.
## Takeaways
- Compare plans on premiums plus expected bills plus worst case, minus HSA money and tax savings.
- Use the HSA, health FSA and dependent care FSA ($7,500 in 2026) to pay predictable costs with pre-tax money.
- Review life and disability coverage, your 401(k) rate and ESPP enrollment at the same time.
- Check beneficiary forms every year.
## Quiz
Q: You expect a very low-cost year medically. Which plan usually wins on total cost?
  The plan with the lowest deductible
* The high-deductible plan with an HSA, especially with an employer deposit
  It never matters
Why: In a light year you pay little toward the deductible, so the lower premium, the employer HSA deposit and the tax savings usually win.
## Lesson glossary
- Dependent care FSA: A workplace account for child or dependent care costs while you work, paid with pre-tax money.
- Qualifying life event: A change such as marriage, a birth or losing other coverage that lets you change benefits outside open enrollment.

========== l-trad-vs-roth | Retirement Accounts #1 | Traditional or Roth? (widget rothVsTrad)
Summary: Pay tax now or later: how to pick, and why many people hold both.
Idea: Traditional skips tax now and taxes withdrawals. Roth taxes you now and never again. Compare your tax rate now with later.

### Two ways to get the same tax break
Retirement accounts come in two tax flavors. A traditional 401(k) or IRA gives you a deduction today: the money goes in before income tax, grows untaxed, and every dollar you take out later is taxed as income. A Roth 401(k) or IRA works the other way: you pay tax on the money first, it grows, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, growth included.

### The one question that decides it
If the tax rates are the same, the two end up equal: paying 22% at the start or the end of a multiplication gives the same answer. So the choice comes down to one guess: is your tax rate higher now or in retirement?

- Lean Roth when your rate is low now: early in your career, a low-income year, a sabbatical, or the years between retiring and starting Social Security.
- Lean traditional in your peak earning years, when the deduction saves you 24%, 32% or more, especially if you expect lower income later or plan to retire somewhere with no state income tax.
- Your retirement rate depends on Social Security, pensions, rental income and required withdrawals from traditional accounts. A big traditional balance can push it up.
### The fine print that tilts toward Roth

- Limits are per dollar contributed, not per dollar of value. $7,500 in a Roth IRA is worth more than $7,500 in a traditional IRA, because none of it belongs to the IRS. If you max out, Roth shelters more.
- No required withdrawals. Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions for the owner, and Roth 401(k)s no longer have them either (since 2024). Traditional accounts force withdrawals from your 70s.
- Heirs inherit Roth money tax-free; traditional money is taxed when they withdraw it.
- Contributions can come back out of a Roth IRA at any time, tax- and penalty-free. Only earnings are locked up.
### Tax diversification: own some of each
Nobody knows future tax rates. Holding both kinds gives you a lever in retirement: take taxable withdrawals up to the top of a low bracket, then use Roth money for the rest. That flexibility also helps you stay under income lines for health-insurance subsidies and Medicare premiums (see Medicare, RMDs and IRMAA).

### Where the choice shows up

- Your 401(k): many plans offer both traditional and Roth for your own contributions. The employer match usually goes in pre-tax, though plans can now offer a Roth match (taxed to you as pay).
- Your IRA: a traditional IRA deduction phases out if you are covered by a workplace plan and earn above certain incomes, and Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher incomes (see the IRA lesson and the backdoor Roth).

## Worked example: Maya picks Roth early
A 26-year-old in a low bracket today who expects to earn much more later.
1. Maya earns $58,000. After the 2026 standard deduction her top federal bracket is 12%.
2. She can put $6,000 a year into a Roth IRA or a traditional IRA. Traditional would cut her tax by 12% × $6,000 = $720 a year.
3. At 7% a year for 35 years (to age 61), $6,000 at the start of each year grows to about $887,500 in either account.
4. Roth: all $887,500 is hers to spend, tax-free.
5. Traditional, with a 22% rate in retirement: she keeps $887,500 × 78% = about $692,200. If she also invested every $720 saving, that side account adds about $94,300 after a 15% tax on its gains, for about $786,500.
Result: Roth leaves Maya about $101,000 more, because she paid 12% going in instead of 22% coming out.

## Mistakes
- Choosing traditional for the deduction, then spending the tax saving. The comparison only works if the saving is invested.
- Assuming you will be in a lower bracket in retirement. Social Security, required withdrawals and a large traditional balance can keep your rate high.
- Going all traditional in a low-income year (first job, sabbatical, early retirement) when Roth money costs very little tax.
- Putting everything in one bucket. With only traditional money, every retirement dollar raises your taxable income.
## Takeaways
- Same rate now and later means a tie. A lower rate now favors Roth; a higher rate now favors traditional.
- Roth money has no required withdrawals and passes to heirs tax-free.
- At the contribution limit, a Roth shelters more because none of it is owed to the IRS.
- Holding both gives you control over your taxable income in retirement.
## Quiz
Q: You are in the 12% bracket now and expect 22% in retirement. Which usually leaves you more to spend?
  Traditional
* Roth
  They always come out the same
Why: Paying 12% now beats paying 22% later on the same money.
## Lesson glossary
- Tax diversification: Holding money in pre-tax, Roth and taxable accounts so you can choose which to draw from as tax rules and your income change.

- IRS: RMD FAQs (no lifetime RMDs for Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k)s) — https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs
- IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32: 2026 tax brackets and standard deduction — https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf

========== l-ira | Retirement Accounts #2 | When to open an IRA, and in what order to save (widget savingsOrder)
Summary: Who can open an IRA, the 2026 limits and deadlines, and the order to fill your accounts.
Idea: Open an IRA as soon as you have earned income. Fill accounts in order: match, high-interest debt, HSA, IRA, then the rest.

### What an IRA is
An individual retirement account is a tax-advantaged account you open yourself, at any broker, in about ten minutes (see choosing a broker). It sits next to any 401(k) you have at work. You need earned income (wages or self-employment) to contribute, or a spouse with earned income.
An IRA is a container, not an investment. After you add money, buy something inside it, such as a target-date fund or a total-market index fund (see index funds). Money left in cash just sits there.

### 2026 limits and deadlines

- $7,500 a year in total across all your traditional and Roth IRAs, plus a $1,100 catch-up from age 50. You can't put in more than you earned.
- You have until the tax-filing deadline to contribute for a year: money for 2026 can go in until April 15, 2027. A filing extension does not extend this.
- Roth IRA income limits (2026): the amount you can contribute shrinks between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified AGI if you file single, and between $242,000 and $252,000 if married filing jointly. Above that, use the backdoor Roth.
- Traditional IRA deduction: anyone with earned income can contribute, but if you have a workplace plan the deduction fades between $81,000 and $91,000 (single) or $129,000 and $149,000 (joint) in 2026.
### Spousal IRA
If you file jointly and one spouse earns little or nothing, the working spouse's income can fund an IRA for both: up to $15,000 for the couple in 2026, as long as the couple's earned income covers it. It is one of the simplest ways to double a household's IRA savings.

### The order to save in
Each step beats the one after it, so fill them in this order and move to the next when a step is full:

- 1. Starter emergency fund. A month or two of expenses in cash, so a surprise bill does not become card debt (emergency fund).
- 2. 401(k) up to the full match. An instant 50% or 100% return (the match).
- 3. High-interest debt. Paying off a 24% card is a guaranteed 24% return (card debt).
- 4. HSA if your health plan qualifies: deductible going in, tax-free coming out for medical costs (the HSA).
- 5. IRA, Roth or backdoor Roth: more investment choice and lower fees than many 401(k)s.
- 6. The rest of the 401(k), up to $24,500 in 2026 (more from age 50).
- 7. Mega backdoor Roth if your plan allows after-tax contributions (mega backdoor).
- 8. Taxable brokerage account for everything else (extra money).Then finish the emergency fund to three to six months alongside steps 4 to 8.

### Lower incomes: the Saver's Credit
If your income is modest, you may get a tax credit just for saving: in 2026 it applies up to $40,250 of AGI for single filers and $80,500 for joint filers. Contributions to an IRA or 401(k) both count.

## Worked example: Jordan fills the boxes
Single, 30, earns $85,000, can save $1,500 a month. The employer matches 50% of the first 6% of pay.
1. Jordan already has a cushion and no card debt, so steps 1 and 3 are done.
2. Step 2: 6% of $85,000 is $5,100 into the 401(k). The employer adds 50% of that: $2,550 of free money.
3. Step 4: the health plan is HSA-eligible, so $4,400 goes into the HSA (the 2026 self-only limit).
4. Step 5: $85,000 is under the $153,000 Roth line, so $7,500 goes straight into a Roth IRA.
5. Step 6: the year's savings are $18,000. $18,000 − $5,100 − $4,400 − $7,500 = $1,000 left, which goes into the 401(k).
Result: Jordan's $18,000 becomes $20,550 a year in tax-advantaged accounts, and nothing is left on the table.

## Mistakes
- Skipping the match to fund an IRA first. The match is the best return you will ever get.
- Opening an IRA and leaving the money in cash. Buy a fund inside it.
- Waiting for a "good time" to open one. Each year you skip is a year of tax-free growth you can never contribute again.
- Contributing to a Roth IRA with income above the limit and not fixing it. An excess contribution costs 6% a year until you remove or recharacterize it.
## Takeaways
- You need earned income (or a spouse's) to fund an IRA. The 2026 limit is $7,500, plus $1,100 from age 50.
- You can contribute for a year until the tax-filing deadline the next April.
- Match first, then high-interest debt, HSA, IRA, the rest of the 401(k), mega backdoor, then taxable.
- A spousal IRA lets a working spouse fund an IRA for a non-working one.
## Quiz
Q: Your employer matches 50% of the first 6% you contribute. After a small emergency fund, where should your next dollar usually go?
  A Roth IRA
* Your 401(k), up to the full match
  A taxable brokerage account
Why: The match is an instant 50% return that no other account can beat.
## Lesson glossary
- Earned income: Pay from work: wages, salary, tips and self-employment profit. Interest, dividends and Social Security do not count for IRA contributions.

========== l-backdoor-roth | Retirement Accounts #3 | The backdoor Roth IRA, step by step (widget backdoorRoth)
Summary: Earn too much for a Roth IRA? Contribute to a traditional IRA, convert, and watch the pro-rata rule.
Idea: High earners can still fund a Roth IRA: put after-tax money in a traditional IRA, convert it, and report it on Form 8606.

### Who it is for
Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher incomes ($153,000 to $168,000 single, $242,000 to $252,000 joint in 2026). But there is no income limit on converting a traditional IRA to a Roth. The backdoor Roth uses that gap. It is legal, widely used and follows the normal IRS forms.

### The steps

- 1. Open both accounts at the same broker: a traditional IRA and a Roth IRA.
- 2. Contribute up to $7,500 for 2026 ($8,600 at 50+) to the traditional IRA and take no deduction. This after-tax money is called your basis.
- 3. Convert the money to your Roth IRA. Many people leave it in cash and convert within days, so there are few earnings to tax.
- 4. Invest inside the Roth IRA.
- 5. File Form 8606 with your tax return. Part I reports the nondeductible contribution; Part II reports the conversion. Your broker sends a 1099-R for the conversion.You can't undo a conversion: recharacterizing conversions has not been allowed since 2018.

### The pro-rata rule: the trap
The IRS treats all your traditional, rollover, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs as one pot, measured on December 31 of the year you convert. You can't convert only the after-tax dollars: each converted dollar carries the same mix as the whole pot. With $7,500 of basis and $92,500 of old pre-tax rollover money, 92.5% of the conversion is taxable.
The fix: roll the pre-tax IRA money into your current 401(k) (if the plan accepts roll-ins) before December 31. 401(k)s, inherited IRAs and Roth IRAs are not counted, and each spouse's IRAs are checked separately.

### Roth five-year rules

- Contributions come out first, any time, with no tax or penalty.
- Each conversion has its own five-year clock, starting January 1 of the conversion year. Withdraw converted money before five years and before 59½ and you owe a 10% penalty on it.
- Earnings are tax-free once you are 59½ and five tax years have passed since your first Roth IRA contribution of any kind.
### The bigger cousin
If your 401(k) allows after-tax contributions and in-plan Roth conversions, the mega backdoor Roth can move tens of thousands more a year into Roth. A tax professional can check your plan and your IRA balances before you start.

## Worked example: Priya cleans up first
Priya and Sam file jointly and earn $310,000, above the 2026 Roth IRA limit. Their top federal bracket is 24%.
1. Priya has a $45,000 rollover IRA from an old job, all pre-tax.
2. If she contributes $7,500 after tax and converts it, her pot on December 31 is $52,500. Only $7,500 ÷ $52,500 = 14.3% is after-tax.
3. So 85.7% of the $7,500 conversion, $6,429, is taxable: about $1,543 of tax at 24%.
4. Instead, in October she rolls the $45,000 into her current 401(k), which accepts roll-ins. Her pre-tax IRA balance is now $0.
5. In November she contributes $7,500, converts it a week later and files Form 8606. Sam does the same with his own IRAs, which are checked separately.
Result: They move $15,000 into Roth IRAs for 2026 with almost no tax, instead of a surprise $1,543 bill.

## Mistakes
- Forgetting an old rollover, SEP or SIMPLE IRA. The pro-rata rule counts every one of them on December 31.
- Not filing Form 8606. Without it the IRS can treat your after-tax contribution as pre-tax and tax it twice.
- Taking a deduction for the traditional IRA contribution. A backdoor contribution must be nondeductible.
- Withdrawing converted money within five years while under 59½ and paying a surprise 10% penalty.
## Takeaways
- No income limit applies to Roth conversions, so high earners can contribute after tax and convert.
- The pro-rata rule looks at all your pre-tax IRA money on December 31 of the conversion year.
- Rolling pre-tax IRA money into a 401(k) first makes the conversion almost tax-free.
- File Form 8606 every year you do it.
## Quiz
Q: You have $92,500 in a pre-tax rollover IRA and convert a $7,500 nondeductible contribution. About how much of the conversion is taxable?
  None of it
  About 7.5%
* About 92.5%
Why: The pro-rata rule treats the $100,000 pot as 92.5% pre-tax, so 92.5% of what you convert is taxed.
## Lesson glossary
- Form 8606: The IRS form for nondeductible IRA contributions, conversions and Roth IRA withdrawals. It tracks your after-tax basis.
- Basis: Money already taxed, such as a nondeductible IRA contribution. It is not taxed again when converted or withdrawn.
- Recharacterization: Treating an IRA contribution as if it went to the other kind of IRA. Allowed for contributions, not for conversions.

========== l-social-security | Retirement Accounts #4 | Social Security: when to claim (widget ssClaim)
Summary: Claim at 62, 67 or 70: the monthly check, the break-even age and the tax.
Idea: You can claim from 62 to 70. Every year you wait raises the check for life, so the best age depends mostly on how long you live.

### How your benefit is figured
Social Security averages your 35 highest-earning years, adjusted for wage growth. Missing years count as zeros. You need 40 credits to qualify; in 2026 you earn one credit for each $1,890 of pay, up to four a year. The result is your primary insurance amount: the monthly check at full retirement age. Your own estimate is free at ssa.gov/myaccount.

### The claiming window: 62 to 70

- Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
- Claim at 62 and the check is cut 30%, for life.
- Wait past 67 and it grows 8% a year, to 124% at 70. Nothing is gained by waiting past 70.
- Every age gets the yearly cost-of-living raise: 2.8% for 2026 and 3.6% for 2027, announced in October 2026.In 2026 the biggest possible check at 67 is $4,152 a month; the average retired worker gets about $2,071.

### The break-even age
Claiming early gets you more checks; waiting gets you bigger ones. Compared with claiming at 62, waiting to 67 pulls ahead at about 78 years and 8 months, and waiting to 70 at about 80 years and 4 months. If you expect to live past your early 80s, waiting usually collects more. Good reasons to claim early: poor health, no other money to live on, or being single with a short family lifespan.

### Married couples: spousal and survivor checks

- A spouse can get up to 50% of the worker’s full benefit, claimed at the spouse’s own full retirement age (32.5% if claimed at 62; spousal checks stop growing at 67).
- When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the larger of the two checks.
- So the higher earner’s claiming age decides the check the survivor may live on for decades. That is one of the strongest reasons for the higher earner to wait.
- A divorced spouse can claim on an ex’s record if the marriage lasted 10 years.
### Working while you claim
Before full retirement age the earnings test applies. In 2026, Social Security holds back $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 ($25,560 in 2027). In the year you reach full retirement age the limit is $65,160 and it holds back $1 for every $3. From full retirement age on there is no limit. Held-back money comes back: at full retirement age your check is raised to credit those months.

### Taxes on your benefits
Add half your benefits to your other income (including tax-free interest). That is your combined income.

- Single: above $25,000 up to 50% of benefits is taxable; above $34,000 up to 85%.
- Married filing jointly: above $32,000 up to 50%; above $44,000 up to 85%.These lines are not adjusted for inflation, so more retirees cross them every year. Some states tax benefits too. You can have tax withheld with Form W-4V.

### Will it be there?
The 2026 Trustees Report projects the retirement trust fund runs dry in late 2032. After that, payroll taxes would still pay about 78% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts. Plan for a possible cut. Claiming at 62 to beat it would lock in a permanent 30% cut, which is usually the worse deal. In January 2025 the Social Security Fairness Act also ended two rules (WEP and GPO) that reduced benefits for many people with government pensions.

## Worked example: Maria’s claiming choice
Maria, born 1962, has a full benefit of $2,000 a month at 67. She is married and earned more than her husband.
1. At 62 she would get 70%: $1,400 a month, or $16,800 a year. At 70 she would get 124%: $2,480 a month, or $29,760 a year.
2. By age 80, claiming at 62 has paid 18 years × $16,800 = $302,400. Claiming at 70 has paid 10 years × $29,760 = $297,600. Early is still slightly ahead.
3. Break-even: $1,400 × (age − 62) = $2,480 × (age − 70). That solves to about 80 years and 4 months.
4. By age 90: claiming at 62 pays 28 × $16,800 = $470,400; claiming at 70 pays 20 × $29,760 = $595,200. Waiting wins by $124,800.
5. If Maria dies first, her husband switches to her check. Waiting to 70 makes his survivor check $2,480 instead of $1,650 (survivor checks on an early claim have a floor of 82.5% of her full benefit).
Result: If Maria expects to live past about 80, waiting to 70 collects more, and it protects the spouse who outlives her.

## Mistakes
- Claiming at 62 because “Social Security will go broke”. Even after the trust fund runs short, payroll taxes keep paying most benefits, while an early claim locks in a 30% cut for life.
- A married higher earner claiming early without thinking about the survivor, who may live on that smaller check for 20 years.
- Claiming before 67 while still working full time, then being surprised when checks are held back by the earnings test.
- Waiting past 70. The check stops growing at 70, so every month later is money you never get back.
## Takeaways
- Claim anytime from 62 (70% of the full benefit) to 70 (124%). Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
- Waiting to 70 beats claiming at 62 if you live past about 80; health and family history matter most.
- For couples, the higher earner’s check becomes the survivor’s check, so delaying protects the spouse who lives longer.
- Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable once combined income passes $34,000 single or $44,000 joint.
- Check your earnings record at ssa.gov/myaccount every year; mistakes there lower your benefit.
## Quiz
Q: Your full benefit at 67 is $2,000 a month. About how much is it if you wait to claim at 70?
  $2,160
* $2,480
  $2,800
Why: Each year past 67 adds 8%, so three years add 24%: $2,000 × 1.24 = $2,480.
## Lesson glossary
- Survivor benefit: The check a widow or widower can switch to: up to 100% of what the spouse who died was receiving.
- Combined income: Your other income plus tax-free interest plus half your Social Security. It decides how much of your benefit is taxed.

========== l-medicare-rmd | Retirement Accounts #5 | Medicare, RMDs and IRMAA (widget rmdCalc)
Summary: Enroll in Medicare on time, plan required withdrawals and stay under the IRMAA lines.
Idea: Medicare starts at 65 and forced withdrawals start at 73 or 75. Both depend on your income, so plan them together.

### Medicare at 65

- Part A covers hospital stays. It is premium-free if you or your spouse paid Medicare tax for 40 quarters. The 2026 hospital deductible is $1,736.
- Part B covers doctors and outpatient care: $202.90 a month in 2026, with a $283 yearly deductible.
- Part D covers drugs, bought from a private plan. Many people add a Medigap policy, or choose a Medicare Advantage plan instead of Parts A, B and D.
### Sign up on time
Your Initial Enrollment Period lasts seven months: the three months before the month you turn 65, that month, and the three after. Miss it and Part B costs 10% more for each full 12 months you could have had it, for life. If you or your spouse still work for an employer with group health coverage, you can usually wait and use an eight-month special window when that job coverage ends. COBRA and retiree plans do not count as job coverage for this rule. Original Medicare generally does not cover care outside the US.

### IRMAA: the income surcharge
Higher earners pay more for Parts B and D. The 2026 premium is set by your income from 2024, two years earlier. The first line is $109,000 single or $218,000 married filing jointly. Each tier is a cliff: one dollar over the line costs the whole surcharge for the year, for each spouse on Medicare. A big Roth conversion or home sale at 63 can raise your premiums at 65. If your income dropped after retiring, ask Social Security to use newer income with Form SSA-44.

### Required minimum distributions

- RMDs start at 73 if you were born 1951–1958 and at 75 if born 1960 or later. If you were born in 1959, check the latest IRS guidance.
- Each year’s RMD is last December 31’s balance divided by the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table number for your age: 26.5 at 73, 24.6 at 75, 16.0 at 85.
- They apply to traditional IRAs, 401(k)s and 403(b)s. Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k)s have none while you live.
- The first RMD can wait until April 1 of the next year, but then you take two in one year.
- Miss one and the penalty is 25% of the shortfall, cut to 10% if you fix it within two years.
### Why Roth conversions before RMDs can help
The years between retiring and RMDs are often your lowest-income years. Converting some pre-tax money to Roth then, in slices that fill the 12% or 22% bracket, means paying tax at a low rate now instead of a higher one later. It shrinks future RMDs, keeps you under IRMAA lines and leaves heirs tax-free Roth money (most heirs must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years). Watch the trade-offs: conversions raise your income for health subsidies before 65 and for IRMAA two years later. See Roth conversions and withdrawal order.

## Worked example: Ken’s RMDs and the IRMAA line
Ken, single and born 1961, expects $70,000 a year of other income from Social Security and a pension.
1. Born after 1959, his RMDs start at 75. If his IRA holds $1,200,000 on the December 31 before, his first RMD is $1,200,000 ÷ 24.6 = $48,780.
2. His income that year is $70,000 + $48,780 = $118,780, above the $109,000 single IRMAA line.
3. Two years later he pays $284.10 a month for Part B instead of $202.90, plus $14.50 a month on his drug plan: ($81.20 + $14.50) × 12 = $1,148.40 extra that year.
4. Instead, between 65 and 74 he converts enough to Roth, in slices taxed at 12% and 22%, that his IRA is $900,000 at 75. His first RMD becomes $900,000 ÷ 24.6 = $36,585.
5. Now his income is $70,000 + $36,585 = $106,585, under the line. The Roth part grows tax-free with no RMDs.
Result: Smaller forced withdrawals kept Ken under the first IRMAA line, saving about $1,148 a year in Medicare premiums, and future RMDs will be smaller too.

## Mistakes
- Missing the Part B window because you have COBRA or a retiree plan. Neither counts as job coverage, and the late penalty lasts for life.
- Forgetting the two-year lookback: a big conversion or home sale at 63 raises Medicare premiums at 65.
- Delaying the first RMD to April 1, then taking a second one by December 31. Two RMDs in one year can push you into a higher bracket and over an IRMAA line.
- Converting so much in one year that you jump into a high bracket. Convert in measured slices that fill your current bracket.
## Takeaways
- Enroll in Medicare in the seven months around your 65th birthday unless you have current job coverage.
- IRMAA uses income from two years earlier, and each tier is a cliff, so plan big income events.
- RMDs start at 73 (born 1951–58) or 75 (born 1960+): last year-end balance ÷ the IRS table divisor.
- Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k)s have no RMDs while you live.
- Low-income years before RMDs are the time for measured Roth conversions.
## Quiz
Q: Your IRA held $984,000 last December 31 and this year’s divisor is 24.6. What is your RMD?
  $24,600
* $40,000
  $98,400
Why: RMD = balance ÷ divisor = $984,000 ÷ 24.6 = $40,000.
## Lesson glossary
- Initial Enrollment Period: The seven months around your 65th birthday month when you can sign up for Medicare without a penalty.
- Uniform Lifetime Table: The IRS table of divisors used to work out each year’s required minimum distribution.

========== l-retire-age | Early or Late Retirement #1 | Retire at 55, 62, 67 or 70? (widget retireAge)
Summary: The age you stop work changes the savings you need, Social Security and health cover.
Idea: Each extra year of work adds a year of saving, removes a year to fund and grows Social Security for life.

The day you stop working is the biggest lever in any retirement plan. It decides four things at once, and they all push in the same direction.

### Four things the date decides

- Years of saving. Every year you work you add money and give the pile another year to compound.
- Years to fund. Retire at 55 and plan to 95, and your money must last 40 years. Retire at 70 and it is 25.
- Social Security. Claim at 62 and you get 70% of your full benefit for life. Wait to 67 for 100%, or to 70 for 124% (for anyone born in 1960 or later).
- Health insurance. Medicare starts at 65. Every year before that you buy your own cover.
### Retiring early: the bridge years
Early retirees must bridge two gaps. The first is access: money taken from a 401(k) or IRA before 59½ usually owes a 10% penalty on top of income tax. Ways around it include the Rule of 55 (leave your job in or after the year you turn 55 and take money from that employer’s 401(k) without the penalty), Roth IRA contributions (you can always take out what you put in), and a taxable brokerage account. See money out before 59½.
The second is health insurance until 65: a spouse’s plan, COBRA or the marketplace. It can cost more than people expect. See health insurance before 65 and bridging the gap to Medicare.

### Retiring late: the quiet superpower
Working a few more years helps three ways at once: more contributions (including catch-up contributions from 50), more growth, and fewer years to pay for. Delaying Social Security past 67 adds 8% a year up to 70, and that bigger check is adjusted for inflation for the rest of your life.
The catch: plenty of people stop earlier than planned because of health, a layoff or caring for family. Treat working to 70 as a bonus, and make sure an earlier date still works.

### Plan for a long life
Longevity risk is the risk of outliving your money. A plan that works to 85 can fail at 92. Plan to at least 95, and longer if your family lives long. Delaying Social Security is one of the cheapest ways to insure a long life, because the larger check never runs out. For couples, the higher earner’s benefit also becomes the survivor’s benefit. More in Social Security: when to claim.

### The middle path
Retirement does not have to be a cliff. Part-time work, consulting or a lower-stress job in your late 50s and early 60s can cover spending and health insurance while your savings keep growing. Even $20,000 a year of earnings in the first few years cuts what you withdraw when a market drop would hurt most. See Coast FIRE and Barista FIRE.

## Worked example: One saver, four dates
A 45-year-old with $400,000 saved puts away $25,000 a year, earns 4% after inflation, plans to spend $60,000 a year and expects $2,400 a month of Social Security at 67. Health insurance before 65 costs $12,000 a year. All figures are in today’s dollars and the plan runs to 95.
1. Retire at 55. Savings reach about $892,000. From 55 to 61 she needs $72,000 a year ($60,000 plus $12,000 of health cover) with no Social Security.
2. At 62 she claims 70% of her benefit: $2,400 × 12 × 0.70 = $20,160 a year. That still leaves $39,840 a year to withdraw after Medicare starts at 65. She needs about $1.05 million, has $892,000, and runs out at 81.
3. Retire at 62. Seven more years of saving lift the pot to about $1.37 million, and she skips seven years of paying for everything alone. She needs about $787,000: 174% funded.
4. Retire at 67. Her full benefit is $28,800 a year, so she withdraws only $31,200 a year. About $1.80 million saved against $541,000 needed.
5. Retire at 70. Social Security grows to 124%: $35,712 a year. She withdraws $24,288 a year from about $2.11 million.
Result: Working from 55 to 62 turned a plan that runs out at 81 into one with room to spare. Retiring at 58 with part-time income is a middle path worth testing in the widget.

## Mistakes
- Planning only to average life expectancy. Many people live well past it, so plan to 95 or beyond.
- Forgetting the health insurance bill between your last day of work and Medicare at 65.
- Claiming Social Security at 62 by default. It locks in a check about 30% smaller for life, and can shrink a surviving spouse’s benefit too.
- Counting on working until 70 with no plan B if health or a layoff ends your career earlier.
## Takeaways
- Each extra year of work adds a year of saving, removes a year to fund and grows Social Security.
- Retiring before 65 means paying for health insurance; before 59½ it means planning penalty-free access to your money.
- Social Security pays 70% of the full benefit at 62, 100% at 67 and 124% at 70, for life.
- Plan for the money to last to at least 95.
- Part-time work in the early years is a powerful middle path.
## Quiz

  90%
Why: Claiming 60 months early cuts the benefit by 30%, for life. Waiting to 70 raises it to 124%.

========== l-retire-plan | Early or Late Retirement #2 | Your retirement plan on one page (widget retirePlanner)
Summary: Savings, saving rate, returns, spending and Social Security, year by year to 95.
Idea: Five numbers decide almost every retirement plan. Put them on one page and update them once a year.

A retirement plan does not need a 40-page report. It needs five numbers, a sense of which accounts the money sits in, and an hour once a year to update them.

### The five numbers that matter

- What you have, split by account type.
- What you add each year, including any employer match (see the 401(k) match).
- Your return after inflation. Small changes here compound into big differences over decades.
- What you will spend in retirement, in today’s dollars. Start from what you spend now, then add travel and health care and remove work costs and the mortgage if it will be paid off.
- Guaranteed income: Social Security, a pension, rent. Get your Social Security estimate from a my Social Security account at ssa.gov.
### Today’s dollars, not future dollars
At 2.5% inflation, prices double in about 29 years (72 ÷ 2.5, the rule of 72). A future $2 million sounds rich, but in today’s money it may buy what $1 million buys now. Think and plan in today’s dollars: the planner shows both.

### Why the account type matters

- Pre-tax 401(k) and traditional IRA money is taxed as income when you take it out. $1 million pre-tax is not $1 million to spend.
- Roth money comes out tax-free after 59½ (and five years).
- HSA money is tax-free when spent on medical costs.
- Taxable brokerage money is taxed only on gains, often at low long-term rates, and has no age rules, which makes it the natural bridge for early retirees.More in Traditional or Roth? and Turn savings into a paycheck.

### Read the withdrawal rate
Your first-year withdrawal divided by your savings is your withdrawal rate. In US history, about 4% a year, raised with inflation, lasted 30 years through almost every period (see the 4% rule). A higher rate for a few bridge years can be fine if Social Security will soon take over part of the load.

### What a one-page plan leaves out
The planner uses one steady return. Real markets swing, and a crash in your first years of retirement hurts most (see sequence of returns risk). It also skips required minimum distributions, detailed taxes, long-term care and contribution limits. Use it to find the direction, then stress-test it and re-run it every year or after any big life change.

## Worked example: A mid-career couple
The “Mid-career couple” preset in the planner, using a 6.5% return and 2.5% inflation.
1. Both 40, they have $320,000 pre-tax, $70,000 Roth, $90,000 taxable and $25,000 in an HSA: $505,000 in all.
2. They save $2,500 a month ($30,000 a year) and get a 5% match on $180,000 of pay ($9,000). That is $39,000 a year, rising with inflation.
3. Retiring at 56, they have about $1.78 million in today’s dollars.
4. From 56 to 66 they spend $100,000 a year with no Social Security. Taking it from the taxable account first, the first-year withdrawal is about 5.9% of savings. Before 59½ they need money they can reach without the 10% penalty.
5. At 67 Social Security pays $4,200 a month, $50,400 a year: half their spending. Withdrawals drop sharply from there.
Result: The plan lasts past 95 with about $540,000 to spare in today’s dollars; they could spend up to about $105,900 a year.

## Mistakes
- Treating a pre-tax balance as all yours. Income tax comes out of every dollar you withdraw.
- Mixing today’s and future dollars, so a big future number looks safer than it is.
- Using a high steady return as if it were a promise. Test lower returns too.
- Building the plan once and never updating it as pay, savings and goals change.
## Takeaways
- Five numbers decide most plans: savings, saving rate, return after inflation, spending and guaranteed income.
- Plan in today’s dollars.
- Pre-tax, Roth, HSA and taxable money are not equal once taxes are counted.
- A first-year withdrawal near 4% has held up historically; higher bridge years can work if Social Security follows.
- Re-run the plan every year.

  About 50 years
Why: Rule of 72: 72 ÷ 2.5 ≈ 29 years.

========== l-retire-income | Early or Late Retirement #3 | Turn savings into a paycheck (widget paycheckBuilder)
Summary: Which account to spend first, the bucket plan, annuities and the dividend myth.
Idea: In retirement you build your own paycheck: Social Security is the floor, and withdrawals fill the gap in an order that keeps lifetime taxes low.

### The paycheck stack
Start with income that lasts for life and rises with inflation: Social Security and any pension. Your savings fill the gap between that floor and what you spend. How much you can take each year is the withdrawal rate question; this lesson is about where each dollar comes from.

### Which account first?

- Taxable first, then pre-tax, then Roth. The classic order. It lets tax-advantaged accounts grow longest, but it can leave a huge pre-tax balance that later meets RMDs, Social Security tax and IRMAA all at once.
- Fill the low brackets. Each year take (or convert to Roth) pre-tax money up to the top of the 12% bracket, and cover the rest from taxable savings. In the gap years before Social Security and RMDs this often cuts lifetime tax. Long-term gains from the taxable account can still land in the 0% bracket.
- Proportional: a slice from every account every year. Simple and steady.
- Keep Roth money for last, for big one-off costs, or for heirs: it has no RMDs and comes out tax-free.In the examples above, spreading pre-tax withdrawals over more years (proportional or bracket-filling) leaves more after tax than taxable-first. The best order depends on your balances and tax rates, so test your own numbers, or ask a fee-only planner or CPA to.
Before 65, your income also sets health insurance subsidies; after 63 it sets Medicare premiums two years later.

### The bucket plan

- Cash: about one to two years of the spending Social Security does not cover, in a high-yield savings account, money market fund or T-bills (see where to park cash).
- Bonds: roughly two to five more years in short-term, high-quality bonds.
- Stocks: the rest, for long-term growth.You spend from cash. After good years you refill from stocks; after bad years you refill from bonds and leave stocks alone to recover. The value is behavioral: you never have to sell stocks in a crash, which softens sequence of returns risk and keeps panic out of your decisions. Buckets add no return on their own.

### Annuities: buying a paycheck
A single-premium immediate annuity (SPIA) turns a lump sum into monthly income for life. Pros: you cannot outlive it, and it lets you spend more confidently. Cons: the money is gone for good, most payments do not rise with inflation, and you depend on the insurer (state guaranty funds cover only up to a limit). Avoid complex variable or indexed annuities with high fees and surrender charges. The cheapest inflation-protected annuity most people can buy is delaying Social Security.

### The dividend myth
“Only spend the dividends” feels safe, but a dividend is part of your return paid out, and the share price drops by the same amount on the payout date. Chasing high-yield stocks concentrates you in a few industries and can raise your tax bill. Spend from total return: dividends, interest and selling a little when needed.

### Make it feel like a paycheck

- Set an automatic monthly transfer from your brokerage cash to checking.
- Have tax withheld: Form W-4R for IRA withdrawals, W-4V for Social Security, or pay quarterly estimates.
- Rebalance and refill the cash bucket once a year, on a set date.

## Worked example: A couple’s gap years at 62
Married, both 62, spending $84,000 a year. Social Security of $3,800 a month starts at 67. Federal tax uses 2026 brackets and the $32,200 standard deduction.
1. Until 67 there is no Social Security, so all $84,000 comes from savings: $34,000 from the brokerage account and $50,000 from the traditional IRA.
2. Taxable ordinary income: $50,000 − $32,200 = $17,800, all in the 10% bracket: $1,780 of tax. Half the brokerage sale is gain ($17,000), and total taxable income of $34,800 is under the $98,900 line, so that gain is taxed at 0%.
3. They also convert $40,000 to Roth each year. Ordinary taxable income becomes $57,800: tax = $2,480 + 12% × ($57,800 − $24,800) = $6,440. The gain still fits under $98,900, so it stays at 0%.
4. The conversion cost $6,440 − $1,780 = $4,660 of extra tax, about 11.7% of the $40,000 moved.
5. Five years of this moves $200,000 to Roth at roughly 12%, which shrinks the RMDs at 75 that would otherwise stack on top of Social Security and be taxed at 22% or more.
Result: Paying about 12% on conversions in the gap years beats paying 22% or more on bigger forced withdrawals later.

## Mistakes
- Spending only dividends and loading up on high-yield stocks, which concentrates risk without adding return.
- Leaving all pre-tax money for last by default, then facing RMDs, taxable Social Security and IRMAA at once in your 70s.
- Buying a complex annuity with high fees and surrender charges after a sales pitch, instead of comparing simple quotes or delaying Social Security.
- Keeping next year’s spending money in stocks, so a crash forces you to sell low.
## Takeaways
- Social Security and pensions are the floor; savings fill the gap.
- Withdrawal order changes lifetime tax: filling the low brackets in the gap years often beats taxable-first.
- Hold one to two years of spending in cash so you never have to sell stocks in a crash.
- A simple immediate annuity, or delaying Social Security, can buy lifetime income; avoid high-fee products.
- Spend from total return: dividends, interest and occasional sales.

- SPIA: You pay an insurer a lump sum once and get a fixed monthly payment for life.
- Total return: Price change plus dividends and interest: everything an investment earns.

========== l-retire-countdown | Early or Late Retirement #4 | The ten-year countdown (widget countdownList)
Summary: What to do ten, five, two and one year before you stop work.
Idea: The last ten years before retirement are your biggest saving years and your best chance to fix gaps.

Most people earn the most, and can save the most, in the decade before they retire. It is also the last stretch where a mistake is cheap to fix. Use it on purpose.

### Ten years out

- Catch-up contributions. From the year you turn 50 you can add extra to a 401(k) and an IRA, and a bigger 401(k) catch-up applies at ages 60 to 63. See the 401(k) limits.
- Clear high-interest debt while a paycheck still arrives (card debt).
- Check your Social Security record. Missing years of earnings mean a smaller benefit.
- Decide on the mortgage: pay it off, keep a cheap fixed rate, or downsize.
### Five years out

- Start the glide path. A crash just before or after you retire does the most damage. Shift gradually so a few years of spending sit in bonds and cash (stocks, bonds and your age).
- Choose where you will live. Housing, state tax, health care and family all change with the place.
### Two to three years out

- Test-drive the budget. Live on your planned retirement spending for a year and invest the rest.
- Plan health insurance until 65. Medicare’s initial enrollment period runs seven months: the three months before the month you turn 65, that month, and the three after. Missing it without other qualifying coverage can add 10% to your Part B premium for each full year you were late, for life.
- Pick your Social Security age, as a couple if you are married (when to claim).
- Update estate documents and beneficiaries (wills, beneficiaries and trusts).
- Build a cash bucket of one to two years of spending.
### The year you retire, and after

- Set up your paycheck: which account pays for what, and how much each month (turn savings into a paycheck).
- Think before rolling over a 401(k). If you retire between 55 and 59½, money left in that employer’s plan can be reached without the 10% penalty; once it moves to an IRA it usually cannot.
- Use the low-tax window. The years between your last paycheck and Social Security or required minimum distributions are often your cheapest years to convert pre-tax money to Roth (Medicare, RMDs and IRMAA).

## Worked example: Maria’s last ten years
Maria is 52 and plans to retire at 62. The catch-up figures are the 2026 limits.
1. At 52 she raises her 401(k) to the 2026 limit of $24,500 plus the $8,000 catch-up for 50 and older: $32,500 a year. Her IRA takes $7,500 plus a $1,100 catch-up.
2. At 60 and 61 the 401(k) catch-up for ages 60 to 63 rises to $11,250 (2026 figure), so she can put in $35,750 a year.
3. At 57 she starts shifting from 90% stocks toward 60% and fills a cash bucket with one year of spending.
4. At 59 she lives on her planned $5,000 a month for a year and invests everything above it. The budget holds, with $400 a month to spare.
5. At 61 she prices a marketplace health plan for ages 62 to 64, decides to claim Social Security at 67, and updates the beneficiaries on every account.
Result: Ten years of catch-ups alone let her save roughly $97,500 more than the standard limits allow ($8,000 × 8 + $11,250 × 2 + $1,100 × 10), before any growth.

## Mistakes
- Leaving everything for the final year, when the big levers (saving more, paying off debt) have little time left to work.
- Missing the Medicare enrollment window around your 65th birthday and paying a late penalty for life.
- Forgetting beneficiary forms. They override your will, and an ex-spouse can still be listed.
- Going to all cash out of fear, or staying 100% in stocks right up to the last day.
## Takeaways
- The last decade is usually your strongest saving decade: use catch-up contributions.
- Clear high-interest debt and decide on the mortgage while you still have a paycheck.
- Shift gradually toward safer assets and keep one to two years of spending in cash.
- Test-drive the budget, plan health cover to 65 and pick a Social Security age before you leave.
- The early retirement years are a low-tax window for Roth conversions.

  On your 65th birthday

  The day you retire
