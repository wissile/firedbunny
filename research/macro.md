## 1. The Federal Reserve

### Structure and mandate
- The Fed has three parts: the Board of Governors (a federal agency in D.C.), 12 regional Reserve Banks (with 24 branches), and the FOMC. https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/structure-federal-reserve-system.htm
- Board: 7 governors, nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, serving staggered 14-year terms. The Chair, Vice Chair and Vice Chair for Supervision serve 4-year terms. Same source + https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/bios/board/default.htm
- FOMC: 12 voting members (7 governors, the NY Fed president, and 4 of the other 11 Reserve Bank presidents on 1-year rotating terms). It holds 8 regularly scheduled meetings a year. https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm
- 2026 voting presidents: Williams (NY, FOMC Vice Chair), Hammack (Cleveland), Kashkari (Minneapolis), Logan (Dallas), Paulson (Philadelphia). Same source.
- Statutory mandate: "maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates," usually called the dual mandate. The inflation goal is **2% measured by the annual change in the PCE price index**. Adopted Jan 2012; reaffirmed Jan 27, 2026. https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/FOMC_LongerRunGoals.pdf
- Aug 22, 2025 framework revision: dropped flexible *average* inflation targeting (the 2020 "make-up" overshoot) and the "shortfalls" wording, and kept the 2% target. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-fed-does-listen-how-it-revised-the-monetary-policy-framework
- Minutes are released 3 weeks after each decision. An SEP (with the dot plot) comes out 4 times a year, at the Mar, Jun, Sep and Dec meetings. https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm

### Tools (current settings effective Sep 17, 2026)

## 4. Inflation mechanics and history
- **CPI vs PCE:** CPI uses a fixed-basket (modified Laspeyres) formula and covers out-of-pocket household spending, with weights from household surveys. PCE uses a Fisher-Ideal formula that captures substitution and has broader scope (it includes employer and government-paid medical care), with weights from business data. The four sources of difference are the formula, weight, scope and "other" effects. https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/555
  - CPI usually runs higher: ~0.5 pp on average since 2000 and ~0.3 pp since 2008. The weight effect, driven largely by housing, is the biggest contributor. https://www.clevelandfed.org/newsroom-and-events/publications/economic-trends/2014-economic-trends/et-20140417-pce-and-cpi-inflation-whats-the-difference.aspx
  - Note: in Aug–Sep 2026, core PCE (3.0%) ran **above** core CPI (2.6%). That is unusual and is partly tied to BEA's methodology update (see section 2).
- **Headline vs core:** core excludes food and energy because they are volatile. Sep 2026 shows the gap clearly: headline 3.7% vs core 2.6%, driven by energy at +20.4%. https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm
- **Shelter weight (Dec 2025 relative importance):** shelter **35.6%** of CPI (owners' equivalent rent 26.2%, rent 7.8%), which is ~**44.6% of core CPI** (computed: 35.625 / 79.919). Food 13.7%; energy 6.4%. https://www.bls.gov/cpi/tables/relative-importance/2025.htm
- **Peak:** CPI **9.1% y/y in June 2022**, the largest since Nov 1981 (energy +41.6%, food +10.4%). https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2022/consumer-prices-up-9-1-percent-over-the-year-ended-june-2022-largest-increase-in-40-years.htm
- **Tariffs and prices (as reported):** a Minneapolis Fed analysis (Aug 28, 2026) finds tariffs added roughly **0.2–0.4 pp to core PCE** as of Jul 2026, after delayed pass-through (little effect through Dec 2025).
  - Apparel inflation went from 0.3% (Dec 2025) to 3.5% (Jul 2026).
  - Separately, AI-driven computer equipment prices (+12.2% y/y) add about 0.4 pp. https://minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/initially-delayed-the-pass-through-of-tariffs-to-consumer-prices-has-arrived
- **Stagflation:** rising prices together with sluggish demand and relatively high unemployment. Classic case: the 1970s, partly driven by oil shocks. https://www.britannica.com/topic/stagflation
  - Oct 2026 is not textbook stagflation: unemployment is 4.2% and GDP is growing. But energy-driven inflation alongside weak sentiment and weak payrolls is the "stagflation-lite" worry. (Interpretation, not a sourced claim.)
